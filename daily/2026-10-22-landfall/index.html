The physical internet, country by country

Landfall

How ten countries physically plug into the internet: the cables, exchanges and satellites they rely on, where those links run thin, and what three real outages did to ordinary people.

Explainer · 10 country dossiers · 3 outage case files · about 22 minutes

At 5.27pm on 15 January 2022 the undersea volcano Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai erupted. By 6.40pm Tonga had vanished from the internet.3 The kingdom had one international fibre-optic cable, running about 827 km to Fiji, and the eruption broke it.4 For roughly five weeks, a country where remittances were worth an estimated 43.9% of GDP the previous year had to make do with a few satellite links.5,6

Tonga is the extreme case, but every country has the same basic arrangement. Its internet arrives through a handful of physical routes: cables that come ashore at particular beaches, buildings where networks swap traffic, and dishes pointed at the sky. Some countries have dozens of routes. Others have two or three that share a canyon, a strait or a single stretch of sand. This guide explains the parts, then visits ten countries that show the system's strengths and its weak points, and ends with three outages told from the ground.

Submarine cables

Nearly all traffic between continents travels through glass on the seabed. The usual line, repeated by the UN's telecoms agency, is that cables carry more than 99% of international data.11 TeleGeography, the research firm that maps the industry, says the figure is right but that nobody can calculate it precisely, because there is no data on intercontinental satellite traffic. The hardest number behind it is an old one from the US regulator: in 2013 satellites accounted for 0.37% of America's international capacity.12 A safer phrasing, used by the UN Environment Programme and the industry's protection body in 2009, is "well over 95 per cent".2

A cable is a bundle of fibre pairs inside layers of steel, copper and plastic. Optical amplifiers, called repeaters, sit along it at intervals of around 70 km, and power is fed down a copper conductor from equipment at the landing stations.2 A modern system can carry a great deal: Google's Dunant cable across the Atlantic has 12 fibre pairs and a capacity of 250 terabits per second.13 Cables are engineered to last at least 25 years, but are often retired sooner because they become economically obsolete.1

Who builds and owns them

Four companies make and lay about 98% of the world's undersea cables, according to the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS): SubCom (US), Alcatel Submarine Networks (France) and NEC (Japan), which together held 87% of the market in 2021, and China's HMN Technologies, formerly Huawei Marine, with 11%.14

Ownership has changed more than the hardware. Cables were traditionally financed by consortia of national telecoms carriers. Today Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon are major investors in new systems.1 TeleGeography found that in 2024 content and cloud companies accounted for almost three quarters of all international bandwidth demand; as recently as 2016, backbone carriers were the majority.15

Faults and repairs

Breaks are routine. The International Cable Protection Committee (ICPC) puts the global total at roughly 150 to 200 faults a year and attributes around 70–80% to accidental human activity, mostly fishing gear and ships' anchors.8 TeleGeography's figure for fishing and anchors is lower, at about two thirds.1 Natural hazards such as earthquakes and underwater landslides cause fewer than 10% of faults overall, but around 30% in water deeper than about 1,500 m.2

Repairs depend on a small fleet. There are about 60 ships capable of the work,10 and TeleGeography estimates that two thirds of cable maintenance ships will reach the end of their service lives by 2040.16 Owners pay into regional maintenance agreements that keep ships on standby: the Atlantic agreement, for example, is a members' cooperative with ships dedicated to it all year.17 Once a ship is over the break, the repair itself is a specialised job of 10 to 24 hours;2 getting there, and getting permission to work in another country's waters, often takes far longer.10

Exchanges and caches

Once traffic is ashore it has to be handed between networks. Networks either pay a larger carrier for transit or connect directly and swap traffic without charging each other, which is called peering. Internet exchange points (IXPs) are the buildings where many networks do this at once.18

A country without a working exchange can end up sending traffic between two local networks out of the country and back again, a detour known as tromboning.19 A 2012 study for the Internet Society found that Kenya's exchange cut local latency from 200–600 milliseconds to 2–10, and that Nigeria's had a similar effect and drew Google into placing a cache in the country.19 By 2020 Google, Facebook, Netflix and Akamai all had caches in both countries, and nearly 70% of traffic stayed local.20 Google says its caches inside internet providers' networks typically serve 70–90% of its cacheable traffic.21 The practical result: when an international cable fails, a well-cached country loses less of what people actually use.

Satellites

Traditional communications satellites sit 35,786 km above the equator. In a 2022 test in Australia, the network researcher Geoff Huston measured round trips of about 660 milliseconds through one, against about 60 milliseconds through Starlink, whose satellites orbit at about 550 km.22 By March 2026 there were more than 10,000 Starlink satellites in orbit, according to a count reported by EarthSky.23

Satellites remain small in total capacity compared with fibre, and cost more per bit. Their strength is reach: remote places, ships and backup.1 In Tonga, below, satellites were the lifeboat while the cable waited for a repair ship; in West Africa some firms turned to Starlink, though most traffic moved to another cable; on Matsu in 2023 the backup was a microwave link.

Mobile networks and the law

A phone's signal only travels wirelessly as far as the mast. From there it is carried, or "backhauled", by microwave links or fibre; Ericsson expects a roughly even split between the two worldwide by 2030, excluding North East Asia from that forecast.24 So a mobile network is only as international as the cables behind it.

The legal protection is old. The 1884 Paris convention on telegraph cables made it a punishable offence to break or injure one "wilfully or by culpable negligence".25 The 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea guarantees the freedom to lay cables and, in Article 113, requires states to make it an offence for ships flying their flag, or persons under their jurisdiction, to damage cables beneath the high seas.26 Article 58 applies it to exclusive economic zones too,27 but it stops at territorial waters, and the punishment is left to each state's own law.

03 · Ten countriesWhere the links run thin

Ten countries chosen because each shows a different way of being connected: a corridor, a hub, an island, a frontier, a continent's gateway. Counts and traffic figures move; each is given with the date of its source.

Egypt

The corridor
Why it matters
The shortest path between Europe and Asia runs through Egypt. TeleGeography estimates that cables through the Red Sea carry more than 90% of all capacity between the two continents,28 and estimates published in 2022 and 2025 put about 17% of the world's internet traffic on Egypt's route.29,30
Landings
CSIS lists Mediterranean landings at Port Said, Alexandria, Abu Talat and Sidi Kerir, and Red Sea landings at Suez, Zafarana, Ras Ghareb and Hod El Dars. It counted 14 active systems in November 2025, with plans for more than 21 within three years.29 Between the two seas, cables travel overland, the shortest route being about 200 km from Port Said to Suez along the canal.29
Operators
State-controlled Telecom Egypt sits on almost every consortium, because foreign projects need a licensed Egyptian partner to get permits.29 DCD, citing Submarine Cable Networks, reported in 2022 that 12 cables crossing Egypt had paid it at least $369m for rights of use.30 Its 2019 landing deal for the PEACE cable, which also lands in Pakistan, Djibouti, Kenya and France, carried terrestrial crossing between Zafarana and Abu Talat.31
Weak point
Concentration. "It's not like there's another Egypt you can go to," Doug Madory, then of Kentik, told DCD.30 In January 2008 two cables, FLAG and SEA-ME-WE 4, were cut in the Mediterranean off Alexandria; Egypt's internet was disrupted by up to an estimated 70% and India's by up to 60%. A ship's anchor was blamed in some reports, but no cause was confirmed.32
Politics
New routes try to avoid Egypt altogether, among them Google's Blue-Raman system through Israel, Jordan and Saudi Arabia.29

Djibouti

The strait
Why it matters
Djibouti faces Yemen across the Bab-el-Mandeb, the gap between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, which CSIS puts at about 26 km wide at its narrowest.29 Nearly every Europe–Asia cable through the Red Sea passes it. The country also hosts American, French, Japanese, Italian and Chinese military bases.33
Landings
Roughly a dozen systems, depending on how and when they are counted.34 Djibouti Telecom called 2Africa its ninth cable when it landed in May 2022, while the company was building a new landing station.35 Earlier arrivals include EASSy, Seacom and SEA-ME-WE 5.36
Operators
State-owned Djibouti Telecom was the sole provider of mobile and internet services as of 2023.37 The country is a gateway for landlocked neighbours: Cloudflare described it in 2017 as vital for Ethiopia and South Sudan,36 and in February 2026 Djibouti Telecom, Ethio telecom and Sudatel agreed a land fibre corridor from Djibouti's landing stations across Ethiopia to Sudan.38
Weak point
Conflict at sea. In February 2024 three cables (Seacom, AAE-1 and EIG) were damaged in the southern Red Sea. The US National Security Council's John Kirby said they were "most likely" cut by the anchor of the Rubymar, a cargo ship disabled by a Houthi missile; Houthi representatives denied targeting cables, and as of October 2026 no formal finding had been published.39,40 Repairs needed a permit to work in Yemeni waters.40
Politics
The US and China compete here for cable contracts. A US Congressional Research Service note recorded American espionage concerns about a Huawei Marine-built cable linking Djibouti and Pakistan.33

Singapore

The hub
Why it matters
Singapore is South East Asia's meeting point: Geoff Huston of APNIC counts 35 cable systems converging on the Singapore Strait.10
Landings
In June 2023 the government said 26 cables landed at three landing stations, and set out a plan to double the number of landing sites within ten years.41 Cables may only come ashore at designated sites, listed by the regulator IMDA as of February 2026 as Changi, Tuas West and Tuas South. Approval runs through four stages involving at least ten government agencies.42
Operators
Its exchanges are among Asia's largest; Equinix's Singapore exchange alone listed about 470 member networks in October 2026, according to PeeringDB data compiled by the Internet Society.43 The government paused new data-centre construction from 2019 and reopened it in 2022 through capped, competitive allocations.44
Weak point
The neighbours' waters. Repairs in neighbouring waters depend on other governments: Malaysia exempted foreign cable-repair ships from its cabotage rules in 2019, revoked the exemption in 2020 and decided in 2024 to reinstate it.45,46
Politics
SEA-ME-WE 6, from Singapore to France, became a test of US–China rivalry. Reuters reported that HMN Tech was first in line to build it, that the US pressed members towards SubCom, which won the contract, and that China Telecom and China Mobile then withdrew.47

Taiwan

The frontier
Why it matters
An island democracy that China claims, with all its international traffic arriving by sea. Its Ministry of Digital Affairs (MODA) counted 15 international and 10 domestic cables at the end of February 2026, all designated critical infrastructure.48
Landings
Most come ashore in the north, at Toucheng, Tamsui and Bali (Pali); Fangshan in Pingtung is the only southern landing.49
Operators
Chunghwa Telecom operates the cables to Matsu, including the two cut in 2023, and the Taiwan–Penghu No. 3 cable.50,51 In 2023 it signed a deal with Eutelsat OneWeb for low-orbit satellite backup.52
Weak point
Anchors and earthquakes. MODA says Taiwan's waters averaged seven to eight cable incidents a year over four years, 38.3% of them from dragged anchors.48 The Hengchun earthquake of December 2006 set off undersea sediment flows that broke cables across the Luzon Strait and disrupted traffic across Asia.2,53
Courts and politics
In June 2025 a Taiwanese court sentenced the Chinese captain of the Togo-flagged Hong Tai 58 to three years in prison for damaging the Taiwan–Penghu No. 3 cable, finding he had wilfully anchored in a prohibited zone; he said it was negligence.54,55 In a civil case reported in April 2026, a court ordered him to pay Chunghwa Telecom about US$560,000–570,000 in damages.51 In a separate January 2025 case involving the Shunxin-39, the ship's owner denied involvement, and as of October 2026 no finding of fault had been reported.56 Tougher penalties, including confiscating ships used to cut cables, passed in December 2025.57 Matsu's 2023 outage is the third case file below.

Japan

The Pacific gateway
Why it matters
CSIS calls Japan the hub for nearly all bandwidth between the US and Asia, and says 99% of Japan's own communications depend on subsea cables.58
Landings
More than 20 international landing stations as of 2025, concentrated on the Boso peninsula in Chiba, which serves Tokyo, and around Shima in Mie, which serves Osaka.58,59 KDDI's Chikura station, opened in 2014, stands about 28 m above sea level and was designed for tsunamis and the strongest grade of shaking.60
Operators
NTT, KDDI and SoftBank. Japan has five large cable ships, three in the NTT group and two in KDDI's; NEC, a leading cable maker, has no fleet of its own.59
Weak point
Geography and earthquakes. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake damaged several cables, though TeleGeography called the effect on Japan limited; the outages clustered on segments landing at Ajigaura and Kitaibaraki in Ibaraki.61
Resilience
The government is subsidising landings away from Chiba and Mie, including SoftBank stations at Tomakomai in Hokkaido and Itoshima in Fukuoka.59,62

United Kingdom

The Atlantic beach
Why it matters
London is one of the world's main interconnection hubs. A parliamentary committee counted 64 cables landing in the UK in 2025.63 Its largest exchange, LINX, reported a peak of just over 12 terabits per second across its exchanges in January 2026.64
Landings
Evidence to Parliament in 2025 listed 11 transatlantic cables, landing in Cornwall, Somerset and Merseyside, and noted that most were low-capacity and more than 20 years old.65
Operators
Google's Grace Hopper cable, from New York to Bude and Bilbao, landed in 2021.66 2Africa, Meta-backed and around 45,000 km long, landed at Bude in 2024.67
Weak point
Bude. The same evidence said nearly 75% of the UK's potential transatlantic capacity was concentrated on two cables there, Grace Hopper and Amitié.65 Remote islands are thinner still: in October 2022 Shetland lost phone and broadband services after both its cables, to Faroe and to the mainland, broke within days of each other.68 The Faroese operator said it had reason to believe a fishing vessel damaged its cable.69
Politics
In January 2025 the defence secretary, John Healey, told MPs the Russian ship Yantar was being used to map Britain's undersea infrastructure; Russia denies tampering with cables.70 A parliamentary committee reported in September 2025 that the government was too timid on cable security, while finding no imminent threat to national connectivity. It noted that the maximum fine under the Submarine Telegraph Act 1885 was £1,000.63,71

Finland

The shallow sea
Why it matters
Finland's links to the rest of Europe cross the shallow, crowded Baltic, where a series of cables and pipelines were damaged between 2023 and 2025.
Landings
C-Lion1, run by state-majority-owned Cinia, runs 1,173 km from Helsinki to Rostock and is the country's only cable straight to Central Europe; other cables link Finland to Sweden and Estonia.72
Operators
Cinia and Elisa run the main international links.72,73 The regulator Traficom stresses redundancy: after the December 2024 damage its director-general said the use of the internet "is not dependent on just one cable or even its backup connection".73
Weak point
Anchors in a busy sea. C-Lion1 was cut in November 2024, the first break in its history, with little visible effect on users.72 On 25 December 2024 the Estlink 2 power cable and four telecoms cables were damaged in the Gulf of Finland.73
Courts and politics
Finnish prosecutors charged officers of the tanker Eagle S, alleging its anchor was dragged for about 90 km. A district court dismissed the case in October 2025 for lack of jurisdiction; in August 2026 the Court of Appeal overturned that and sent it back. As of October 2026 no one has been found guilty.74,75 Over the earlier Balticconnector damage in 2023, China said in 2024 that the Hong Kong-flagged Newnew Polar Bear had caused it by accident in a storm.76 NATO launched a Baltic patrol mission, Baltic Sentry, in January 2025.77

Nigeria

The west-coast gateway
Why it matters
Africa's most populous country depends on a handful of cables along the west coast, almost all landing in Lagos.
Landings
Glo-1 landed at Lagos in 2009.78 MainOne, founded by Funke Opeke, was bought by Equinix in 2022.79 Google's Equiano landed in Lagos in 2022 with a design capacity about 20 times that of any other cable then serving the region.80 2Africa's landing at Qua Iboe in Akwa Ibom is described as the country's only cable landing outside Lagos.81
Operators
The Lagos exchange, IXPN, was reported in June 2026 to have passed 2 terabits per second of peak local traffic.82 In 2012 researchers found it had cut local latency from 200–400 milliseconds to 2–10.19
Weak point
Shared seabed. Four of the coast's main cables cross the same submarine canyon off Abidjan, and on 14 March 2024 all four failed there.83,84 See the second case file.
Resilience
Equiano stayed up in March 2024 and took on traffic from the broken systems.85,86

Brazil

The single beach
Why it matters
One stretch of coast at Praia do Futuro in Fortaleza receives 16 cables, and NIC.br, which runs the Brazilian internet registry, says it handles nearly 90% of the country's international traffic.87,88
Landings
Besides Fortaleza, cables land at Praia Grande near São Paulo. Monet links Florida, Fortaleza and Praia Grande; Seabras-1 runs direct from São Paulo to New York.89,90 In 2024 the state of Ceará moved a planned desalination plant away from the Fortaleza cables.88
Operators
IX.br, run by NIC.br, describes itself as the largest collection of internet exchange points in the world.91 Teletime reported a record national peak of 55 terabits per second in June 2026, with 35.9 at São Paulo.92
Weak point
For decades most of Latin America's international bandwidth ran to the United States; TeleGeography's 2024 analysis put the region's share connected to the US and Canada at 79%, down from 85% in 2019.93
Politics
After the 2013 revelations of US surveillance, Brazil and the EU agreed in 2014 to build a direct cable between Lisbon and Fortaleza; Dilma Rousseff said: "We don't want businesses to be spied upon."94 The result, EllaLink, opened in 2021 between Sines and Fortaleza.95

South Africa

Two coasts
Why it matters
The continent's southern terminus has cables on both coasts, so it can usually reroute when one side fails.
Landings
West-coast cables land around Cape Town, at Melkbosstrand, Yzerfontein and Duynefontein; east-coast cables at Mtunzini and Amanzimtoti in KwaZulu-Natal and at Gqeberha.96,97 Equiano reached Melkbosstrand in 2022.98
Operators
NAPAfrica, run by Teraco, passed 5 terabits per second of peak traffic in February 2025, with more than 655 networks.99 The regulator ICASA put the country's international internet bandwidth at about 4 terabits per second in September 2025.100
Weak point
The Congo Canyon. In 2020 an underwater avalanche there ran for more than 1,000 km and broke cables.101,102 In August 2023 SAT-3 and WACS failed in the same canyon; Kentik found Cape Town–London latency rose from about 150 to 195 milliseconds as traffic moved to Equiano.103
Resilience
When EASSy and Seacom were cut off the east coast in May 2024, the Internet Society found South Africa was among the countries least affected, thanks to other cables and local peering.104

05 · Case filesThree outages, seen from the ground

Engineers measure outages in terabits and latency. These three are told by what stopped working for the people living through them.

Tonga, 2022

FAULT 01 · Volcanic eruption
CableTonga Cable to Fiji, plus the domestic cable
CauseVolcanic density currents
Cut offAbout five weeks
Restored22 February 2022 (international)

Tonga had been here before. In January 2019 the same cable was severed, and the country fell back on limited satellite capacity;3 a Tongan police report, as summarised in a New Zealand government briefing, concluded there had been no foul play and that a ship had dragged its anchor.106

  1. 15 Jan 2022 · 17:27Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai erupts. Traffic begins to fall within minutes, and Tonga is fully disconnected by 18:40.3
  2. 19 JanDigicel restores some international calls by satellite. The chairman of Tonga Cable, Samiuela Fonua, says finishing the repair within a month would be lucky.107 Calls run over a link that can handle only 400 at a time, and data and payment services remain down; Digicel's regional chief hopes to bring back "bank ATMs, EFTPOS and other services".108
  3. Late Jan–FebRemittances from Tongans abroad stall. A MoneyGram agent, Sela Latailakepa, told Rest of World there was "just no internet connection" to serve customers.109 The repair ship CS Reliance has to collect equipment in Samoa first, and the operators run short of spare cable, borrowing lengths from New Caledonia.4,107
  4. 22 FebThe international cable is reconnected after about 90 km of it is replaced.4,6 The domestic cable to the outer islands is still broken; the next day SpaceX donates 50 satellite terminals for the worst-hit islands.110
  5. 12 Jul 2023The domestic cable is finally repaired, about 18 months after the eruption.111
"First post on Facebook since January 15th! So Blessed for the opportunity!"Penisimani Akauola Tonga, a school chaplain, on 22 February 2022, as reported by Reuters4

Scientists later established what broke the cable. The eruption sent flows of ash and debris along the seabed at up to 122 km/h, for more than 100 km,112 the fastest underwater flows ever recorded.113

What it shows · One cable means no redundancy at all. Satellite kept voice calls going but not the payment and remittance systems an island economy runs on, and a repair needed a ship from another country and spare cable borrowed from a third.

West Africa, 2024

FAULT 02 · Underwater landslide
CablesWACS, MainOne, SAT-3, ACE
CauseProbable landslide in the Trou Sans Fond canyon
Countries hit13, by Cloudflare's count
Last repair8 May 2024
  1. 14 Mar 2024 · from about 05:00 UTCFour cables fail off Côte d'Ivoire. Cloudflare watches disruption spread through the day from The Gambia, Guinea and Liberia to Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, then Nigeria, Benin, Cameroon and Togo: 13 countries in all.114 MainOne's first assessment points to seismic activity on the seabed, about 3 km down.115
  2. 14–15 MarIn Nigeria, customers of many large banks cannot use apps or USSD codes, TechCabal reports.116 Failed transactions pile up and payment points at petrol stations and supermarkets get crowded; some firms switch to Starlink.117 Ghana's stock exchange extends trading by an hour.118
  3. 17 MarThe Electricity Company of Ghana warns customers about problems buying prepaid electricity credit.119
  4. Late MarStudents struggle to reach course materials and online classes. "It affected my studies," a University of Ghana student told the campus station Radio Univers.120 Traffic shifts onto Google's Equiano cable, which had not been damaged.86
  5. 6 Apr–8 MayRepairs finish in turn: SAT-3 on 6 April, ACE on 17 April, WACS on 29 April and MainOne on 8 May, according to Ghana's regulator.83

The cause took a year to pin down. Researchers at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography found that hydrophones built to listen for nuclear tests had picked up a signal from the canyon on 12 March 2024, which they interpret as an underwater landslide. All four breaks lay in the Trou Sans Fond canyon, about 107 km off Abidjan.84 Why the cables failed two days after the signal is not explained.

What it shows · Four cables are not four routes if they cross the same canyon. A newer cable on a different path, Equiano, carried much of the region's traffic, but the disruption was uneven: in Liberia it lasted more than 12 hours on the first day, by Cloudflare's measurement.114

Matsu, 2023

FAULT 03 · Ships suspected
CablesTaiwan–Matsu No. 2 and No. 3
Suspected causeTwo Chinese vessels, per Taiwan's regulator; not proven
ResidentsAbout 14,000
First repair31 March 2023
  1. 2 Feb 2023The first of two cables linking the Matsu islands to Taiwan is cut about 50 km out. Taiwan's National Communications Commission says a Chinese fishing vessel is suspected.121
  2. 8 FebThe second cable is cut; the commission suspects a Chinese cargo ship.121 The government stops short of calling the cuts deliberate, and there is no direct evidence that the ships were responsible. Chunghwa's records show the cables had been cut 27 times in five years.121
  3. February–MarchChunghwa Telecom beams a microwave link from Taiwan, much slower than the cables.121,122 Texts take hours, calls drop, and residents struggle to pay electricity bills, book doctor's appointments and receive deliveries.121 At night people gather outside Chunghwa's office with phones and laptops to use its Wi-Fi.122
  4. 31 MarOne cable is repaired after about 50 days of limited service.123,124
"A lot of tourists would cancel their booking because there's no internet."Chen Yu-lin, who runs a bed and breakfast on Matsu, to the Associated Press121

Both Matsu cables failed again in early 2025. This time the Ministry of Digital Affairs blamed natural deterioration, reserved microwave capacity for government, banks and hospitals, switched on satellite stations, and restored both cables by mid-March.125,126

What it shows · A short domestic cable can matter as much as an ocean crossing. The backup kept Matsu online, but at a trickle, and it took a repair ship and weeks of waiting to restore normal life.
