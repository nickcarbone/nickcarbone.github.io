How the World Drinks Beer

A comparative atlas of nineteen beer cultures: what goes in the glass, who pours it, what comes with it, and the laws, taxes, empires and weather that made each one different.

Same drink, different rooms

Order a lager in eleven cities and you will be handed eleven different things. In Cologne it arrives in a glass holding a fifth of a litre, and a waiter keeps replacing it until you cover it with a beer mat1,2. In Rio it comes as one large bottle for the whole table and a 190 ml glass for you3,4. In parts of China it may come at room temperature unless you ask5,6. The liquid can be much the same. Everything around it differs.

Those differences are rarely a matter of national character. They come from things that can be dated and documented: a tax band, a closing time, a brewing ban, a colonial concession, a royal decree about food. Pull on almost any beer habit and you find one of them at the other end.

The law got there first

Bavaria’s beer gardens exist because a 1539 rule banned summer brewing, so brewers kept beer in cellars and planted chestnuts above them for shade. Drinkers still bring their own food because innkeepers objected in 1812, when the king let the cellars sell beer, and the cellars were forbidden to sell meals7,8. Japan’s supermarket shelves filled with happoshu and malt-free ‘third beer’ because brewers designed drinks to fit cheaper tax bands, and the gap was only closed on 1 October 20269,10. Britain kept the imperial pint through metrication because the law kept it for draught beer11,12. In Australia, a wartime closing time of six o’clock produced the ‘six o’clock swill’, an hour of hard drinking for men coming off work, and the habit lasted in some states into the late 1960s13.

Empires left breweries behind

China’s best-known beer was first brewed by a company of British and German investors in German-held Qingdao in 190314,15. Hanoi’s bia hơi descends from brewing that French colonists brought in the 1890s16,17. Nigeria became one of Guinness’s biggest markets on the back of an export stout, and in 1962 got the company’s first brewery outside Britain and Ireland18,19. In South Africa the state took the opposite route: it seized the trade in a home-brewed sorghum beer and used the profits to pay for segregated townships20,21.

Heat, grain and the size of the glass

Climate shows up in glassware. Brazil’s small glass and shared bottle keep beer cold in the heat3. Spain drinks small measures, mostly in bars and mostly with food22,23,24. Southern Africa’s traditional beers are made from what grows there, sorghum, millet and maize25,26. Vietnam’s bia hơi is light, brewed daily and drunk within the day16.

Ritual does the rest

Some customs have no clear cause beyond a house style kept up long enough to become law in all but name: the Czech choice of four pours27, the Dutch skimmed head28, the Belgian rule of one glass for each beer29, the Japanese rule that you never fill your own glass30. Where an origin story is attached, it is often wrong. The legends at the end of this guide set the popular version beside the record.

One lager, eleven bars

The same order, as it arrives in each city.

  1. Prague

    A half-litre hladinka, foam to the rim. A stroke goes on the paper slip on your table27,31.

  2. Cologne

    A 0.2-litre Stange of Kölsch. Another arrives before you ask, until you cover the glass with your mat1,2.

  3. Munich

    A one-litre Maß under chestnut trees, with food you brought from home32,7.

  4. Amsterdam

    A small glass with a tall head, skimmed level by the bartender28.

  5. Madrid

    A caña, a little under a half-pint, and a tapa beside it22,24.

  6. Rio de Janeiro

    One large bottle in the middle of the table and a 190 ml glass each, refilled before it can warm3,4.

  7. Mexico City

    A bottle with lime in the neck, or the same beer turned into a michelada33,34.

  8. Tokyo

    The first order of the night, and you never fill your own glass35,30.

  9. Qingdao

    Possibly at room temperature unless you ask; or, before the pandemic at least, fresh from the tap in a plastic bag5,15.

  10. Hanoi

    Bia hơi brewed that day, on a plastic stool by the kerb16.

  11. Sydney

    A schooner, 425 ml. Order the same word in Adelaide and get 285 ml36.

The atlas

Nineteen beer cultures, roughly west to east within each region. Filter by what shaped them, or jump to one.

Central Europe

Czechia

The heaviest beer drinkers on earth, and the fussiest about the head.

The usual measure0.5 l or 0.3 l mug
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Pale lager, ordered by strength in degrees: a desítka is lighter, a jedenáctka or dvanáctka fuller and a little stronger37. The first batch of Pilsen lager was brewed on 5 October 1842 under Josef Groll, a brewmaster from Bavaria38,39.
Where
The hospoda, where some tables are kept for the štamgast, the regular31. Draught is the pub drink, ideally tank beer; a bottle, a lahváč, is mostly drunk at home37.
How it’s served
Say only ‘one beer, please’ and the waiter will ask ‘Malé, nebo velké?’: 0.3 litres or a half37. The pour is a choice in its own right. A hladinka is the standard, foam to the rim; a šnyt is a small beer under a thick head; a mlíko is almost all creamy foam, drunk in one go; a čochtan has no foam at all27.
The habit
Your order is marked on a slip of paper left on the table for the whole visit. Lose it and you may be charged a steep flat fee31. Toast with na zdraví and look people in the eye31.
Why it’s like this
Pilsen’s burghers had brewed in turns, the quality varied, and in the end they built a brewery of their own and hired a Bavarian to run it38. Within months the new lager was on sale in Prague38. Czechia has topped the world for beer drunk per head every year since 1993, at 148.8 litres a person in 202440.

Germany

Bavaria

Where a royal decree of 1812 still decides what you may bring to the table.

The usual measure1 l Maß
  • Law and tax
  • Climate and season
  • Food first
What’s in the glass
Lager and wheat beer, shaped by the Reinheitsgebot issued at Ingolstadt on 23 April 1516 under Duke Wilhelm IV, which allowed only water, barley and hops. Yeast is not mentioned; it was added later41.
Where
The beer garden: tables under chestnut trees, planted over the cellars where beer was once kept cool8.
How it’s served
By the Maß, a one-litre mug32. The 1516 order itself set the price of a Mass: one Munich pfennig in the cooler half of the year, two in summer41.
What comes with it
Obatzda, the spiced cheese spread, with a pretzel, and Steckerlfisch, fish grilled on a stick32. Or whatever you carried in yourself.
The habit
You may bring your own food to a traditional beer garden, though not your own drink7,32.
Why it’s like this
A Bavarian rule of 1539 limited brewing to the cooler months, so brewers dug cellars into the terraces above the Isar and shaded them with shallow-rooted chestnuts7,8. In 1812 King Maximilian I Joseph let them sell beer on site. Innkeepers protested, so the cellars were barred from selling food, and drinkers brought their own8,7. The purity order had its own motive: keeping wheat and rye for bread41.

Germany

Cologne and Düsseldorf

Two neighbouring cities, two local beers, and a waiter who will not stop bringing them.

The usual measure0.2 l Stange
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Kölsch in Cologne; dark, bitter Altbier in Düsseldorf, where house breweries such as Schumacher, Uerige, Füchschen and Schlüssel pour their own1,42.
Where
The brewery tavern, the Brauhaus1.
How it’s served
Kölsch comes in a slim 0.2-litre cylinder called a Stange, drawn from a ten-litre barrel and carried by a waiter called a Köbes, in a blue knitted jacket and apron1. Düsseldorf pours its Alt into its own shapely glasses42.
What comes with it
Halver Hahn, which is a rye roll with aged Gouda rather than half a chicken; Himmel un Äd; Sauerbraten1.
The habit
The Köbes swaps your empty glass for a full one without being asked and marks each on your beer mat. Put the mat on top of the glass when you have had enough1,2.
Why it’s like this
The small glass and the endless round are a house style, kept by the breweries and their waiters rather than by any law.

Western Europe

Belgium

On UNESCO’s list of humanity’s intangible heritage since 2016.

The usual measurea glass for every beer
  • Custom and ritual
  • Food first
  • Law and tax
What’s in the glass
Nearly 1,500 types of beer, made with a range of fermentation methods43. A handful come from Trappist monasteries, among them Chimay, Orval, Rochefort, Westmalle and Westvleteren44.
Where
The café; specialist beer cafés list hundreds of beers29.
How it’s served
Every beer has its own branded glass, and serving it in the wrong one, or at the wrong temperature, counts as poor form29.
What comes with it
Beer goes into the cooking as well as beside it, and into cheese: some are washed in beer as they ripen43.
The habit
Matching the glass to the beer29.
Why it’s like this
UNESCO inscribed Belgian beer culture in 2016, noting that some Trappist communities brew and give their profits to charity43. A popular story credits the 1919 Vandervelde law, which banned spirits in cafés until 1984, with pushing drinkers towards strong beer. The historian Roel Mulder finds no sign of it: average strength rose only from 3.29% in 1900 to 3.40% in 1940, and most strong Belgian styles only became popular after 195045.

Western Europe

The Netherlands

Beer drawn with a tall head, then trimmed flat with a blade.

The usual measuresmall fluitje or vaasje
  • Custom and ritual
  • Food first
What’s in the glass
Pils, above all.
Where
The café.
How it’s served
In small glasses, the slender fluitje and the stubbier vaasje; one guide gives them as 20 cl and 33 cl, though sizes vary from bar to bar46. The bartender lets the head rise over the rim and then skims it level once the glass is on the bar28.
What comes with it
Bitterballen and the rest of the borrelhapjes, the snacks of early-evening drinks46.
The habit
The early-evening borrel, with small beers and something fried.
Why it’s like this
The skimmed head is treated as a craft: Koninklijke Horeca Nederland, the hospitality trade body, teaches it as part of tapping properly28.

Northern Europe

Britain

Metric everywhere except the bar, where the law keeps the imperial pint.

The usual measure568 ml pint
  • Law and tax
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Lager in volume, but the national cause is cask-conditioned ‘real ale’: beer that finishes maturing in the cask it is served from, poured without added carbon dioxide47. Four enthusiasts founded the Campaign for Real Ale in 1971 to defend it48.
Where
The pub.
How it’s served
In a pint of 20 imperial fluid ounces, 568 ml49. Often in a nonic glass, whose bulge near the rim helps grip and stacking and stops the rim chipping; the name is a contraction of ‘no nick’49.
The habit
Buying in rounds. The anthropologist Kate Fox advised offering yours when your companions are about three-quarters through their drinks, and counted fussing over exact fairness as just as bad as meanness50. Mass Observation saw Bolton drinkers in the late 1930s keeping pace with one another, ‘drinking level’, the habit rounds depend on50.
Why it’s like this
Metric Britain kept the pint on purpose. The Weights and Measures Act 1985 keeps it as a lawful unit for selling draught beer and cider11, and a 1988 order requires beer and cider sold loose, rather than pre-packed, to come in a third, half or two-thirds of a pint or a multiple of half a pint12. The two-thirds measure was added in 201151.

Northern Europe

Ireland

Stout’s spiritual home, where most of the beer drunk is lager.

The usual measurepint, poured twice
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Stout, though less of it than the image suggests. In 2022 lager had 58.8% of the market and stout 34.7%52. Arthur Guinness leased his brewery at St James’s Gate in 1759 for 9,000 years at £45 a year, and stopped brewing ale in 1799 to concentrate on porter53.
Where
The pub, more than in most countries: 63.5% of beer, and 65.7% of stout, was sold in pubs, restaurants and hotels in 202252.
How it’s served
Stout is poured in two parts, left to settle in between. Guinness says the full pour takes 119.5 seconds, a figure that comes from the brand rather than from any independent standard54.
The habit
Waiting for the second half of the pour.
Why it’s like this
Ireland ranked fourth in the world for beer drunk per head in 202440.

Southern Europe

Spain

Small, cold, and nearly always with something to eat.

The usual measurecaña
  • Food first
  • Climate and season
What’s in the glass
Lager.
Where
The bar: more than 60% of Spanish beer is drunk in bars, restaurants and hotels23.
How it’s served
A caña is a small glass of draught, a little under a British half-pint; a doble is about twice that; a quinto is a fifth-of-a-litre bottle; a clara is beer cut with lemon soda22.
What comes with it
Tapas. In industry figures for 2015, up to 84% of Spaniards said they drank beer with food, and beer was the cold drink most often sold in bars24.
The habit
Ordering a small one, then another.
Why it’s like this
Beer and tapas are treated by the industry as a pair, el binomio cerveza-tapa24.

North America

United States

A beer market rebuilt from the wreckage of Prohibition, state by state.

The usual measurecan, bottle or pint
  • Law and tax
What’s in the glass
Light lager sells most; in 2025 Michelob Ultra overtook Modelo Especial as the best-selling beer, on Circana retail data cited by its brewer55. Alongside it, a craft sector of 9,578 breweries out of 9,724 in 2025, with 13.4% of volume56.
Where
Bars, sports bars, brewery taprooms and, above all, home.
How it’s served
Cold, in cans and bottles.
The habit
Homebrewing, legal at federal level only since 1 February 197957.
Why it’s like this
National Prohibition began on 17 January 1920 and was repealed by the 21st Amendment in 193358. Repeal left alcohol law to the states, which built three-tier systems separating producers, distributors and retailers so no one firm controls every step59. Homebrewing became federally legal only after Congress passed H.R. 1337 in October 197857.

North America

Mexico

The world’s biggest beer exporter drinks it with lime, salt and chilli.

The usual measure210 ml to 1.2 l bottles
  • Empire and trade
  • Climate and season
  • Food first
What’s in the glass
Pale lager and darker Vienna-style lager. The story that Emperor Maximilian brought the Vienna style in the 1860s does not hold up well: lager brewing in Mexico is hard to trace before about 1880, and Victoria, first made in Toluca in 1875, is probably the first Vienna-style beer60.
Where
The cantina, the street stall and the shop fridge.
How it’s served
By the bottle, in a ladder of sizes from the 210 ml coronita to the 940 ml caguama and the 1.2-litre caguamón61.
What comes with it
Cerveza preparada: a chelada is beer with lime and salt, a michelada adds sauces and spice. Where the name comes from is disputed33.
The habit
Lime in the neck of the bottle. Nobody knows who started it; the explanations range from rust on bottle caps to marketing34,33.
Why it’s like this
Mexico exported about US$6.3 billion of beer in 2025, around 36% of the world’s beer exports and almost four times the Netherlands in second place62. Mexico’s own consumption rose 5.4% in 202440.

South America

Brazil

One big bottle in the middle of the table and the smallest glass in this guide.

The usual measure190 ml copo americano
Servedice-cold
  • Climate and season
  • Immigration
What’s in the glass
Bottled lager, and chope: unpasteurised draught63.
Where
The boteco, an informal corner bar that began life as a small general store64.
How it’s served
Beer traditionally comes in 600 ml bottles shared among the table, or in the cheaper one-litre litrão64. Everyone fills their own copo americano, a 190 ml glass made by Nadir Figueiredo since 1947, which has been shown at the Museum of Modern Art in New York4. Chope is served estupidamente gelado, stupidly cold63.
The habit
Sharing: the bottle goes in the middle and every glass is filled from it3.
Why it’s like this
Heat explains the small glass: it is empty before the beer can warm, and the shared bottle keeps it moving3. German immigration from 1824 gave Brazilian brewing an early push63.

East Asia

Japan

Where the tax bands invented whole new kinds of beer.

The usual measuremug or shared bottle
Servedice-cold
  • Law and tax
  • Custom and ritual
  • Food first
What’s in the glass
Lager, and a family of beer-like drinks invented to dodge the beer tax: happoshu with little malt and ‘third beer’ with little or none9.
Where
The izakaya; and in summer the rooftop beer garden, a fixture since the first is said to have opened on an Osaka office block in 195365.
How it’s served
Cold, in a mug, often as the first order of the night: toriaezu biru, ‘beer for now’35. Many izakaya add a small dish, otoshi, to the cover charge35.
What comes with it
Yakitori35.
The habit
You pour for others and let them pour for you. Topping up your own glass is considered rude, and juniors fill seniors’ glasses first30.
Why it’s like this
Brewers built cheaper drinks around the tax bands, so the government decided in 2016 to close the gap in stages. On 1 October 2026 beer, happoshu and third beer moved to a single rate of ¥54.25 a 350 ml can10,66. The legal definition of beer was loosened in 2018 to allow 50% malt and fruit or spices9.

East Asia

South Korea

A country that eats fried chicken with its beer, or pours soju into it.

The usual measureglass, often mixed
  • Food first
What’s in the glass
Lager, frequently as somaek: soju and maekju, beer, mixed to about 10% alcohol67.
Where
The chicken shop.
How it’s served
By the glass, often mixed at the table67.
What comes with it
Fried chicken: the pairing has its own word, chimaek, chicken plus maekju68.
The habit
Daegu, known as the nation’s chicken mecca, holds a Chimac Festival68.
Why it’s like this
Lims Chicken, opened in 1977, is regarded as the first fried-chicken shop, and the 2002 World Cup, watched with snacks and beer, accelerated the trade69. Where somaek began is unknown, even to the people who drink it; one account traces the idea to journalists in 1980, and the 1997 financial crisis is credited with making it popular67.

East Asia

China

The biggest beer market on earth, and one where you may need to ask for it cold.

The usual measurebottle, or a bag
Servedoften room temperature
  • Empire and trade
What’s in the glass
Lager, from breweries founded by foreigners. Tsingtao began on 15 August 1903 as the Germania-Brauerei in German-held Qingdao, owned by the Anglo-German Brewery Company14,15. Harbin’s brewery was set up in 1900 by Jan Wróblewski, in a city growing up around the Chinese Eastern Railway70.
Where
The restaurant table.
How it’s served
Often at room temperature. Cold drinks are widely seen as bad for digestion, and Molson Coors once changed its temperature-sensitive cans in China to turn blue at a warmer point5. In Qingdao, at least before the pandemic, fresh draught was sold to take away in thin plastic bags15.
The habit
Asking bing de ma?: ‘is it cold?’6
Why it’s like this
China has been the world’s largest beer market by volume for 22 years running, though consumption fell 3.7% in 202440. Tsingtao passed to Japan’s Dai Nippon Brewery in 191615.

Southeast Asia

Vietnam

Beer brewed that morning and drunk by the evening, on a plastic stool.

The usual measureglass, street-side
  • Empire and trade
  • Climate and season
What’s in the glass
Bia hơi: light, unfiltered draught of about 3–4%, brewed daily without preservatives and meant to be drunk within a day16.
Where
The pavement, especially in Hanoi’s Old Quarter16.
How it’s served
By the glass, from a keg, for very little money16,17.
What comes with it
Peanuts, grilled pork skewers, spring rolls16.
The habit
Sitting low, on small plastic stools at the kerb16.
Why it’s like this
French colonists brought commercial brewing; the Hommel brewery opened in Hanoi in the 1890s16,17.

Oceania

Australia

Order a schooner in Adelaide and you get a different glass from one in Sydney.

The usual measure285 ml to 570 ml, name by state
  • Law and tax
  • Work and hours
What’s in the glass
Lager.
Where
The pub.
How it’s served
By glass names that change at state lines. A schooner is 425 ml in most states but 285 ml in South Australia, where a ‘pint’ is 425 ml; the 285 ml glass is a middy in New South Wales and Western Australia and a pot in Victoria and Queensland. Individual pubs vary too36.
The habit
The size names are a test of where you come from.
Why it’s like this
From 1916, as a wartime measure and under pressure from the temperance movement, pubs in several states, among them South Australia, Victoria, New South Wales and Tasmania, began closing at six. Men who finished work at five crammed in for an hour of hard drinking known as the six o’clock swill. Closing went back to ten in Tasmania in 1937, New South Wales in 1954, Victoria in 1966 and South Australia in 196713.

Southern Africa

South Africa

Home-brewed sorghum beer, once taken over by the state to pay for segregation.

The usual measureshared drum, or lager bottle
  • Law and tax
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Lager in volume, and umqombothi, a Xhosa beer of maize meal with sorghum and maize malt, fermented overnight and strained25.
Where
At home and at ceremonies; in the twentieth century, in municipal beer halls20.
How it’s served
Umqombothi is shared from a communal drum25.
The habit
Umqombothi is brewed for homecomings after initiation, for the ancestors, and for weddings and funerals25.
Why it’s like this
A Natal law of 1908 let town councils monopolise the sale of sorghum beer to African workers, and the profits paid for barracks, more beer halls and native administration, the model known as the Durban system. In 1929 it provoked riots and a boycott20. Under apartheid, sorghum-beer profits were a main source of finance for Black townships21. South Africa rose from tenth to seventh in world beer consumption in 202440.

Southern Africa

Zambia, Zimbabwe and Malawi

A sorghum beer you shake before you drink.

The usual measurecarton
  • Climate and season
  • Custom and ritual
What’s in the glass
Chibuku, made mainly from malted sorghum and maize, sometimes with millet71.
Where
Sold across all three countries26.
How it’s served
In a carton, where it carries on fermenting and getting stronger. The solids settle to the bottom, so it is shaken before drinking71,26.
The habit
Shaking the carton.
Why it’s like this
Chibuku was developed in the 1950s at Kitwe on Zambia’s Copperbelt by Max Heinrich, a German settler, working with local women brewers26.

West Africa

Nigeria

Where an export stout brewed for the colonies became a national drink.

The usual measurebottle
  • Empire and trade
What’s in the glass
Lager, and Guinness Foreign Extra Stout, a 7.5% stout descended from the export porters once shipped to the West Indies19.
Where
Bars.
How it’s served
By the bottle.
Why it’s like this
Guinness was being drunk in Africa by 182719. In 1962 it built a brewery at Ikeja, near Lagos, its first outside Britain and Ireland19, in what was then described as the largest single market in the world for Foreign Extra Stout18. By CNN’s account Nigeria later overtook Ireland as Guinness’s second-biggest market19.

Legends and the record

Six stories told over the bar, set against what the sources show.

Belgian beer got strong because spirits were banned in 1919.

Not supported

The Vandervelde law did ban spirits in cafés from 1919 to 1984. But average Belgian beer strength barely moved between 1900 and 1940, and most strong styles took off after 195045.

Emperor Maximilian gave Mexico its Vienna lager.

Unlikely

Maximilian reigned for about three years and there is no evidence of large-scale lager brewing in Mexico until around 188060.

A proper pint of Guinness takes 119.5 seconds.

Marketing

The figure is Guinness’s own, part of its brand ritual54.

The lime in a Mexican beer keeps flies off (or cleans rust off the cap).

Unknown

Several explanations circulate and none is documented34.

Ireland is a stout country.

Half true

It is a lager country with a large stout share: 58.8% lager against 34.7% stout in 202252.

The 1516 Reinheitsgebot was a consumer-protection law about purity.

Incomplete

It also fixed the price of beer, and kept wheat and rye for bakers41.
