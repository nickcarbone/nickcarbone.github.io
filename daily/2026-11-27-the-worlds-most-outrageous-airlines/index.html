The Outrageous Airlines

Please read before take-off

Seventeen airlines that sold seats with stunts, cheeky adverts, painted fish and public feuds: what each one actually did, and what became of them.

On the apron, in order of when the mischief started. Tap a fin to jump to its card. Colours are approximate and drawn by hand; no logos are reproduced.

Most airlines spend fortunes trying to look calm. A handful have gone the other way, deciding that the cheapest seat on a plane is attention, and that the quickest way to get it is to be rude, odd or loud.

This is a guide to those carriers, from Braniff’s 1960s colour-bomb to Ryanair’s running feud with Elon Musk. Some of the noise was a business model: if you can’t outspend the flag carrier on advertising, you can get the newspapers to cover your advert for free. Some of it was simply the personality of the person at the top. Several of the loudest names no longer fly, and that is part of the story too.

Each card gives the stunt or habit that made the airline famous, the context, and where things stand. Statuses are as of 27 November 2026. Everything is sourced; the numbers in the text link to the list at the end.

The advert

Copy designed to be complained about, so the complaint becomes the coverage.

The stunt

Arm-wrestling, cross-dressing, boarding a rival’s first flight in a boiler suit.

The paint job

Liveries and uniforms meant to be photographed: pink jets, a 129-foot salmon, labels on the toilets.

The price

Fares, fees and passes so stripped-back they became a joke the airline told about itself.

Show 17 airlines
Row 1Makeover 1965, grounded 1982

Braniff International

Dallas, Texas

  • Paint and uniforms
  • Adverts
  • No longer flying

In 1965 a staid Texas carrier hired the agency Jack Tinker & Partners, where Mary Wells was working, and came out the other side as “The End of the Plain Plane”. The designer Alexander Girard painted the fleet in seven solid colours and reworked roughly 17,000 details, from the interiors down to the soap packets and playing cards.1,3 Emilio Pucci dressed the stewardesses in space-age outfits that included plastic bubble helmets to protect hairdos on windy aprons, with layers designed to come off as the flight went on.2,3

Braniff then commissioned Alexander Calder to paint a DC-8-62 in 1973 (“Flying Colors”) and a Boeing 727 for the American Bicentennial.2 Its 1968 “When You Got It, Flaunt It” television campaign sat unlikely pairs side by side in the cabin: Andy Warhol with the boxer Sonny Liston, Salvador Dalí with the baseball pitcher Whitey Ford.4

The people behind it

Wells founded Wells Rich Greene in 1966 with Braniff as its first client, and married the airline’s president, Harding Lawrence, in 1967.3

What happened

After deregulation Braniff expanded hard, and it stopped flying on 12 May 1982, the first major US airline to go bankrupt in 20 years.95,5

Row 21949–1988

Pacific Southwest Airlines

San Diego, California

  • Paint and uniforms
  • No longer flying

PSA started in San Diego in 1949 with a single leased DC-3 to Oakland, and became California’s commuter airline.6 Its signature was a smile painted under the nose of every jet, which appeared in the late 1960s.6 Stewardesses wore miniskirts from 1966, and later hot-pink outfits with go-go boots; the airline called itself “The World’s Friendliest Airline” and let its pilots joke over the PA.6,7

The comeback

In 2006 US Airways painted the smile back on the nose of a retro-liveried jet.92

What happened

USAir agreed to buy PSA in December 1986 for $400 million, and its last flight was on 8 April 1988.93,8

Row 31971–

Southwest Airlines

Dallas, Texas

  • Stunts
  • Pricing

Herb Kelleher and Rollin King sketched the airline’s first route, a triangle between Dallas, Houston and San Antonio, on a cocktail napkin; it started flying in 1971 with three aircraft.9 When it listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 1977 it took the ticker LUV, a nod to its Love Field base.79

Its best-known stunt was a trademark dispute settled by arm-wrestling. Both Southwest and Stevens Aviation, an aircraft-maintenance firm, were using “Plane Smart” slogans. On 20 March 1992, at the Dallas Sportatorium, Kelleher, then 61, took on Stevens’s chief executive Kurt Herwald in “Malice in Dallas”. Kelleher lost, Herwald let Southwest keep using the slogan anyway, and both companies gave money to charity.10,11

“We take our competition seriously, but not ourselves.” Kelleher’s line on the episode.10

The habit

Southwest cabin crew became known for comic safety announcements, one of which went viral in 2014.80

Where it stands

Still flying, but less unusual: free checked bags ended for tickets bought from 28 May 2025, and open seating ended on 27 January 2026.12,13

Row 41984–

Virgin Atlantic

Crawley, England

  • Stunts

Richard Branson’s airline began on 22 June 1984 with one Boeing 747, “Maiden Voyager”, flying from Gatwick to Newark with the cricketer Ian Botham among the guests.14 From then on the founder was much of the advertising, and needling British Airways was a speciality.

The rivalry turned serious in the “dirty tricks” case. In January 1993 BA agreed to pay £610,000 in libel damages, £500,000 of it to Branson personally, and its chairman Lord King apologised.15,16 Virgin kept up the teasing for years: in 1999, when the BA-sponsored London Eye ran into trouble being raised, Virgin flew an airship over it reading “BA can’t get it up!!”.17,18

The other stunt

Branson’s most memorable shift in uniform was on someone else’s airline: see AirAsia, below.

Where it stands

Still flying; Delta Air Lines is a shareholder, and Virgin joined the SkyTeam alliance in 2023.19

Row 51985–

Ryanair

Dublin, Ireland

  • Adverts
  • Stunts
  • Pricing

Ryanair’s first scheduled service, from Waterford to Gatwick on 8 July 1985, used a 15-seat Embraer Bandeirante.20,21 It grew into a carrier of more than 200 million passengers a year,28 and its chief executive Michael O’Leary turned provocation into a free marketing channel.

In February 2009 he told the BBC that Ryanair was looking at putting coin slots on toilet doors, so that passengers “might actually have to spend a pound to spend a penny”; the company later said there were no immediate plans.22 In 2010 he floated standing-room “vertical seats” at the back of the cabin.23 Neither happened. The adverts have drawn regulators too: the UK Advertising Standards Authority banned a “Red hot fares & crew” ad in 2012 for causing widespread offence, and in 2021 ruled the “Jab & Go” Covid-vaccine ad misleading after 2,370 complaints.24,25

In January 2026, after O’Leary dismissed fitting Starlink wi-fi and Musk called him an “utter idiot” and mused online about buying the airline, Ryanair answered with a 100,000-seat sale aimed, it said, at “idiots on X”. O’Leary’s verdict: “I suspect he’s a bigger idiot than me.”26,27

The method

Say something outrageous, let it run for a news cycle, then quietly confirm it isn’t happening.

Where it stands

Ryanair reported 208.4 million passengers in the year to 31 March 2026, with a fleet of 647 aircraft.28

Row 61995–

easyJet

Luton, England

  • Adverts
  • Stunts
  • Pricing

Stelios Haji-Ioannou’s airline flew its first service, Luton to Glasgow, on 10 November 1995, at a launch fare of £29: pitched as the price of a pair of jeans.29,30 Booking was by phone only, with no tickets.31

On 22 May 1998, when British Airways launched its own low-cost offshoot, Go, Stelios and six colleagues bought seats on its first flight, turned up in orange boiler suits and offered every passenger a free easyJet flight.32 In 2003 easyJet ran a cleavage advert reading “Discover weapons of mass distraction”, and an unauthorised David Beckham ad (“Hair today… gone tomorrow?”) that ended with his agents asking for a charity donation.33,34

The rival

In 2010 Ryanair ran ads giving Stelios a long, Pinocchio-style nose; it apologised and paid him £50,100 in libel damages.35

Where it stands

Still flying, still orange.

Row 7Fernandes era from 2001

AirAsia

Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia

  • Stunts
  • Pricing

Tony Fernandes, a former music-industry executive, bought the loss-making AirAsia in 2001 for a token one ringgit, taking on its debts and two ageing Boeing 737s.36,37 The slogan was “Now Everyone Can Fly”, and in 2009 a “1 Million Free Seats” sale sold, by the airline’s own count, 402,222 seats in 24 hours.38

In 2010 Fernandes and Branson, who both owned Formula One teams, bet that whichever team finished lower would send its owner to work as a flight attendant on the other’s airline. Branson’s team lost. On 12 May 2013 he worked a charity flight from Perth to Kuala Lumpur in a red AirAsia skirt, serving drinks and spilling some on Fernandes.39,40

The look

Red everything: aircraft, uniforms and, for one day, Branson.

Where it stands

Still flying.

Row 8Tail heroes since 2002

Norwegian

Oslo, Norway

  • Paint and uniforms

Norwegian’s outrage was gentle and permanent: since its first jet in 2002, each aircraft has carried a portrait of a “tail-fin hero” on its tail.68 The heroes reflected its markets. Freddie Mercury joined the British set in 2017, alongside Roald Dahl, Bobby Moore, Amy Johnson and Freddie Laker; in March 2019 it added a group of British women including Ada Lovelace, Emmeline Pankhurst, Florence Nightingale and Mary Seacole.69,70 The San Francisco politician Harvey Milk became an American hero the same year.68

The effect

Every aircraft became a conversation starter, and every new face a press release.

Where it stands

Still flying, though it abandoned long-haul in January 2021.71 The tails were redesigned in 2022.72

Row 92003–2006

Hooters Air

Myrtle Beach, South Carolina

  • Paint and uniforms
  • No longer flying

The restaurant chain’s chairman, Bob Brooks, bought a charter carrier and turned it into an airline whose first scheduled flight, Myrtle Beach to Atlanta, ran on 6 March 2003.41,42 The jets were orange and white, and Hooters Girls flew on board to run games and trivia, alongside the certified cabin crew.94 At its peak, in summer 2005, it served 15 destinations, including Nassau.41

The verdict

“I’ve got a fair amount of money, but I don’t have enough to fix this animal,” Brooks said as he grounded it.42

What happened

Brooks grounded the airline in April 2006, three years after its first flight.42,41

Row 102004–

Wizz Air

Budapest, Hungary

  • Paint and uniforms
  • Pricing

Founded in September 2003 by József Váradi, a former Malév chief executive, Wizz flew its first service from Katowice in Poland on 19 May 2004.43,44 Its aircraft are hard to mistake: “Wizz Pink” was kept when the brand was refreshed in 2015, with the earlier purple replaced by cobalt blue.45

In August 2024 it launched an “All You Can Fly” subscription: €499 for a year at first, limited to 10,000 members, with flights bookable only within 72 hours of departure and a €9.99 fee per flight.46

The catch

The pass was unlimited in name; the 72-hour booking window and per-flight fee did the limiting.46

Where it stands

Still flying; it closed its Abu Dhabi subsidiary from 1 September 2025.47

Row 11Salmon jets 2005–2023

Alaska Airlines

Seattle, Washington

  • Paint and uniforms

Alaska is otherwise a sober airline, which makes its fish more surprising. On 1 October 2005 it unveiled “Salmon-Thirty-Salmon”, a Boeing 737-400 painted nose to tail as a king salmon by the artist Mark Boyle. Thirty painters took 24 days over it; an ordinary 737 takes about a week.48 A second fish, on a 737-800 and nearly 129 feet long, followed in 2012; Alaska called it among the most intricately painted commercial aircraft in the world.49,50

Who paid

The work was a promotion with the Alaska Seafood Marketing Institute.49

What happened

The second salmon flew its farewell in April 2023 and was succeeded in May by “Xáat Kwáani” (Salmon People) by the Tlingit artist Crystal Worl.51,52

Row 12Ads from 2008; wound down 2026

Spirit Airlines

Florida

  • Adverts
  • Paint and uniforms
  • Pricing
  • No longer flying

Spirit’s adverts ran on innuendo. In 2008 a “Many Islands Low Fares” sale drew a complaint from the flight attendants’ union; the president, Ben Baldanza, replied that the only thing Spirit found obscene was its competitors’ fares.84 In June 2010, during the BP oil spill in the Gulf of Mexico, it ran “Check Out The Oil On Our Beaches”, showing women in bikinis, and stood by it.85

In 2014 it formalised the model as the “Bare Fare”, covering a passenger and one personal item, with everything else extra, and repainted its planes a bright all-over yellow.86,87

The decline

Spirit filed for Chapter 11 in November 2024, emerged in March 2025, and filed again in August 2025.88,89

What happened

On 2 May 2026 it began an orderly wind-down, effective immediately, after rescue talks failed; a bankruptcy court approved the wind-down days later.90,91

Row 13Safety films since 2009

Air New Zealand

Auckland, New Zealand

  • Adverts
  • Paint and uniforms

Air New Zealand turned the safety video, the one part of a flight nobody watches, into its advertising. In June 2009 “Bare Essentials” showed pilots, cabin crew and the chief executive Rob Fyfe wearing nothing but body paint in the shape of their uniforms, to promote domestic fares with “nothing to hide”.53 In 2011 the fitness instructor Richard Simmons led crew in neon lycra in “Fit to Fly”.54

Then came Middle-earth. “An Unexpected Briefing” (2012), with Gollum and a cameo from Peter Jackson, passed 9 million views in eight days by the airline’s count, and the 2014 “Most Epic Safety Video Ever Made”, directed by Taika Waititi with Elijah Wood, reached 16 million in a week.55,56,57

The trick

A captive audience of every passenger, on every flight, plus everyone online.

Where it stands

Still flying.

Row 14Flying 101, 2010; grounded 2022

kulula.com

Johannesburg, South Africa

  • Adverts
  • Paint and uniforms
  • No longer flying

South Africa’s lime-green budget airline treated its planes as joke books. Its “Flying 101” Boeing 737-800, rolled out in January 2010, was covered in arrows and labels: the exit rows were the “throne zone”, the registration was the “secret agent code”, and the toilets were marked with a reference to the mile-high club.58,59,60

In the run-up to the 2010 World Cup, kulula called itself the “Unofficial National Carrier of the You-Know-What”. FIFA’s lawyers objected to what they called an unauthorised association, kulula withdrew the ad, and the row did the publicity work for it.61

The parent

kulula was the low-cost brand of Comair, which had flown since 1946.62

What happened

Comair grounded all flights, kulula’s included, on 1 June 2022 and moved into liquidation days later.62,63

Row 15Dance video 2010

Cebu Pacific

Manila, Philippines

  • Stunts

In September 2010 a video of Cebu Pacific cabin crew dancing a safety demonstration to a mash-up of Lady Gaga and Katy Perry went round the world, passing 6 million views within days.64,65 The airline said a standard demonstration was still given before take-off and called the dance part of its “fun culture”; a flight attendants’ association and the women’s group Gabriela called it demeaning.65

The airline also built a reputation for in-flight games, from crew-led “bring me” contests to quizzes run over social media.66 Its campaigns play on Juan, the Filipino everyman, as in #EveryJuanWillFlyAgain in 2020.67

The argument

Fun for passengers, or a gimmick at the cabin crew’s expense: both views were aired in 2010.65

Where it stands

Still flying.

Row 162011–

Vietjet

Hanoi, Vietnam

  • Paint and uniforms
  • Stunts

Vietjet flew its first service, Ho Chi Minh City to Hanoi, on 25 December 2011.73 It soon became known abroad as the “bikini airline”. In 2012 Vietnam’s civil aviation authority fined it for an unapproved in-flight show, a bikini performance to celebrate a new route to the beach resort of Nha Trang.74 In 2018 models in bikinis performed on the flight carrying the national under-23 football team home, and the regulator fined the airline again; its founder Nguyen Thi Phuong Thao apologised.75

“We don’t mind people associating the airline with the bikini image… If that makes people happy, then we are happy.” Nguyen Thi Phuong Thao, quoted in Fortune.76

The founder

Bloomberg reported in 2016 that the image had helped make Thao Vietnam’s first woman billionaire.77

Where it stands

Still flying.

Row 172012–2019

WOW air

Reykjavík, Iceland

  • Paint and uniforms
  • Pricing
  • No longer flying

Skúli Mogensen founded WOW in 2011 and painted its Airbus fleet purple. The pitch was transatlantic flights via Reykjavík at fares from $99.78,81 The marketing matched: a “Purple Friday” sale, and in 2018 a summer “travel guide” job paying $4,500 a month to tour Iceland and post about it.82

The numbers

In 2018 it carried 3.5 million passengers with 11 aircraft.83

What happened

After rescue talks with Icelandair and the investor Indigo Partners fell through, WOW stopped flying on 28 March 2019, stranding passengers.78,83

What the noise was for

The pattern across these cards is that outrage was cheapest for the airlines with the least money. Braniff’s makeover came while US fares were still set by regulators, so image was one of the few things an airline could compete on. Ryanair, easyJet, Spirit and kulula used provocation because a row with a regulator, a rival or FIFA bought coverage that a newcomer could not afford to buy outright.

It did not guarantee survival. Of the seventeen, six no longer fly: Braniff, PSA, Hooters Air, kulula, WOW and Spirit. The ones still going tend to have kept the joke and dropped the parts that cost money: Southwest now charges for bags and assigns seats, and Ryanair’s toilet coin slots and standing seats stayed in the newspapers.12,13,22
