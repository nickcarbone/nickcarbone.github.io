General register of cocktail births · Twenty entries

The Cocktail Birthplace Atlas

where they say it was born

Twenty classic cocktails credited to a particular bar, each tested against the earliest printed record, with what stands at the address now and where to order the drink today.

Published 24 October 2026 · About 31 minutes · Prices checked 24 October 2026

DailyNo. 006MMXXVI

Bars like to say where a drink was born, and they say it on menus, plaques and the sides of bottles. This register takes 20 of those claims and tests each against the earliest record the desk could find: when the drink first appears in print, who is named, and whether the dates fit the people.

Every entry is written up as a certificate, with its verdict stamped on it and a note on the room it was supposedly born in. Below each is a short register for ordering the drink at or near its birthplace today. The five signature drinks of The Hotel Bar Atlas (the Singapore Sling, Vieux Carré, Red Snapper, Hanky Panky and Serendipity) are left out here.

Prices were checked on 24 October 2026. Only four of the bars publish the drink's price online; elsewhere the register gives a dated report, or says there is none.

I The scale

Each certificate carries one of four stamps. They judge the claim as the bar tells it, not the drink.

Documented. A period source ties the drink to the bar or its maker, at or close to the claimed date.

Credited. Widely and plausibly attributed and consistent with the record, but no contemporaneous proof of the birth itself.

Contested. Serious rival claims, or evidence that cuts against the standard story.

Legend. The story is unsupported, or the record contradicts it.

1. John Collins

later the Tom Collins
Place of birth, as claimedLimmer's Old House
Father, as claimedJohn Collins, head waiter
Date, as claimedBefore the 1840s
The room todayGone

Limmer's, on the corner of Conduit Street and George Street (now St George Street) in Mayfair, was a sporting hotel known for its gin punch; Captain Gronow's memoirs put that reputation as early as 1814.1,2 Its head waiter was real. The New Sporting Magazine described a John Collins flourishing there in 1837, and an American newspaper in 1876 called him “the first and most famous of Limmer's waiters”.1 The Oxford Companion gives him as John Collin, about 1771 to 1843.3

What no period source shows is Collins inventing anything. The drink seems to have grown out of the house gin punch and taken the waiter's name, and the Oxford Companion says the tall drink had reached Canada and Australia by 1864, and the United States soon after.3

Earliest record foundHarry Johnson's New and Improved Bartender's Manual (1882) prints both a John Collins (Holland gin) and a Tom Collins (Old Tom gin), and is the earliest recipe the desk could confirm. Sources disagree on whether Haney's 1869 manual or Jerry Thomas's 1876 guide carried one first.2,4
Rival claimantsHow “John” became “Tom” is argued over: the Great Tom Collins Hoax of 1874, a New York prank in which people were told a Tom Collins was insulting them in a nearby bar, or simply the Old Tom gin in the glass.3,4
First known specOld Tom gin, sugar and a few dashes of lemon or lime over broken ice, topped with a bottle of plain soda (Johnson, 1882).2
Poured nowLondon dry gin, fresh lemon and syrup, built tall and topped with soda. In the United States “John Collins” came to mean a whiskey version after Prohibition.3
What stands there nowLimmer's closed in 1876, its furniture was auctioned and the building was reported demolished that year; a hotel later reopened under the old name.1 The desk could not confirm what stands on the corner today.

Registration district: New Orleans, United States

2. Sazerac

Place of birth, as claimedSazerac Coffee House, Exchange Alley
Father, as claimedAntoine Amédée Peychaud, in the popular story
Date, as claimed1850s
The room todayA namesake took the name later

The menu version: Antoine Peychaud, a Royal Street pharmacist, served brandy with his own bitters, and the Sazerac Coffee House on Exchange Alley made the drink famous in the 1850s. That story comes largely from Stanley Clisby Arthur's 1937 book Famous New Orleans Drinks and How to Mix 'Em.8,9 Part of it collapses on dates: the word “cocktail” was in print by the first decade of the 1800s, when Peychaud was a small child.9

David Wondrich's reading of the record points to the 1890s instead. An 1895 newspaper profile called the Sazerac House bartender Vincent Miret the city's best mixer of whisky cocktails, the “Sazerac” cocktail first appears in print in 1899, and in 1902 the Times-Picayune reported the local belief that Miret's successor, Billy Wilkinson, had created it.10 The bar's link to the drink is real; the 1850s birth is not shown.

Earliest record found1899 in print, per Wondrich; the earliest printed recipe is in William Boothby's The World's Drinks and How to Mix Them (1908), which calls for Selner bitters rather than Peychaud's.10,8
Rival claimantsNo rival bar, but rival fathers: Peychaud in the 1850s against Miret and Wilkinson in the 1890s.10 The coffee house's early proprietors and Thomas Handy's dates also differ between sources.8,11
First known specWondrich's reconstruction of the 1890s drink: rye, rich syrup, Peychaud's and a dash of Angostura, an absinthe rinse and lemon peel, stirred and poured into a chilled glass.10
Poured nowRye, syrup, Peychaud's, an absinthe or Herbsaint rinse and a lemon twist, usually without the Angostura. The Roosevelt lists Herbsaint.12,6
What stands there nowAfter Prohibition the Sazerac House traded on Carondelet Street; it closed in 1948, according to the Oxford Companion.11 The Roosevelt hotel's Sazerac Bar is a namesake: Seymour Weiss bought the rights to the name in 1949 and opened a bar on the hotel's Baronne Street side on 26 September that year; in 1959 the name moved to the hotel's 1938 main bar, which still carries it.13,12,14 The Oxford Companion places the coffee house in the old Merchants' Exchange; that building burned in 1960 and a hotel was built on its site in 1969.11,15

3. Ramos Gin Fizz

Place of birth, as claimedImperial Cabinet Saloon
Father, as claimedHenry C. Ramos
Date, as claimed1888
The room todayGone

Henry Charles Ramos's fizz was famous enough that by 1895 the press called his Imperial Cabinet, at Gravier and Carondelet, the “Gin Fizz place”.16 That same year a New York book printed it as the “New Orleans Fizz”.17 He later moved to the Stag saloon on Gravier Street, which by one account went through 5,000 eggs a week.16,18

The year on the menus, 1888, is bar lore; Wondrich puts Ramos's first shaken fizz at about 1892 to 1894.17 The man and the bar are documented close to the date. The exact birthday is not.

Earliest record foundPress in 1895, and the same year a New York book's “New Orleans Fizz”. Ramos's own recipe, from an interview with Don Higgins of the New Orleans Item-Tribune, was in print by 23 September 1928, when the paper ran it after his death.17,16
Rival claimantsNone for the drink.
First known specOld Tom gin, lemon and lime, sugar, egg white, cream and a few drops of orange-flower water, shaken long and finished with a little soda (1928).16
Poured nowMuch the same; the Roosevelt uses Henry Ramos Old Tom gin, which the Sazerac Company launched in 2019.6,18
What stands there nowBoth saloons closed with Prohibition. The Roosevelt bought the rights to market the drink from Ramos's heirs, which is why its Sazerac Bar is the drink's home now.17 What stands on the two saloon sites today could not be confirmed.

Registration district: Yokohama, Japan

4. Bamboo

Place of birth, as claimedGrand Hotel
Father, as claimedLouis Eppinger
Date, as claimed1890s
The room todayA namesake took the name later

Louis Eppinger, a German-born bartender, ran the bar of Yokohama's Grand Hotel from about 1890, and Japan claims the sherry-and-vermouth Bamboo as his.22,23,19 The trouble is the calendar. The Western Kansas World of 11 September 1886 described the “bamboo”, three parts sherry to one of vermouth, as a new drink an unnamed Englishman had introduced to New York bars.24 That is three or four years before Eppinger reached Japan.22,24

William Boothby credited Eppinger with the drink in 1908, but he did not say it was made in Yokohama.24 If Eppinger did invent it, Difford's suggests it happened earlier, in his San Francisco or Portland years.24 The Yokohama birth does not survive the record.

Earliest record found1886, in American newspapers; first drinks-book recipe in Thomas Stuart's Stuart's Fancy Drinks and How to Mix Them (1904).24,22
Rival claimantsThe unnamed Englishman of 1886, and a New York “Boston Bamboo” of the 1890s.22,24
First known specThree parts sherry to one of vermouth, probably sweet (1886).22,24
Poured nowDry sherry and dry vermouth, often in equal parts, with orange bitters; the sweet version is usually called an Adonis.22
What stands there nowThe Grand Hotel closed after the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake. Hotel New Grand opened in 1927 as its successor and keeps the drink on its bar list.25,26,19

5. Last Word

Place of birth, as claimedDetroit Athletic Club
Father, as claimedUnknown
Date, as claimedBy 1916
The room todayMembers only

A souvenir menu sent out with the Detroit Athletic Club's magazine in the summer of 1916 lists the Last Word by name and price, with no recipe.31 Difford's gives the price as 35 cents, twice that of a Manhattan.32 Nobody knows who made it. The first printed recipe, in Ted Saucier's Bottoms Up (1951), credits the club and says the vaudeville performer Frank Fogarty “first introduced” it about 30 years earlier; the club's own history notes that Fogarty visited in December 1916, months after the menu, and suggests he carried the drink to New York.32,31

The drink sank from view until the bartender Murray Stenson found it in Saucier's book and put it on the menu at Seattle's Zig Zag Café in the early 2000s.31,32

Earliest record foundThe club's 1916 menu; the first recipe is Saucier's Bottoms Up (1951), “Courtesy Detroit Athletic Club”.31,32
Rival claimantsNone to the club. The creator is unknown.
First known specEqual quarters of gin, maraschino, green Chartreuse and lime, shaken and served in a cocktail glass (1951).32
Poured nowStill usually equal parts; Difford's leans slightly towards the gin.32
What stands there nowThe club still occupies its 1915 Albert Kahn building at 241 Madison Street.33 It put the Last Word back on its menu in 2009 and named a rooftop lounge after it, but you need a member to get you in.31,28,27 Whether the room where it was poured in 1916 survives is not recorded in the sources the desk found.

Where to order it The club, as a member's guest; otherwise Chartreuse Kitchen & Cocktails, Midtown Detroit

6. Negroni

Place of birth, as claimedCaffè Casoni, Via de’ Tornabuoni
Father, as claimedFosco Scarselli, for Count Camillo Negroni
Date, as claimed1919
The room todayMoved or rebuilt

Florence's story: in 1919 the bartender Fosco Scarselli made Camillo Negroni his usual Americano with gin in place of soda water. Its chief modern source is Luca Picchi's 2002 book on the drink.37,38 No printed source from 1919 or 1920 ties the drink to Casoni.37

Equal parts gin, Campari and Italian vermouth appear in Paris in 1929 as the “Campari Mixte”, without the name.38 The name itself turns up in print in the late 1940s.37,38 Wondrich doubts Camillo was a count, and a branch of the Corsican Negroni family credits a 19th-century French general instead; Wondrich calls the letter behind that claim anachronistic.37,39

Earliest record foundSame recipe, different name: “Campari Mixte” in L'Heure du Cocktail (1929). By name: Wikipedia gives 1947, Difford's 1949.38,37
Rival claimantsGeneral Pascal-Olivier de Negroni, in Senegal in the mid-1800s, per his family; a proposed San Francisco origin.39,37
First known specEqual parts gin, Campari and Italian vermouth with a lemon zest (1929, unnamed). Early named versions vary, including a long drink and one heavy on Campari.38,37
Poured nowStirred equal parts, over ice, with orange.37
What stands there nowCasoni became Caffè Giacosa. In 2001 Giacosa moved one door down to make room for a Roberto Cavalli boutique, and Cavalli closed both in 2017.40,41 The Giacosa name was bought and reopened in July 2023 as Giacosa 1815, across Via della Spada from the old corner.42,43 The desk could not confirm what occupies the original unit.

7. Pisco Sour

Place of birth, as claimedMorris' Bar, Calle Boza
Father, as claimedVictor V. Morris
Date, as claimedAbout 1920
The room todayGone

Victor Morris, an American from Salt Lake City, ran a bar near the Plaza San Martín from 1916 until his death in 1929.47,48 An advertisement in the magazine Mundial on 22 April 1921 credited “Mister Morris” with the drink, which makes him the documented popularizer.45

Invention is less certain. A 1903 Lima cookbook already has a punch of pisco, sugar and egg white, and the bitters and egg white of today's drink are often credited to Mario Bruiget, who trained at Morris' and later worked at the Hotel Maury.47,49,45,48 A Chilean claim for an 1872 invention in Iquique was traced by the researcher Guillermo Toro-Lira to a newspaper item about the whiskey sour.45

Earliest record foundThe name in the Lima magazine Hogar in 1920 and the Mundial advertisement of 1921, both per later research.45
Rival claimantsThe Hotel Maury's oral tradition; the debunked Chilean claim.49,45
First known specNo recipe from Morris's bar survives; it was probably a plain pisco, lime and sugar sour like the whiskey sours of the day.45,47
Poured nowPisco, lime, syrup and egg white, shaken, with drops of Angostura on the foam.45
What stands there nowMorris' Bar closed in 1929.48 A 2015 RPP piece estimated the site as shoe shops next to the Galerías Boza on Jirón de la Unión.49

Where to order it Gran Hotel Bolívar bar, Plaza San Martín

Getting in
The hotel's bar was taking part in Lima's pisco festivities in July 2026.44 Its house serve is the oversized “Pisco Sour Catedral”.45,46

8. Boulevardier

Place of birth, as claimedHarry's New York Bar
Father, as claimedErskine Gwynne
Date, as claimed1927

Harry MacElhone's Barflies and Cocktails (1927) is the house book of the bar at 5 rue Daunou. In an essay by Arthur Moss within it, the Boulevardier appears as the drink of Erskine Gwynne, the American who ran a Paris monthly called Boulevardier: one third each of Campari, Italian vermouth and bourbon.54,55,56

That is a period source naming the drink and its man in the year claimed. It then disappeared from cocktail books until Ted Haigh revived it in 2007.54

Poured nowHeavier on the whiskey: Ted Haigh's 2007 revival used 3:2:2, now the international standard, and Punch's favourite versions use two parts to one each, mostly with rye.54,56
What stands there nowSame address. The bar opened in 1911 as the New York Bar; most accounts say MacElhone bought it in 1923, though Difford's has him founding it. The MacElhone family still runs it.57,50

9. Hemingway Daiquiri

Papa Doble, Hemingway Special
Place of birth, as claimedEl Floridita
Father, as claimedConstantino Ribalaigua Vert
Date, as claimed1930s
The room todaySame room still pouring

Constantino Ribalaigua, who began at the Floridita in 1914 and owned it from 1918, built his reputation on the daiquiri.60,61 The story is that Ernest Hemingway, living at the Ambos Mundos hotel in the 1930s, asked for one without sugar and with double rum: the Papa Doble.60,62 Grapefruit and maraschino, which turned it into the Hemingway Special, are credited by Difford's to another Floridita bartender, Antonio Meilán.62

The bar printed souvenir recipe booklets in 1934, 1935, 1937 and 1939.63 The desk could not confirm which edition first names a Hemingway drink, so the attribution rests on the bar's tradition and later accounts.

Earliest record foundThe bar's own booklets of the 1930s; a Hemingway entry in them is reported but was not confirmed by the desk.63
Rival claimantsNone to the bar. Within it, the credit is split between Ribalaigua and Meilán.62
First known specTwo jiggers of rum, lime, grapefruit juice and a few drops of maraschino, no sugar, blended with ice.61,64
Poured nowMost modern recipes add syrup and far more maraschino.62
What stands there nowSame room. The bar has stood at Obispo and Monserrate since 1817; it is state-run, and a bronze Hemingway has leaned on the end of the counter since 2003.60,65

10. Hurricane

Place of birth, as claimedPat O'Brien's, St Peter Street
Father, as claimedBar staff; the Oxford Companion names Louis Culligan
Date, as claimed1940s
The room todaySame room still pouring

Pat O'Brien's says the Hurricane was a wartime answer to a whiskey shortage, when distributors made bars take rum in bulk.69,70 The Oxford Companion credits its head bartender, Louis Culligan.71

Two things weaken the claim. The first Pat O'Brien's recipe in print dates from 1956, in Cabaret Quarterly. And a rum company booklet of 1941, Ronrico's The Rum Connoisseur, already has a “Hurricane Punch” of rum, passion fruit and citrus.71,72 Whether Culligan invented the drink or adopted the booklet's is left open.71

Earliest record foundRonrico's “Hurricane Punch” (1941); the Pat O'Brien's recipe, given by Culligan to Cabaret Quarterly (1956).71
Rival claimantsThe Ronrico booklet, possibly linked to New York's Hurricane Club.71
First known specFour ounces of gold rum, two of red passion-fruit mix and the juice of two lemons (1956).71
Poured nowEqual parts of the bar's own Hurricane mix and its rum in a 24oz glass.68
What stands there nowStill pouring at 718 St Peter Street, where the bar has been since the early 1940s (sources say 1941 or 1942).69,73,70 No source says whether the first Hurricane was mixed here or at its earlier address in the same street.

11. Irish Coffee

Place of birth, as claimedFoynes flying-boat terminal
Father, as claimedJoe Sheridan, chef
Date, as claimed1943
The room todayMoved or rebuilt

The story told at the old flying-boat terminal at Foynes, on the Shannon estuary: on a winter night in 1943 a New York flight turned back in bad weather, and the chef Joe Sheridan put whiskey in the stranded passengers' coffee.76,77 The catering manager Brendan O'Regan adopted it.76,77

Early print supports the region. A Washington paper in 1945 and the New York Herald Tribune in 1948 described Irish coffee served at Shannon.78 Sheridan's own claim to have invented it at Foynes appears in print later, in Stanton Delaplane's columns of the mid-1950s.78 Delaplane is the San Francisco travel writer who, with the owner of the Buena Vista, introduced it to America in November 1952.79

Earliest record found9 December 1945, the Washington Sunday Star, from Shannon Airport; per Barry Popik's citations.78
Rival claimantsThe drinks writer Eric Felten has suggested a Dublin pub, the Dolphin, around 1940.80
First known specIrish whiskey, hot coffee, sugar and cream, about one part whiskey to two of coffee (1945).78
Poured nowAt the Buena Vista: two sugar cubes, coffee and whiskey in a small stemmed glass, topped with lightly whipped cream.75,79
What stands there nowThe terminal building is now the Foynes Flying Boat & Maritime Museum, which serves the drink in an Irish Coffee Lounge and a 1940s-style restaurant.81,74 The Foynes restaurant itself closed in 1945 when service moved to Shannon.77

14. Piña Colada

Place of birth, as claimedCaribe Hilton, and Barrachina
Father, as claimedRamón “Monchito” Marrero; or Ricardo García; or Ramón Portas Mingot
Date, as claimed1954, or 1963
The room todayMoved or rebuilt

San Juan has two birthplaces and three fathers. The Caribe Hilton credits its bartender Ramón “Monchito” Marrero with the rum, coconut cream and pineapple drink in 1954; Difford's notes Marrero himself gave 1952.100,101 A colleague at the same hotel, Ricardo García, claimed 1953.102 In the old city, Barrachina restaurant has a plaque saying Ramón Portas Mingot created it there in 1963.103,101

The name is older than all three. Travel magazine described a Cuban “pina colada” of rum, pineapple, lime and sugar in 1922, and a 1950 New York Times column listed a Cuban version made with coconut milk.101,102 What Puerto Rico can claim is the modern drink, built on Coco López cream of coconut from the 1950s.102

Earliest record foundThe name in 1922 (Travel), a coconut version in 1950 (New York Times), both Cuban; no 1950s source tying it to either San Juan venue was found.101,102
Rival claimantsEach other, and Cuba.
First known spec1922: pineapple juice, rum, lime and sugar, shaken and served long, no coconut.101
Poured nowRum, pineapple and cream of coconut, usually blended with ice.100,101
What stands there nowThe Caribe Hilton still serves it, more than 53,000 a year by its count, but the original bar room is not identified.104 Barrachina still trades at 104 Calle Fortaleza.103

15. Caesar

Place of birth, as claimedCalgary Inn
Father, as claimedWalter Chell
Date, as claimed1969
The room todayMoved or rebuilt

Walter Chell, who managed a restaurant at the Calgary Inn, made the drink for the opening of the hotel's new Italian restaurant in 1969, taking his cue from spaghetti alle vongole: vodka, tomato juice and clam nectar he mashed himself.106,107,108

Clam-and-tomato vodka drinks existed before it, including a Mott's and Seagram “Clamdigger” promoted in 1968, but none was called a Caesar, and the Oxford Companion leaves Chell's claim to the name intact.106,107 No printed reference from 1969 itself was found.

Earliest record foundNo 1969 print found; the story is Chell's, told in later interviews.107,106
Rival claimantsEarlier clam-tomato drinks, none named Caesar.106

V Too many fathers

Four of the most famous drinks are missing because no single bar can claim them. The Manhattan's story of a party at the Manhattan Club, with Jennie Jerome as hostess, fails because she was in England, having given birth to Winston Churchill weeks earlier; a rival account names a Broadway bartender called Black.140 The Margarita has at least seven claimants, from Taxco to Galveston, and its name first appears in print in 1953.141 The Martini is fought over by Martinez and San Francisco, and San Francisco put the question to a mock trial in 1983; the Martinez, its likely ancestor, is in print by 1884.142,143,144 The Old Fashioned is claimed for Louisville's Pendennis Club, but Chicago drinkers were ordering old-fashioned cocktails around 1880, before the club was founded; the Oxford Companion says the club may have popularised the drink but cannot be its birthplace.145,146

VI A confession: the Seelbach

Struck from the register

In 1995 Adam Seger took over the bar at the Seelbach Hotel in Louisville and announced that he had found its pre-Prohibition house cocktail: bourbon, triple sec and two kinds of bitters, topped with sparkling wine, supposedly born when a bartender spilled Champagne into a Manhattan.147 The Courier-Journal wrote it up, and it went into cocktail books, including Gary and Mardee Haidin Regan's New Classic Cocktails (1997).147,148 In 2016 Seger told Robert Simonson of The New York Times that he had invented the drink and its story. As Simonson put it: “None of it is true.”147,149 Even the fiction has two dates; accounts set it in 1912 or 1917.147,148

VII How to judge a claim on a menu

Start with print. Find the first time the drink appears under its name, and see whether that source mentions the bar; when the earliest evidence comes decades after the claimed date, the story was probably written backwards. Then ask who is telling it: the bar, the company that owns the name or the rum in the glass, or someone with nothing to sell. Check that the dates fit the people and the ingredients, as Peychaud's birth year and the Bamboo's 1886 newspaper do not. Last, look for the same drink somewhere else first. A claim that clears all of that with a period source is documented; one that fits the record without such a source is credited; serious rivals make it contested; and a story the record contradicts is a legend.
