Six Lost Beers, Brewed Again

Six regional beers that faded or vanished, what the evidence says about each, and a starting recipe for every one at homebrew scale, with one written out in full for brew day.

A beer can disappear for many reasons: war, a change in tax law, a new kind of malt, the arrival of refrigeration, a brewery that gets bought and shut. What survives is usually paper. Brewing logs, trade manuals, regulations and the occasional analysis of a bottle are how brewers now work backwards to beers nobody alive has tasted in their original form.

The six here were chosen because each has a clear home, a documented reason for existing, and ingredients you can still buy. Two vanished completely and came back (Lichtenhainer and grodziskie). One was killed by Prohibition (Kentucky common). One survives but has changed so much that the modern beer and the 19th-century description barely match (bière de garde). One never died but stays local and is protected by law (sahti). And one, London porter, gave rise to every stout in the world before fading from its home city.

The judging bodies are candid about the limits. The Beer Judge Certification Program puts most of these in a "Historical Beer" category for styles that are now known mainly through recreations27, and its porter guideline says plainly that it describes the modern beer, not every historical variation22. So every recipe below marks which choices rest on the historical record and which are a modern brewer's substitution.

The six

in order of colour, palest first

Lichtenhainer

Lichtenhain, near Jena · Thuringia, Germany
Peak late 1800s · last brewed 1983 · revived 1997
1

A pale, smoky, mildly sour wheat beer from the villages around Jena: Lichtenhain, Wöllnitz, Ammerbach, Ziegenhain and Winzerla78. The first written account dates from 1833, though the beer is probably older8. It was at its most popular at the end of the 19th century, when it was brewed across Thuringia, as far away as Eisenach67.

It outlasted most of northern Germany's top-fermented beers, which lager pushed aside, and was brewed until 1983, when the last batch was made at Brauerei Ed. Barfuss Söhne in Wöllnitz. A brewpub in Wöllnitz brought it back in 199778.

The BJCP places it in the same family as Berliner weisse, gose and grodziskie, and describes it as less acidic than Berliner weisse: roughly a smoked gose without the salt and coriander6. The old texts disagree on the grain. Rommel and Fehmann (1915) describe an all-barley beer, lightly smoked and very lightly hopped, soured by a lactic infection that could be spontaneous or deliberately started. Ohlberg (1927) gives one-third wheat to two-thirds barley7.

  • Pilsner malt 1.00 kg (2.2 lb)
  • Wheat malt 1.00 kg (2.2 lb)

Hops

  • German noble-type hop (Hallertau or Tettnang), 4% AA 14 g (0.49 oz) · 60 min

Yeast

Clean ale yeast (US-05 or similar), with a Lactobacillus culture for kettle souring; or LalBrew Sourvisiae on its own

Method

  1. Mash at 66°C (151°F) for 60 minutes.
  2. Kettle sour: bring the runnings to the boil briefly to sanitise, cool to the temperature your Lactobacillus culture asks for, keep the wort unhopped, and sour until it tastes clearly tart but softer than a Berliner weisse. Taste every 12 hours.
  3. Boil 60 minutes with the single hop addition. The boil kills the bacteria.
  4. Ferment at 18–19°C (64–66°F).
  5. Carbonate briskly, around 3 volumes.

What to expect

Straw-gold, hazy, light-bodied and spritzy, with campfire smoke on the nose and a gentle lactic tartness. BJCP: OG 1.032–1.040, 3.5–4.7% ABV6.

Piwo grodziskie

Grodzisk Wielkopolski · Greater Poland
Peak 1880s–1930s · closed 1993/94 · revived 2015
2

A beer of 3 per cent or so that tastes of oak smoke, bitter hops and very little else, served with a huge white head. It is made from wheat malt, most or all of it smoked over oak; Brew Your Own notes that around 1900 not all the wheat was smoked14. The town of Grodzisk, called Grätz under Prussian and German rule, gave the beer its German name, Grätzer1315.

Craft Beer & Brewing dates malting records in Grodzisk to 142615; Brew Your Own puts brewing there back at least 700 years and production at its peak, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, at more than 85,000 barrels a year14. Rail lines carried it beyond the region. After the Second World War, under communism, breweries were consolidated, advertising was banned and speciality styles were discouraged. Lech bought the last producer after 1989 and closed it in the early 1990s; Brew Your Own gives 1993 and Craft Beer & Brewing 19941415.

Polish homebrewers kept the recipe in use, through a competition from 2007 and the "Grodziskie Redivivus" project from 20111415, and Browar Grodzisk resumed commercial brewing in the town in 20151415. Its head brewer, Marcin Ostajewski, says it is nicknamed "Polish champagne" and recommends 3.5 to 4 volumes of carbonation15.

Method

  1. Step mash: Browar Grodzisk uses four steps including a protein rest14. A workable version is 45°C for 20 minutes, 63°C for 30, 72°C for 30, then mash out at 76°C. Modern choice
  2. Boil 60 to 90 minutes. Browar Grodzisk boils for an hour and, as Brew Your Own reports, finds a longer boil makes little difference. Historical boils were longer: about two hours according to the BJCP, three according to Brew Your Own1314.
  3. Ferment cool and clean, at the lower end of your yeast's range.
  4. Fine or cold-crash, then bottle-condition to 3.5–4 volumes of CO215. Use bottles rated for that pressure: ordinary thin-walled bottles can burst.

What to expect

Pale gold and brilliantly clear, with a dense, lasting head, clean oak smoke, firm bitterness and a dry, crisp finish. BJCP: OG 1.028–1.032, 2.5–3.3% ABV, 20–35 IBU13.

Sahti

Häme (Tavastia) and southern Finland
Brewed for at least 500 years · EU-protected name since 2002
3

Strictly speaking Finland is Nordic rather than Scandinavian, but sahti is the region's farmhouse ale with the firmest paper trail. It is a strong, sweet, cloudy beer flavoured with juniper and often made with rye, traditionally brewed for weddings and summer feasts and drunk within a week or two1718. The BJCP says it has been brewed for at least 500 years17; Gordon Strong, citing an Institute of Brewing and Distilling paper, dates the first detailed description to 178018. It is often called Tavastian ale after the Häme region, where Sysmä, Joutsa, Kuhmoinen and Lammi are known for it20; Strong says production today is most concentrated in the southwest18.

Two things set the method apart. The mash is run off through a bed of juniper twigs in a kuurna, a long trough traditionally hollowed from a log. And the wort is usually never boiled, going straight from the trough to fermentation171820. Juniper does much of the work hops do elsewhere. Fermentation is fast and warm with Finnish baker's yeast, which gives the banana and clove notes1718.

Unlike the others here, sahti never vanished. In 2002 the EU registered the name as a traditional speciality guaranteed19, and commercial breweries have made it since the 1980s18.

Grain and adjuncts

  • Pilsner malt 6.60 kg (14.6 lb)
  • Dark Munich malt 900 g (2.0 lb)
  • Rye malt 800 g (1.8 lb) · about 10% of the grain
  • Rice hulls 500 g (1.1 lb)

Hops

  • Low-alpha German hop (e.g. Spalt), in the mash 20 g (0.71 oz) · mash

Yeast

Finnish baker's yeast if you can get it; otherwise a German wheat-beer yeast

Method

  1. Long step mash, rising slowly from about 45°C to 70°C in 5°C steps of 30 minutes, then add the juniper and finish with rests at 75°C and 85°C. This follows Gordon Strong's published schedule18.
  2. Line the bottom of your lauter tun with juniper twigs (and rice hulls) and run off slowly. Collect about 19 litres; sparge only lightly.
  3. Do not boil. Chill the wort straight away.
  4. Pitch at around 10°C (50°F) and let it rise to room temperature as fermentation starts; cool again when it slows18.
  5. Serve still or barely carbonated. Keep it below 5°C (40°F) and drink it within two weeks18.

What to expect

Hazy, orange-brown, full and sweet, with banana and clove from the yeast, a woody, piney edge from the juniper and bread from the rye. BJCP: OG 1.076–1.120, 7–11% ABV17.

Bière de garde

French Flanders · Nord and Pas-de-Calais
Recorded 1880 · bottled from the 1940s · cult in the 1970s
4

The name means beer for keeping. Farm breweries along the French–Belgian border brewed a stronger beer late in the season to store through the warm months before refrigeration, a practice shared with Belgian saison10. Phil Markowski, writing in the Oxford Companion to Beer, says that farmhouse heritage is well established but that written documentation is scarce10.

What documentation there is describes a different beer from today's. In 1880 Louis Figuier wrote of "bière de garde de Lille" aged six to eight months in large wooden barrels, with a very vinous flavour; in 1905 R. E. Evans described it as deliberately allowed to turn sour10. Most of the region's breweries were destroyed in the first half of the 20th century11.

The modern style comes from Brasserie Duyck, which first bottled its Jenlain in the 1940s. Duyck roughly doubled the old draught strength of 3–4 per cent to 6–8 per cent, and its Champagne-style corked bottles made it a cult beer among Lille's students by the late 1970s1011. In the 1970s and 1980s La Choulette, Thellier, Castelain and St Sylvestre moved from table beer and lager to the style10. Today's benchmark is a clean, malty amber, and some brewers still use lager yeast10.

Method

  1. Mash low and long, at 64°C (147°F) for at least 90 minutes, for a dry finish12.

  3. Pitch at 19°C (66°F) and let it rise to about 21°C (70°F) after a few days12.
  4. Once fermentation is finished, hold it near 0–4°C for four to six weeks. That cold "garde" is what turns a rough strong ale into a smooth one.
  5. Carbonate to 2–2.5 volumes12.

What to expect

Copper-amber, with toasty, bready malt, a little caramel and a dry finish. Hops and yeast stay in the background. If you add a Brettanomyces portion, expect a drier, more cellar-like beer closer to the 19th-century descriptions.

Kentucky common

Louisville, Kentucky · Ohio River
Mid-to-late 1800s until Prohibition
5

Louisville's everyday saloon beer: dark amber, highly carbonated and cheap. It was brewed almost exclusively around the city from the later 19th century until Prohibition. Sources differ on when it began; Wikipedia says the 1850s, while the BJCP says after the Civil War14. By the end it accounted for about three-quarters of beer sold in the area12.

It was quick. The beer was racked into casks while still fermenting, krausened, and sealed so it carbonated in the saloon cellar, reaching customers six to eight days after mashing in12. It used six-row barley, a large share of corn and a little black and caramel malt. One theory, which the BJCP calls speculation, is that German immigrant brewers made a dark cream ale and added roasted grain to counter the city's carbonate-heavy water1.

Many modern recipes sour it. The record does not support that. Brewing logs from about 1900 show no sour mash, no long acid rest and no extended ageing12. The idea seems to come from the 1906 Wahl and Henius note about a "bacteria taste". Leah Dienes and Dibbs Harting, who studied the logs, attribute that to hop-tolerant spoilage bacteria from dirty casks at smaller breweries2. Sour versions are entered in a different BJCP category1.

London porter

London · the Thames breweries
First recorded 1721 · gone from Britain by the 1940s or 1950s
6

Porter is first mentioned in print in London in 1721, as a development of the brown beer the city already drank: more heavily hopped and kept longer252223. Its name is traditionally linked to London's working-class porters, who carried loads for a living22. The popular story that a Shoreditch brewer, Ralph Harwood, invented it to replace a drink called "three threads" appears decades later; Martyn Cornell traces it to John Feltham's Picture of London of 1802 and finds no contemporary evidence for it23.

Until about 1800 porter matured for six to 18 months in huge vats, and publicans and brewers blended aged beer with fresh, typically two parts young to one part old25. The hydrometer showed brewers how little extract brown malt gave compared with pale malt25, and after Daniel Wheeler's 1817 roasting drum, black malt could supply colour so that pale malt supplied the sugar2425.

Gravities fell, ageing was dropped, and by the 1870s porter was a lower-gravity, unaged beer in decline22. Accounts of the end differ: Wikipedia gives 1941 for the last British porter, while the BJCP says it had disappeared by the 1950s2522. Guinness stopped making its Irish porter in 197425. It was revived by craft brewers from the 1970s2225. This recipe aims at the mid-19th century, modelled on the Truman's porter of the 1840s that Goose Island and Wimbledon Brewery recreated from records found by the historian Ron Pattinson: mostly pale malt, with brown malt for flavour and a little black for colour26.

Brew-day sheet: Kentucky common

the complete recipe

Of the six, Kentucky common is the one to brew first. Its ingredients are in every homebrew shop, it needs no souring, smoking, juniper or months of cold storage, and it is ready to drink about two weeks after brew day. It is also the style where the record is best: handwritten logs from Louisville's four largest breweries survive, and they agree on ingredients and method2. This sheet follows those logs where it can and says where it doesn't.

Clean American ale yeast (US-05, WLP001 or Wyeast 1056) Modern choice1 pack
Strike and sparge water, total, approx.29 L

The logs show a corn share of 36–38 per cent and a dark malt share of 1.5–2 per cent2; this bill uses 36 per cent maize and 2 per cent black. Flaked maize is pre-gelatinised, so it can go straight into the mash. Historical grits needed a separate cereal mash with a boil2.

Method

  1. Water. Louisville's water was high in carbonate, and the black malt helped bring the mash pH down12. With soft or reverse-osmosis water, Brew Your Own's version acidifies the brewing water to pH 5.5 and adds a teaspoon of calcium chloride to the mash3.
  2. Mash. Single infusion at 67°C (152°F) for 60 minutes, or step it: 55°C for 15 minutes, 63°C for 30, 70°C for 153. The historical mash held a dextrin rest at 69–70°C (156–158°F)2. Add the black and crystal malt at the end of the mash if you want a smoother colour with less roast.
  3. Mash out and sparge at 76–77°C (168–170°F), collecting about 25 litres for a 90-minute boil (scale with batch size).
  4. Boil 90 minutes. First Cluster addition at 60 minutes from the end, second at 30, the Saaz or Hallertau at flame-out. The breweries boiled for two hours with hops at four points2.
  5. Chill to 18°C (64°F) and pitch. The logs show pitching at about 60°F (15.5°C) and fermenting at 66–68°F (19–20°C)2.
  6. Ferment three to five days, letting it rise to no more than 21–22°C. The originals finished primary in three to four days2.
  7. Carbonate high, about 2.8–3 volumes of CO2. The breweries krausened the beer with fresh wort into sealed casks so it carbonated in the saloon cellar12; force carbonation in a keg gets the same result faster. Modern choice
  8. Drink fresh. This was a "present use" beer, out of the brewery six to eight days after mashing in23. Aim to finish it within a month.

Expect: a clear, amber-brown beer, dry and lightly toasty, with a whisper of caramel and very little roast; the BJCP calls it "a clean, dry, refreshing, slightly malty dark beer with high carbonation"1. It should not be sour (see the style notes above).
