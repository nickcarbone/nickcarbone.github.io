The Night Sky Over Twenty Cities

For twenty cities, what light pollution has taken from the sky, the nearest properly dark site and how to reach it, when to go, and what you’ll see when you get there, plus a short guide to finding your way round the stars without an app.

Most people now living have never seen a properly dark sky from home. Falchi and colleagues’ 2016 world atlas of artificial sky brightness found that about 83% of the world’s population, and more than 99% of people in the United States and Europe, live under light-polluted skies. The Milky Way is hidden from more than a third of humanity, including 60% of Europeans and nearly 80% of North Americans.[1]

It is getting worse faster than satellites suggested. Kyba and colleagues analysed more than 50,000 naked-eye reports to the Globe at Night citizen-science programme between 2011 and 2022, mostly from Europe and North America, and found the sky brightening by about 9.6% a year. Satellite measurements had put the rise in global surface brightness at around 2% a year.[2, 3] Kyba’s illustration: a child born where 250 stars are visible would see about 100 by their eighteenth birthday.[3]

The good news is that a dark sky is still within a day’s travel of most big cities, and often much less. For each of twenty cities below, the desk picked the nearest well-documented dark site, usually one certified by DarkSky International, the body formerly called the International Dark-Sky Association, and said so plainly where it isn’t. The national figures for “what the city has lost” come from Falchi’s country table, because the atlas does not publish city percentages; a city centre is always worse than its country’s average.[1]

What the city has lost

The United Kingdom has one of the brightest night skies in Falchi’s atlas: 77.0% of its people cannot see the Milky Way from home, and 26.0% live under skies so bright the eye never fully switches to night vision. The atlas names the London-to-Liverpool and Leeds belt among the widest areas on Earth where the Milky Way is gone.[1]

The dark site

The reserve was announced on 10 May 2016, with a core of 418 km² and a buffer of 1,104 km²; DarkSky noted that nearly 17 million people in Greater London and the south-east could reach it. Be clear about what that means: about two-thirds of the park measured at Bronze, the lowest of the three sky-quality grades. This is a good sky by English standards, not a desert one.[6, 7]

Getting there

Rail does most of the work. The park authority lists ten Dark Sky Discovery Sites, among them Bignor Hill, Harting Down, Iping Common, Devil’s Dyke, Ditchling Beacon and Birling Gap. Southern Railway pairs Bignor Hill with Pulborough station, Ditchling Beacon with Hassocks or Lewes, and Seven Sisters Country Park with Seaford or Eastbourne. The last stretch from a station is a walk or a taxi, and late trains home are the real constraint.[8, 9]

What you’ll see

At 51°N the Milky Way’s bright core in Sagittarius climbs only about 10° above the southern horizon, and from late May to mid-July the sky never gets fully dark. Go between September and March instead: the autumn Milky Way runs overhead through Cygnus and Cassiopeia, Andromeda’s galaxy is a smudge you can find without optics, and Orion carries the winter. Bignor Hill is in the reserve’s core.[8]

What the city has lost

Same national picture as London: 77.0% of the UK cannot see the Milky Way from home.[1] Scotland’s central belt sits inside that figure.

The dark site

Galloway was named a Gold-tier Dark Sky Park in November 2009, the first in the UK, in the same round that recognised Hungary’s Zselic, which makes it among the first in Europe. STV reports a rating of two on the Bortle scale, where one is the darkest.[10, 11, 12] The Scottish Dark Sky Observatory on the park’s edge burned down on 23 June 2021; a replacement at Clatteringshaws Loch was expected to open in late 2026, so check before you plan around it.[12]

Getting there

Plan on a car: there is no railway inside the park, and the official viewing points are reached by forest roads. Forestry and Land Scotland names Clatteringshaws Loch, Stroan Loch and Glentrool as viewing sites, with information at the Kirroughtree visitor hub. Clatteringshaws is about 125 km from Edinburgh and about 90 km from Glasgow as the crow flies.[10]

What you’ll see

This far north (55°N) the galactic core never gets clear of the horizon haze, and the sky stays in twilight from early May to early August. Winter is the season: long nights, the Milky Way arching through Perseus and Auriga, and Orion in the south.

What the city has lost

Ireland is less washed out than Britain: 45.2% of its people have lost the Milky Way at home, according to Falchi’s table.[1]

The dark site

Mayo became Ireland’s first International Dark Sky Park in 2016, covering more than 110 km² of Ballycroy National Park and the Wild Nephin wilderness. It was a Gold-tier designation, and DarkSky named it Dark Sky Place of the Year in 2024. A lighting project at Newport church cut its light output by half.[13, 14] (Kerry’s International Dark Sky Reserve, named in 2014,[19] is the older of Ireland’s two big designations but further from Dublin.)

Getting there

Rome2Rio lists a train from Dublin Heuston to Castlebar and then the 460b bus to Ballycroy, about five hours in all, against roughly three and a half hours by car. The bus stops in Ballycroy village; the free National Park shuttle bus that links the trailheads runs only in summer (early June to the end of August), so out of season the dark viewing spots beyond the village need a car or taxi. The Ballycroy visitor centre, the Claggan Mountain Coastal Trail and the Letterkeen trailhead are the named starting points; the viewing sites are open all year.[16, 15, 13]

What you’ll see

At 54°N the summer nights are too bright and the core too low (it peaks around 7°). Come in the long nights: the autumn Milky Way in September and October, then Orion, Taurus and the Pleiades through the winter.

What the city has lost

Falchi’s team worked out that someone living near Paris would have to travel about 900 km, to Corsica, central Scotland or Cuenca province in Spain, to reach large areas where the zenith is essentially unaffected by artificial light. Nationally, 58.9% of the French cannot see the Milky Way.[1]

The dark site

No DarkSky-certified site exists within the Paris region that the desk could find. The nearest is the Morvan in Burgundy, about 230 km south-east as the crow flies, which DarkSky lists as an International Dark Sky Reserve from 2025. It covers 1,297 km² across 50 municipalities, with a 455 km² core where SQM sensors averaged 21.6 magnitudes per square arcsecond, and about 99.8% of public lighting points are switched off between 10pm and 6am.[17, 18]

Getting there

Treat it as a car trip; the park is rural and the desk could not confirm a practical rail-and-bus route to its core. Go on a moonless night and use the park’s own information for viewing points.[17]

What you’ll see

At 47°N the core reaches about 14° above the southern horizon, so summer is worth the trip: July and August bring the brightest part of the Milky Way low in the south after dark. Autumn and winter skies are darker for longer.

What the city has lost

Germany fares better than France, Poland or the Benelux countries: 41.6% of its people have lost the Milky Way, and only 2.7% live under the very brightest skies.[1] The park’s own flyer says its north-west is its darkest part, being furthest from the light domes of Berlin and Rathenow.[22]

The dark site

Westhavelland became Germany’s first International Dark Sky Reserve on 12 February 2014, about 70 km west of Berlin. DarkSky describes some 750 km² of public and private land and says nearly six million people in Berlin and Brandenburg live within reach.[20] The Rathenow flyer lists readings of about 21.2 to 21.8 magnitudes per square arcsecond at its marked sites, including the Gülpe sports field and the north shore of the Gülper See.[22]

Getting there

berlin.de suggests the RE4 regional train towards Rathenow, then on to Premnitz, about 2 km from the nature-park centre at Milow; by car it is the B188 or B102. Nennhausen station is itself a listed viewing spot. Line numbers and timetables change, so check before you go.[21, 22]

What you’ll see

At 52.7°N the summer core barely clears the horizon (about 8° at best) and late-June nights stay in twilight. Autumn and winter are the reward: the Milky Way overhead, the Andromeda galaxy, and Orion rising over the wetlands.

What the city has lost

38.5% of Hungarians cannot see the Milky Way from home, lower than most of western Europe.[1]

The dark site

Zselic, in the hills above Kaposvár, was named one of Europe’s first International Dark Sky Parks in 2009. DarkSky says the Triangulum galaxy can be seen with the naked eye on clear nights and zodiacal light is often visible in spring and autumn; Kaposvár’s tourism office says no city lies within 30 km of the park’s centre.[23, 11, 25]

Getting there

Zselic is about 170 km from Budapest as the crow flies. Trains and buses reach Kaposvár, but the last stretch, about a quarter of an hour through the forest, needs a car or taxi. The visitor centre at Zselickisfalud has a 40 cm telescope, a 53-seat planetarium and a lookout tower; programmes are guided and mostly in Hungarian, with English by arrangement.[25, 24]

What you’ll see

At 46°N the core rises to about 15°. Summer evenings give the brightest Milky Way; autumn puts the faint Triangulum galaxy, which DarkSky says can be seen with the naked eye here, high in the dark.[23]

What the city has lost

Falchi’s table puts 76.7% of Portugal’s people under skies where the Milky Way has gone, close to Britain’s figure.[1]

The dark site

Alqueva, around the great reservoir in the Alentejo, was certified in 2011 as the world’s first Starlight Tourism Destination. That certificate comes from the Starlight Foundation, not DarkSky International. The organisers claim about 286 clear nights a year (Sky at Night reported it as 286 clear days) and sky brightness of up to 21.6 magnitudes per square arcsecond at some sites; those are their own certification figures, not an independent survey.[27, 26]

Getting there

Sky at Night put it at under two hours’ drive from Lisbon. Rede Expressos coaches run from Lisbon to Reguengos de Monsaraz in about two and a half hours, several times a day according to Omio, but getting from town to a dark viewing spot realistically needs a car or a booked tour.[27, 28]

What you’ll see

At 38°N the core climbs to about 23°, high enough to matter. Photographers recommend May to July for the Milky Way, and the organisers say deep-sky objects down to 52° south can be seen.[27]

What the city has lost

77.6% of Americans cannot see the Milky Way from where they live, and Falchi’s atlas names the Boston-to-Washington corridor among the largest areas where it is lost.[1]

The dark site

Cherry Springs became the second International Dark Sky Park in the world in 2008, at Gold tier, on a 700 m plateau in Potter County. Pennsylvania had declared it the state’s first dark sky park in 2000, and in 1998 it was already listed on an amateur astronomer’s dark-sky registry as free of sky glow in every direction. DarkSky says it gets 60 to 85 nights of ideal observing a year.[30, 29]

Getting there

It is about 340 km from Manhattan as the crow flies, more than five hours by road, and there is no practical public transport. Two areas matter: the Night Sky Public Viewing Area, for stays of a few hours, and the Overnight Astronomy Observation Field for registered all-night users. Park lighting is shielded red.[31, 29]

What you’ll see

At 41.7°N the core reaches about 19°, so July and August evenings show the bright summer Milky Way low over the south. Two star parties a year fill the field; come on another weekend if you want quiet.[30]

What the city has lost

76.2% of Canadians have lost the Milky Way at home, and 48.3% live under skies too bright for full night vision, one of the highest shares in Falchi’s table.[1]

The dark site

Torrance Barrens was the first Dark-Sky Preserve the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada ever designated, in 1999: 1,906 hectares of bare bedrock, wetland and low forest about 11 km from Gravenhurst. There is no power, no programme and no lighting.[32]

Getting there

By car: Highway 400 to Barrie, Highway 11 to Gravenhurst, Highway 169 to Torrance, then south on Southwood Road for about 7 km. Vehicles aren’t allowed on the viewing area; it is a short walk in from the car park. Designated campsites were added in 2022.[32]

What you’ll see

At 45°N the core reaches about 16°. Late summer is best for the Milky Way; the bedrock gives an open horizon, and the long autumn nights are good for the Andromeda galaxy and the northern sky.

What the city has lost

The reserve exists because of loss: DarkSky says light pollution over the mountain had more than doubled in the previous 20 years. Nationally, 76.2% of Canadians cannot see the Milky Way.[34, 1]

The dark site

Mont-Mégantic was the world’s first International Dark Sky Reserve, named in 2007, and spreads over 34 municipalities in the Eastern Townships. About 2,500 light fixtures were replaced, cutting light pollution by about 25%, and the observatory says sky quality is back near the level of 1978, when it opened.[34, 33]

Getting there

It is a car trip, about 190 km east of Montreal as the crow flies. The ASTROLab at the foot of the mountain runs public astronomy evenings in summer; the summit observatory is open only on guided visits. Sépaq says a new summit viewing site, the Esplanade des Étoiles, is due in 2027.[35]

What you’ll see

At 45.5°N the core peaks around 16° above the southern horizon. Summer evenings at the ASTROLab are the easy way in; autumn brings darker, longer nights.

What the city has lost

Same national figure as New York: 77.6% of Americans have lost the Milky Way from home.[1]

The dark site

Newport, at the tip of the Door Peninsula on Lake Michigan, became Wisconsin’s first International Dark Sky Park in 2017, one of 48 worldwide at the time. It is 2,373 acres and Wisconsin’s only wilderness state park, with backpack-only camping. The idea came from an amateur astronomer, Ray Stonecipher, and it took about four years from his idea to the designation.[36]

Getting there

Plan on a car; it is about 380 km north of Chicago as the crow flies and further by road. The state recommends visiting in daylight first to learn the viewing areas; DarkSky notes an unobstructed view east over the lake.[37, 36]

What you’ll see

At 45°N the summer core reaches about 16°, low in the south. The lakeshore suits eastern risings: the summer Milky Way climbing out of Lake Michigan in the evening, and the winter stars over the water.

What the city has lost

Phoenix has no separate figure in the sources the desk found; nationally, 77.6% of Americans cannot see the Milky Way.[1]

The dark site

Flagstaff passed the world’s first outdoor lighting ordinance in 1958 to protect Lowell Observatory, and in 2001 became the first International Dark Sky Place of any kind. Three national monuments nearby, Sunset Crater Volcano, Walnut Canyon and Wupatki, were made International Dark Sky Parks together on 4 May 2016; the Park Service says Wupatki, 26 miles north, has almost no light pollution.[38, 39, 40]

Getting there

Flagstaff is about 230 km by road, two and a half to three hours up I-17. PlanetWare lists FlixBus and Greyhound services of about three hours and Groome shuttles from Sky Harbor airport. For the darkest sky, you then need a car out to the monuments; check their opening hours, which change.[41, 40]

What you’ll see

At 35°N and about 2,100 m, the core reaches about 26°. Spring and summer give the Milky Way; the monsoon brings afternoon storms in high summer, so late spring and early autumn are often clearer.

DarkSky’s John Barentine said in 2017 that Joshua Tree was within a few hours’ drive for more than 22 million people. Nationally, 77.6% of Americans have lost the Milky Way at home.[42, 1]

The dark site

Joshua Tree was designated in 2017 at Silver tier, not Gold. The Park Service says light domes from the Coachella Valley, Los Angeles and Las Vegas reach the park, and said in 2017 that Gold status could follow if lighting improved in the Morongo Basin and Coachella Valley.[42, 43]

Getting there

Drive, and keep driving east. The Park Service names Keys View as affected by light domes and Pinto Wells, in the remote east, as much less so. DarkSky says the eastern wilderness is the best of the park.[43, 44]

What the city has lost

74.4% of Chileans cannot see the Milky Way from home, a figure that mostly reflects Santiago, where much of the country lives.[1] Chile’s lighting rules once covered only the observatory regions of the north; a national standard issued in October 2023 extended them countrywide and restricted blue-rich LEDs.[50]

The dark site

The world’s first International Dark Sky Sanctuary was announced on 9 August 2015: more than 35,000 hectares of AURA observatory land around Cerro Tololo, named for the poet Gabriela Mistral, who grew up in the Elqui Valley. The sanctuary itself sits behind a guarded gate.[45, 46, 47]

Getting there

The public way in is the valley around it. Cerro Tololo, 80 km east of La Serena, runs free tours by reservation; the municipal Mamalluca observatory, about 9 km north-east of Vicuña, runs guided night sessions with telescopes. La Serena is the gateway city, about 400 km north of Santiago.[48, 47, 49]

What you’ll see

At 30°S the galactic core passes almost straight overhead, the Magellanic Clouds are up all year, and the Southern Cross and Canopus are bright fixtures. The southern winter, May to August, gives long nights with the core high.

What the city has lost

46.1% of South Africans live under skies where the Milky Way is no longer visible.[1]

The dark site

Sutherland has no DarkSky certificate; it has something stronger, a legal one. The Karoo and Sutherland Central Astronomy Advantage Areas are declared under the Astronomy Geographic Advantage Act and managed by a state authority to protect the observatories. The South African Astronomical Observatory’s site, home of the Southern African Large Telescope, stands at 1,798 m.[52, 51] DarkSky’s certified places in southern Africa, such as the NamibRand reserve in Namibia and the !Ae!Hai Kalahari sanctuary on the edge of the Kgalagadi, are much further from Cape Town.[53]

Getting there

It is about 370 km by road, via the N1 to Matjiesfontein and north into the Karoo; plan on driving. The SAAO visitor centre runs tours and stargazing by booking.[51]

What you’ll see

At 32°S the core passes near the zenith in the southern winter, the Magellanic Clouds never set, and June to August brings the longest, coldest nights.

What the city has lost

66.7% of Australians have lost the Milky Way at home.[1] Australian astronomy has felt it before: DarkSky notes that Canberra’s light pushed observers from Mount Stromlo towards Siding Spring in the 1950s.[54]

The dark site

Warrumbungle became Australia’s first International Dark Sky Park in 2016, covering 23,312 hectares. Siding Spring Observatory, home of the 3.9-metre Anglo-Australian Telescope, sits alongside and is part of the dark-sky area; the park marked ten years in 2026.[54, 55]

Getting there

What the city has lost

56.4% of New Zealanders cannot see the Milky Way from home, though only 4.8% live under the very brightest skies.[1]

The dark site

Aotea became the first island anywhere to be named an International Dark Sky Sanctuary, in 2017. About 1,000 people live there permanently, nearly 60% of the island is conservation land, and most homes run off-grid. Getting there

What you’ll see

At 36°S the galactic core passes high overhead in winter, and Aotea’s eastern beaches look out over open ocean. Summer brings warm nights and easier ferries; winter brings the best Milky Way.

What the city has lost

The University of Hong Kong’s survey of 199 sites, 2008–09, found the average sky 16.1 magnitudes per square arcsecond, about 160 times brighter than a natural sky; the brightest sites, Mong Kok and Wan Chai, were over 500 times brighter than the darkest.[4] Falchi’s atlas names the area around Hong Kong among the largest regions on Earth where the Milky Way is lost.[1]

The dark site

Hong Kong has no DarkSky-certified place; an “Astropark” opened at Sai Kung in 2010 and was never developed further.[5] The darkest places HKU measured were Shui Hau on southern Lantau and the East Dam of High Island Reservoir in eastern Sai Kung, averaging 20.1 magnitudes per square arcsecond. That is still about four times brighter than the natural sky of 21.6 the same paper uses, but it is the best the territory offers.[4]

Getting there

Green minibus 9A runs from Pak Tam Chung to the East Dam on weekends and public holidays, according to the government hiking site; KMB buses 94, 96R and 289R reach the Sheung Yiu stop near Pak Tam Chung (the last two at weekends and on holidays only), and otherwise it is a walk of about 9 km. The East Dam is about a 40-minute drive from Sai Kung town. The minibus timetable changes, so check before you go.[63, 64]

What you’ll see

At 22°N the core climbs to about 39°, and the Southern Cross skims the southern horizon on spring nights, a few degrees up at best. Look east over the sea, away from the city’s glow.

India has the smallest share of the twenty countries here: 19.5% of Indians cannot see the Milky Way from home. That is a national average across a mostly rural population, and Delhi is nothing like it.[1]

The dark site

India’s first dark sky reserve was agreed in September 2022 and declared that December, set up by agreement between the Union Territory of Ladakh, the Leh hill council and the Indian Institute of Astrophysics, around the Indian Astronomical Observatory at Hanle. It is not a DarkSky International site. It covers roughly a 22 km radius in the Changthang Wildlife Sanctuary, above 4,200 m.[67, 65]

Getting there

Fly to Leh, then it is about 270 km by road, roughly six hours; a bus runs twice a week (Leh to Hanle on Wednesdays and Saturdays). Two dozen locally trained Astronomy Ambassadors, two-thirds of them women, offer telescope sessions for a fee. The observatory itself is open only in daylight. Check permit rules for the area with the Leh authorities before you travel.[66, 65]

What you’ll see

At nearly 33°N and over 4,200 m, the core reaches about 28° through very thin, dry air. Spring and autumn avoid the coldest winter nights; altitude sickness is a real risk, so allow days to acclimatise in Leh.

Without an appReading the sky

  1. Give your eyes half an hour

    The cones in your eyes adapt in about ten minutes, the rods, which do the work at night, in around half an hour; full dark adaptation takes more than 30 minutes. A glance at a white phone screen sets you back. Astronomers read charts by dim red light, which is what the red-light switch at the top of this page is for.[72]

  2. Look beside things, not at them

    The centre of your vision has few rods, so faint objects vanish when you stare. Look slightly to one side, “averted vision”, and they reappear.[72]

  3. Measure with your hand

    At arm’s length, with one eye closed, your little finger covers about 1°, three middle fingers about 5°, a fist about 10°, and an outstretched hand from thumb to little finger about 25°. timeanddate.com puts Polaris about three fists from Dubhe in the Plough. Every altitude in this guide can be checked this way: a core that peaks at 10° sits one fist above the horizon.[69]

  4. North: follow the Plough

    Find the seven stars of the Plough (the Big Dipper). Draw a line from Merak to Dubhe, the two stars at the end of the pan, and carry it on out of the top: the next fairly bright star is Polaris, which sits within a degree of the north celestial pole. Your latitude is roughly its height above the horizon.[68]

  5. South: the Cross and the Pointers

    There is no bright southern pole star. Instead, extend the long axis of the Southern Cross, then draw a second line that bisects the two Pointers, Alpha and Beta Centauri; the south celestial pole lies where the lines meet. Drop straight down to the horizon and you are facing due south.[70, 71]

  6. Pick the night, not just the place

    The calendars in this guide ignore the Moon. A full Moon brightens the sky over even the best site, so aim for the week either side of new moon, and remember that the summer core is an evening object in July and August and a pre-dawn one in spring.

Method

Distances are straight-line (great-circle) distances from the city centre to a representative point in each site, calculated by the desk; roads are longer. The month calendars and core altitudes were also calculated by the desk for each site’s latitude and longitude: “full darkness” is the time the Sun is more than 18° below the horizon on the 15th of the month, and “core up” is the part of that time when the galactic centre (Sagittarius A*, at about right ascension 17h 46m, declination −29°) stands more than 5° above the horizon. The Moon, weather and local terrain are ignored. “Best” months are the desk’s judgement from those figures. Transport details and opening arrangements were checked in November 2026 and change often; check before you travel.
