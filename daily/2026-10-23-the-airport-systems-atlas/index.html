The Airport Systems Atlas

How hubs, alliances, transfer rules, borders and airport rail links shape a journey, with five worked trips that judge a route by its door-to-door time and full cost rather than its headline fare.

An airfare is the one number every booking site puts in large type, and it is often the least useful one. The time it takes to reach the airport, the hour you are told to arrive early, the second security check at a transfer, the passport queue on landing and the coach from an airport 80 kilometres from the city on the ticket all count, and none of them are in the price. This guide explains the systems behind those hidden hours: why airlines funnel passengers through hubs, what an alliance really promises, which rules protect a connection and which leave you on your own, where border lines run through terminals, and how trains have become part of the airline network. It ends with five journeys worked out step by step, and a calculator for doing the same with your own.

AWhy hubs exist→

Until 1978, the US government decided which American airlines could fly which domestic routes and what they could charge. President Jimmy Carter signed the Airline Deregulation Act on 24 October that year, and the airlines that survived the shake-out, in the Smithsonian's words, "adopted the hub-and-spoke system using a few major airports as central connecting points".1 Transport geographers describe the same shift: freed to plan their own routes, carriers consolidated "hub-and-spoke networks feeding traffic to and from strategically located hubs".2

The appeal is arithmetic. A network that flies every city to every other city directly needs a route for each pair: for n cities that is n(n−1)/2 routes, while a hub joins them with n−1.3 Ten spoke cities plus the hub make eleven places and 55 possible city pairs. Point to point, that is 55 routes. Through the hub it is ten, and every one of those ten flights carries a mix of people heading to many different final destinations, which lets an airline fill a big aircraft on a route that could never fill one with local passengers alone.

The cost lands on the traveller as a change of plane, and on the airport as congestion. The Smithsonian's account of deregulation notes that hubs removed many nonstop flights and that fares rose at airports where one airline came to dominate,1 the arrangement the industry calls a fortress hub.

Banks and waves

A hub only works if the connections are short. Many hub airlines therefore schedule in banks or waves: a cluster of arrivals inside a short window, a pause while passengers and bags change aircraft, then a cluster of departures. Research on European networks found that airlines "concentrated their networks by adopting or intensifying wave-system structures" after the EU liberalised air transport, because waves multiply the number of workable connections a hub offers for the same number of flights.4 The price is a runway and terminal that are overloaded for an hour and quiet for the next. The alternative, a "rolling" hub that spreads flights evenly across the day, uses aircraft and staff more efficiently but makes the average connection longer; American Airlines tried de-peaking its hubs and later went back to banks.5

What it means for youIf a connection on a booking site looks oddly long, you may have landed at the end of one bank and be leaving at the start of the next. An earlier or later inbound flight in the same bank can cut the wait by hours.

The superconnectors

Some hubs sit in places whose own population could never support their traffic, and they live on what aviation law calls the sixth freedom. ICAO lists it as the right "of transporting, via the home State of the carrier, traffic moving between two other States", and calls it "so-called" because, unlike the first five freedoms, it is not written into the founding treaties.6 Business Traveller's example is a London–Dubai–Cape Town ticket on Emirates.7 Analysts at CAPA described Dubai, Doha and Abu Dhabi as "the three major sixth freedom hubs in the Middle East".8

The scale shows in the rankings. In Airports Council International's figures for 2025, Atlanta, Delta's fortress hub,5 was the world's busiest airport with 106.3 million passengers, followed by Dubai with 95.2 million and Tokyo Haneda with 91.7 million. Counting only international passengers, Dubai was first, ahead of London Heathrow and Seoul Incheon.9

BAlliances and partners→

No airline flies everywhere, so airlines sell each other's seats, and the agreements behind that come in layers of increasing intimacy. The layer matters to you mostly when something goes wrong.

  1. Interlinethe base layer
    Two airlines agree to accept each other's tickets and bags. IATA's multilateral interline agreements "spell out the basic rules airlines follow when collecting money and issuing documents for carriage on each other's services".10 This is what lets one ticket, with your bag checked through, cover flights on two carriers.
  2. Codeshareone flight, several numbers
    One airline sells seats on another's flight under its own flight number. The US Justice Department has described the range, from simply using another carrier's reservation codes up to "comprehensive integration of marketing and operations". Codeshare partners still compete; without a specific grant of antitrust immunity, competition law applies to them in full.11
  3. Alliancea branded club
    A group of airlines that link loyalty schemes, lounges and transfer handling under one name. It does not by itself set shared fares.
  4. Joint venturethe deepest tie
    Partners pool revenue and costs on a set of routes so that none of them cares whose aircraft you fly on. The US Department of Transportation calls this "metal neutrality": prices, capacity and sales are coordinated across the combined network, which the DOT says lets carriers "achieve merger-like efficiencies". It needs antitrust immunity.12

Three such joint ventures dominate flights between North America and Europe. One combines Delta, Air France, KLM and Virgin Atlantic, tentatively granted immunity in its expanded form by the DOT in August 2019.13 Another joins American Airlines with British Airways, Iberia and Finnair, with Aer Lingus approved to join in December 2020.14 The third links Lufthansa, United and Air Canada; in 2025 the partners applied to add ITA Airways.15 Each venture lines up with an alliance, which is why a transatlantic fare on one partner so often routes you through another partner's hub.

CThe connection→

Every hub has a minimum connection time: the shortest gap, airport by airport and often terminal by terminal, in which a passenger and their bag can be expected to make a transfer. MCTs are agreed by the airlines operating at each airport, with individual airlines filing exceptions, and new industry-standard times must be approved by IATA. The aviation data company OAG publishes more than 157,000 of them, which is how booking engines decide which connections they are allowed to sell.25 KLM, for example, gives its minimum at Amsterdam Schiphol as 40 minutes between Schengen flights and 50 minutes for non-Schengen flights.26 as of Oct 2026

An MCT is a promise only when the connection is on one ticket. Heathrow says airlines only sell connecting itineraries that meet its minimum, and that a passenger who misses a connection because the first flight was late should ask the airline about rebooking; for flights booked separately, the minimums do not apply and the traveller must leave their own margin.27 KLM says a passenger on a protected connection who misses it is automatically rebooked on the next available flight at no cost.26

European law adds money. Under EU Regulation 261/2004, as the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in Air France v Folkerts on 26 February 2013, a passenger on a connecting journey is owed compensation if they reach their final destination three hours or more late, even if the first flight's own delay was too short to qualify on its own.28

Self-transfer: the gap between two tickets

Booking two cheap one-way tickets through a hub can save money, and it moves all the risk to you. At Gatwick, a passenger connecting on a separate ticket has to pass through immigration, collect their bags from reclaim if they were not checked through, and check in again. Even passengers who already hold a boarding pass for the next flight are told to allow at least 60 minutes within a terminal and 90 minutes between terminals, or "you are at serious risk of missing your onward connection"; those with bags to reclaim are pointed to their airline's own minimum.29 Gatwick once tried to sell a safety net: GatwickConnects, launched with the booking firm Dohop in September 2015, let travellers buy separate airlines' flights in one transaction with a guarantee if things went wrong.30 It no longer appears on Gatwick's website. Frankfurt makes the same point from the other side: on one ticket, bags are in most cases checked through automatically; on separately booked connections, you reclaim and check them in again.31

Rule of thumbOn one ticket, the airline owns the risk of a tight connection. On two, you do, so price in a buffer of at least an hour, the chance of losing the second fare, and any bag fee charged twice.

Two ticket tricks, one of them banned

An open-jaw ticket flies you into one city and home from another, and IATA's fare rules define it as a normal fare type.85 It often lets a traveller drop a backtracking leg and replace it with a train. Hidden-city ticketing, booking through a hub you actually want to stop at because the longer ticket is cheaper, is a different matter: American Airlines' conditions of carriage name it as a prohibited practice,86 and Lufthansa's allow it to recalculate the fare when legs are skipped and collect the difference.87 Lufthansa took a passenger to court in Germany over a skipped leg; a Berlin court dismissed the claim in 2018, and Lufthansa withdrew its appeal in 2019.88,89 Skipping a leg also cancels the rest of the itinerary on most airlines, and a checked bag goes to the ticketed destination.

Preclearance: crossing the border before you leave

The US has found one way round its own rule. At Preclearance airports, CBP officers inspect passengers before departure, so they land in the US as domestic travellers and skip the arrival queue. CBP lists 15 locations in six countries: Dublin and Shannon in Ireland; Aruba; Bermuda; Nassau in the Bahamas; Abu Dhabi; and nine Canadian airports (Calgary, Edmonton, Halifax, Montreal, Ottawa, Toronto, Vancouver, Victoria and Winnipeg).38 as of Oct 2026 O'Hare notes that precleared passengers can change terminals there without leaving security and being screened again.36 For a traveller connecting onward inside the US, that can be the difference between a comfortable transfer and a missed one.

ERail meets air→

On short feeder routes, the European airlines have been replacing aircraft with trains that carry flight numbers. At Frankfurt, where the airport has its own long-distance station, Lufthansa Express Rail (the service known until 2015 as AIRail39) connected 28 cities by 2024, with more than 240 daily ICE trains carrying a Lufthansa flight number. In November 2024 Lufthansa and Deutsche Bahn said 432,000 travellers had used it since the start of the year, a record, with more than 500,000 bookings confirmed by the year's end.40 The ticket covers train and flight together, with a reserved seat and a guaranteed connection.41

Austrian Airlines runs the same idea from Vienna Airport as AIRail, on ÖBB Railjets that show an Austrian Airlines flight number on the station screens. Austrian lists Linz, Salzburg, Graz, Klagenfurt and Innsbruck; ÖBB's own page lists Linz, Salzburg and Graz.42,43 In France, Air France and SNCF sell Train + Air journeys through Paris-Charles de Gaulle on one booking, with a free seat on the next train or flight if either is delayed or cancelled. In September 2026 they extended it to every OUIGO train serving the airport and reported that Train + Air traffic had risen 43% year on year over the previous six months.44

France's 2h30 rule

France went further and banned some flights outright. Article 145 of the Climate and Resilience Law of 22 August 2021 prohibits scheduled domestic flights where a direct train, running several times a day, makes the trip in under two and a half hours.45 The European Commission approved the measure in December 2022, after France dropped proposed exemptions, including one for routes carried mainly by connecting passengers, which the Commission said risked discriminating between carriers.46 The implementing decree of 22 May 2023 covered three routes, from Paris-Orly to Bordeaux, Nantes and Lyon, whose flights had in practice already stopped in 2021. Routes from Charles de Gaulle survived, because the train from that airport takes too long or the timetable does not allow a full day at the destination.45,47

London alone has six major international airports: Heathrow, Gatwick, Stansted, Luton, City and Southend.57 Most big cities have at least a second option, and low-cost airlines have made a business of airports whose names promise more than their location delivers. Bergamo is 45 km from Milan, by its airport's own figure;58 Beauvais about 80 km north of Paris, by the Paris tourist office;59 Girona 85 km from Barcelona, by the operator Aena;60 Hahn around 100 km from Frankfurt Airport;61 Skavsta 100 km south of Stockholm.62

The distance is only half of it. Beauvais's official shuttle to Porte Maillot, on the western edge of Paris, takes from 1h15, traffic permitting, and costs €17.90 one way bought online;63 the tourist office says to allow about 1h30.59 The direct bus from Girona airport to Barcelona Nord takes 75 minutes and costs €35 return.64 Shuttles from these airports are usually timed to flights, so a delayed arrival can mean waiting for the next one.

The airport in two countries

The opposite case is EuroAirport Basel-Mulhouse-Freiburg, which stands on French soil under a Franco-Swiss convention signed in Bern on 4 July 1949 and calls itself the world's only binational airport.65,66 It has a Swiss sector, where prices are in Swiss francs, and a French sector priced in euros.65 From the Swiss side, bus 50 runs every seven to ten minutes to Basel SBB station in about 20 minutes.67 One building serves three countries' worth of catchment, so it is worth checking for anyone heading to north-west Switzerland, Alsace or southern Baden.

Airside or landside?

Sleeping at the airport has one question that matters more than price: is the hotel inside the transit area? Changi's transit hotels in Terminals 1, 2 and 3 are airside, and Changi warns guests not to clear immigration if they want to stay in them. The YOTELAIR in Changi's Jewel complex is in the public area, so its guests must pass through immigration.74 The Aerotel at Heathrow is in the Terminal 3 arrivals hall, landside.75 A landside hotel means a passport check, perhaps a visa, and security again in the morning.

2 · London to New York, or onward

Nonstop vs Preclearance via Dublin

Two ways to the same city. A nonstop from Heathrow is shorter in the air and simpler on paper. Going via Dublin adds a flight, but the US border moves to Ireland.

  • HeathrowArrive three hours before a long-haul flight, Heathrow advises.78 On landing in New York you join the CBP inspection queue; if you are flying on within the US, you collect your bag, recheck it and clear security again before the next flight.36
  • DublinDublin Airport also advises three hours for long-haul flights. US flights leave from Terminal 2, where Preclearance runs all US immigration, customs and agriculture checks before departure, and you "arrive in the U.S. as a domestic passenger".82,83
  • Either wayVisa Waiver travellers need an approved ESTA; Dublin advises applying at least 72 hours ahead.82

Verdict: for New York alone, the nonstop usually wins on total time. The Dublin routing earns its extra flight when the trip continues: you land as a domestic passenger, so a connection to a second US city loses the border queue and the bag recheck that would otherwise sit inside it. At O'Hare, precleared passengers can change terminals without being screened again.36 The ground at the London end counts too, because the Dublin leg can start from whichever London airport is easiest to reach.

5 · Fourteen hours in Istanbul

A long connection, used well

Suppose a Turkish Airlines itinerary, on one ticket in Economy, lands at Istanbul at 06:00 and connects at 20:00, 14 hours later.

  • HotelTiming aside, the layover clears the 12-hour Economy threshold for Turkish Airlines' transfer hotel, but only if no shorter connection exists on that route, "even if that flight is fully booked". Apply at the Hotel Desk on the arrivals level.71
  • TourFourteen daytime hours also fit Touristanbul's 6–24-hour window and the daytime tour slots, provided the tour ends at least 1h25 before the next flight. You can have the tour or the hotel in one layover, not both.69,70
  • BorderBoth mean leaving the airport, so you need whatever entry permission your passport requires for Türkiye; the airline says visas are the passenger's responsibility.70

The same daytime gap at Changi falls inside the Free Singapore Tour's 5.5-to-24-hour window, though the tour also needs your flights to fit its slots; and an airside transit hotel offers a bed without entering Singapore at all.68,74

Verdict: a long connection on a single ticket with an airline that runs these schemes can cost less than a shorter one bought on two tickets with a night in a hotel you pay for. Before booking a fare for its layover, check the rules on the day; they are the airline's to change.
