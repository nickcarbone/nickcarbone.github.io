Ten Ideas That Change How You See a Room

Ten ideas from philosophy, oldest first, each set loose on something ordinary in your day, with where it came from, the best argument against it and a question to carry until bedtime.

The chair in the corner of the bedroom, the one that has slowly disappeared under jumpers, is a puzzle first put about 2,400 years ago. The flight delay on the departures board is the subject of a handbook a pupil distilled, in the second century, from the teaching of a former slave. The meeting you sat through yesterday is a case study in a book published two years after its author died.

Philosophy has a reputation for being about nothing in particular. Its most durable ideas are mostly about something very particular, made small enough to hold up against a heap of grain, a ship’s timbers, a promise of five dollars. Each of the ten below starts from its original text, gets applied to one ordinary thing, then has to face the strongest case against it. Each ends with a question, worded so you can carry it around for a day and see what it catches.

They run oldest first. Quotations from older texts are from public-domain translations; the twentieth-century books are paraphrased, with a few words quoted where the wording itself matters.

1

The chair under the clothes

Eubulides, 4th century BC

The sorites paradox

The Megarian philosopher Eubulides is usually credited with the first version of the puzzle1. Its name comes from the Greek soros, a heap1. One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding a single grain to something that is not a heap never seems to turn it into one. Run that step enough times and you arrive at the conclusion that no amount of sand is a heap, which is plainly false. The same argument works for baldness, starting from a man with one hair1, and for any word whose edges are fuzzy: tall, old, rich, late, cluttered.

Applied to the chair

Monday, one jumper goes over the back of the bedroom chair. Nobody could call that a mess. Tuesday, a shirt. Each evening adds a single item, and each evening it is reasonable to say that one more thing cannot be the thing that made it a mess. By the second Sunday you cannot see the chair, and the argument with whoever you share the room with goes nowhere, because each of you is right about a different step.

The paradox explains why that argument has no winner. “Messy” has no boundary in the word itself. Households that stop arguing about it usually do so by inventing a boundary the word never had: nothing stays on the chair overnight, the chair holds three things. The rule is arbitrary, and that is its virtue. Law does the same thing when it turns “old enough” into a birthday.

The strongest objection

The paradox assumes the word really has no sharp edge. A school of thought called epistemicism, defended by Timothy Williamson among others, says it does: vague terms have sharp boundaries whose locations are hidden from us1. On that view there is an exact jumper at which the chair becomes messy, and the trouble is only that nobody can know which one. The paradox then dissolves, because one of its steps is false: there is a single grain that makes a heap.

It solves the logic at a price. Even its defenders grant that epistemicism is intuitively implausible, and it seems to multiply mysteries1. But it is a serious position, and it has a practical sting: if the line is real, then “one more won’t matter” is sometimes simply wrong.

Carry this today

Which argument I have today is really about where to draw a line that nobody can find?

2

The renovated house

Plutarch, about AD 100; Hobbes, 1655

The ship of Theseus

Plutarch’s Life of Theseus records that the ship in which the hero came back from Crete, a thirty-oared galley, was kept by the Athenians for centuries2. In Bernadotte Perrin’s translation:

“They took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places, so that this ship became a standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth, some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel.”

Plutarch, Theseus 232

Thomas Hobbes, in De Corpore (1655; English translation 1656), made it harder. Suppose someone had kept the old planks as they were taken out and put them back together in the same order. Then, he wrote, “there would have been two Ships Numerically the same, which is absurd”3.

Applied to the house

Take a terraced house bought twenty years ago. Since then: a new roof, new wiring, the back wall knocked through, the sash windows replaced, the floorboards lifted and relaid, the kitchen gone twice. Not much of the material the first owner touched is still there. It is still called the same house by the bank, the council, the postman and you.

The puzzle asks what “the same” is tracking. The address and the deeds are continuous. The shape from the street is mostly continuous. The material has largely changed. Which of those you think matters tells you something about what you value in a home, and it decides practical questions too: whether a renovation has “ruined” a period house, or whether the old fireplace you sold to a salvage yard took some of the house with it.

The strongest objection

Hobbes’s rebuilt ship is the objection. If gradual replacement keeps a thing the same, and keeping the same parts in the same arrangement also keeps it the same, then you end up with two ships that are each the original, and they cannot both be34. One of the two principles has to go. In an earlier critique of Thomas White’s De Mundo, Hobbes answered differently for different kinds of thing: a ship rebuilt plank by plank would be “another body”, while a man stays “always the same man” through the steady flux of his matter4.

So continuity is not a general answer. Imagine the salvage yard sells your original windows, doors, fireplace and floorboards to one buyer who reassembles them in the same layout two streets away. Many people would feel that the old house has some claim to be over there.

Carry this today

What would have to be replaced before this stopped being the same thing to me?

3

The delayed flight

Epictetus, early 2nd century AD

What is up to us

Epictetus was born in the 50s AD at Hierapolis, in what is now Turkey, spent part of his life as the slave of Epaphroditus, an administrator under Nero, and later taught as a free man at Nicopolis in Greece, where he died around 1356. His teaching survives because the historian Arrian wrote it down as the Discourses, of which the Handbook, or Enchiridion, is a short abridgement6. It opens with a sorting exercise. In George Long’s translation:

“Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion, movement towards a thing, desire, aversion… not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices…”

Epictetus, Enchiridion 15

A few chapters later comes the line most people know: “Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things”5. For Epictetus the faculty that judges and chooses, which he called prohairesis and the Stanford Encyclopedia renders as volition, is the real self, and it is by nature unimpeded6.

Applied to the departures board

The flight is delayed three hours. The weather, the late incoming aircraft and the crew’s legal hours sit firmly in the second column. In the first column: whether you treat this as a catastrophe, how you speak to the person at the gate, and what you do with three hours. The exercise sounds like a platitude until you try writing the two lists down. Most of the anger at a gate is aimed at the second column, by people who have quietly left the first one empty.

The strongest objection

The line is in the wrong place. Epictetus puts property and reputation outside our power, yet a delayed passenger can rebook, claim compensation, complain, or choose a different airline next time. Most of life is partly in our control, and a strict two-column habit can teach resignation where a phone call would work.

There is an older objection from the other direction. Blaise Pascal thought it arrogance to believe a person could perfect their inner life by their own efforts6. The first column may be smaller than Epictetus hoped: our opinions and desires are shaped by tiredness, habit and fear that we did not choose either.

Carry this today

What am I treating as fate that is actually a phone call?

4

The noise in the night

William of Ockham, 14th century

Ockham’s razor

William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) was an English Franciscan whose name is attached to the most quoted rule in the history of explanation. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy points out that the famous wording, that entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity, appears nowhere in his texts, though the sentiment is his7. He treated it as a cautionary rule of method, and used it relentlessly: he argued that things like quantities and relations do not exist as entities over and above individual substances and their qualities7. In his fullest statement, nothing should be posited without a reason, experience or the authority of Scripture to require it7.

The classic Latin phrasing came three centuries later. The Irish Franciscan John Punch wrote non sunt multiplicanda entia sine necessitate in a commentary on Duns Scotus printed in 1639, calling it a common axiom of the scholastics, and without attributing it to Ockham8.

Applied to the hallway at 3am

A thud downstairs. Possible explanations: a burglar, the cat, a picture falling, the heating pipes ticking as they cool, something you cannot explain. The razor does not tell you which is true. It tells you where to start: with the explanation that needs the fewest extra things to exist. A burglar requires a person, a way in and a motive; the cat requires the cat you already own. Ockham’s rule is mostly a defence against the explanation you prefer because it is more interesting.

The strongest objection

Ockham’s own contemporary made it. Walter Chatton (c. 1290–1343), who sparred with Ockham for years, proposed an “anti-razor”: if the things you have posited are not enough to make a true statement true, you must posit another one97. Simplicity is a rule for where to look first. It is not evidence. A world in which the simplest explanation were always true would be a convenience, and nothing guarantees it.

At 3am that is the whole point. Sometimes there is somebody downstairs. The razor is good advice for ranking suspects and bad advice for refusing to go and look.

Carry this today

Which explanation am I adding because it is more interesting, rather than because the evidence asks for it?

5

The office at twenty to seven

David Hume, 1739–40

The is–ought gap

Near the start of Book 3 of A Treatise of Human Nature, David Hume added an observation which, he wrote, may perhaps be found of some importance. In every system of morality he had read, he said, the author reasons for a while in the ordinary way, describing God or human affairs, and then, suddenly, “instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not”10. The move is never explained. Hume thought that paying attention to it would “subvert all the vulgar systems of morality”10. The lesson generally drawn from the passage is that you cannot get a conclusion about what should be done purely from facts about what is.

Applied to the office

Everyone on the team is still at their desk at twenty to seven. That is a fact. “So I should stay until seven” does not follow from it. To get there you need something else: that promotions go to people who stay late (another fact) plus that you want a promotion; or that matching your colleagues is a duty; or that leaving first is a betrayal. Each of those can be argued about, which is the point. Office culture runs on unstated premises, and Hume’s gap is a way of finding them. Ask what the missing “ought” is, and say it out loud. Sometimes it survives being said. Often it does not.

The strongest objection

In 1964 John Searle published “How to Derive ‘Ought’ from ‘Is’” in the Philosophical Review11. His chain runs in five steps. Jones uttered the words “I hereby promise to pay you, Smith, five dollars.” So Jones promised to pay Smith five dollars. So Jones placed himself under an obligation to pay Smith five dollars. So Jones is under an obligation to pay Smith five dollars. So Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars11. Searle’s point was that some facts are institutional facts, such as promises, contracts and rules of games, and they carry obligations inside them.

The argument has been fought over ever since, but it changes how the office looks. If your contract says the working day ends at half past five, that is an institutional fact with an “ought” built in, and it points towards the door.

Carry this today

What unstated “should” is hiding inside today’s “this is just how it’s done”?

6

The trolley in the car park

Immanuel Kant, 1785

The universal law test

Kant’s Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) sets out the categorical imperative13. Its first formula, in the translation the Stanford Encyclopedia uses, says: “act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law”12. The test has steps. State the rule you are actually acting on. Imagine it as a law everyone follows. Ask whether that world is even conceivable, and then whether you could rationally want to live in it12. Kant’s standard example is the lying promise: a world where everyone makes promises they don’t intend to keep is a world where promises mean nothing, so the lie defeats itself12.

Applied to the supermarket car park

It is raining, the trolley bay is forty metres away, and you are tempted to leave the trolley beside the car. The rule you are acting on is roughly: when it’s raining, leave the trolley wherever is convenient. Make that a law. On every wet day the car park fills with loose trolleys rolling into doors, and there are none by the entrance, so you walk to the far end in the rain to fetch one. The test does not depend on your single trolley doing damage. It asks whether your reason would still work if everyone had it, and here it would not.

The strongest objection

Benjamin Constant pushed the test to breaking point. If lying is always wrong, then when a murderer comes to your door asking where your friend is hiding, you may not lie to him13. Kant replied in 1797, in the essay “On a Supposed Right to Tell Lies from Benevolent Motives”, and accepted the conclusion: “a lie always harms another”, and principles of right must hold without exceptions1413.

For most readers, that answer is the objection. A test that cannot let you protect your friend has gone wrong somewhere, and Kant’s defenders have spent two centuries reworking the maxim (what exactly are you universalising?) to get a better result. The trolley is safe. The hard cases are where the test is least sure of itself.

Carry this today

If everyone did exactly what I’m about to do, would I still be able to do it?

7

The music through the wall

John Stuart Mill, 1859

The harm principle

John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty was published in London in 18591516. Its central claim is a single principle:

“That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others.”

Mill, On Liberty, chapter 115

A person’s own good, Mill went on, is not a sufficient warrant. Over himself, his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign. The principle applies to society’s disapproval as much as to the law.

Applied to the party wall

It is one in the morning and the neighbours are playing music you can feel through the plaster. Mill’s principle splits the complaint in two. The volume and the hour are your business, because they stop you sleeping, and lost sleep is a harm. The music itself, which is terrible, is not your business at all. The useful thing about the principle is that it makes you notice how often those two complaints arrive together, with the second one doing most of the shouting.

The strongest objection

In 1959 the judge Patrick Devlin (later Lord Devlin) gave the Maccabaean Lecture in reply to the Wolfenden Report, which had recommended that consensual homosexual behaviour in private should no longer be a crime17. Devlin argued that a society is held together by a shared morality and may use the law to protect it: “No society can do without intolerance, indignation and disgust; they are the forces behind the moral law”17. The legal philosopher H.L.A. Hart answered him, drawing on Mill, that there must remain a realm of private morality that is not the law’s business17.

Devlin’s example has aged badly. The harder part of the objection has not: “harm” is elastic. Whoever decides what counts as harm controls the principle, and people who are offended by something usually describe it as harm. Mill’s line holds only as well as the honesty of the person drawing it.

Carry this today

Is what is bothering me a harm, or only something I dislike?

8

The job you say you are

Jean-Paul Sartre, 1943

Bad faith

In Being and Nothingness, published by Gallimard in 1943 and first translated into English by Hazel Barnes in 195618, Jean-Paul Sartre describes a waiter in a café. His movements are a little too precise, a little too rapid; he bends forward a little too eagerly. Sartre’s diagnosis is that he is playing at being a waiter in a café1819. The performance is a way of treating himself as a thing, a waiter in the way an inkwell is an inkwell, and so of escaping the fact that at every moment he could do otherwise. Sartre called this kind of self-deception mauvaise foi, bad faith: a denial of one’s own freedom, often by identifying completely with a role1819.

Applied to the counter

Listen for sentences that begin “I’m just”. I’m just the receptionist, I can’t authorise that. I’m just not a morning person. I’m just the kind of person who says what they think. Each turns a choice into a property, something you are rather than something you keep doing. Sometimes the role really does limit you: the receptionist may genuinely have no authority. Sartre’s question is whether you have checked, or whether the role is a comfortable place to stand while declining to decide.

The strongest objection

Sartre’s freedom is too big. The historian of philosophy Frederick Copleston called the view that all our actions result from free choice “highly implausible”18. A.J. Ayer was blunter, dismissing much of the book as an exercise in misusing the verb “to be”18. Maurice Merleau-Ponty disputed his account of freedom from Phenomenology of Perception (1945) until his last manuscripts20.

The practical form of the objection is about fairness. Habits, bodies, debts and other people’s expectations are not costumes we choose to wear. Telling someone with few options that their role is bad faith can be a cruelty dressed as philosophy. The waiter may be eager because he needs the tips.

Carry this today

Which sentence beginning “I’m just…” did I use today to close a door I could have opened?

9

The meeting

Ludwig Wittgenstein, 1953

Language-games

Ludwig Wittgenstein died in 1951 before his Philosophical Investigations was ready; it was published in 1953, in Elizabeth Anscombe’s translation21. It opens with a builder and an assistant who share a language of four words, “block”, “pillar”, “slab” and “beam”: the builder calls one out and the assistant brings it22. Wittgenstein calls such a whole, the language together with the actions it is woven into, a language-game22. In section 23 he lists examples to show how many there are: giving orders and obeying them, guessing riddles, cursing, greeting, praying22. The term was meant to bring out that speaking a language is part of an activity21, and the book’s best-known line follows from it: the meaning of a word is its use in the language21.

Applied to the meeting

“Happy to discuss” usually means no. “Just to push back slightly” announces strong disagreement. “Let’s take this offline” ends a conversation while sounding like it extends one. None of these makes sense from the dictionary, and all of them work perfectly well among people who know the game. The more useful lesson is about confusion. Many bad meetings are two games played at once: one person brainstorming, where any idea is a fair move, and another deciding, where every idea spoken counts as a commitment. Each thinks the other is playing badly.

The strongest objection

Bertrand Russell wrote that he had not found in the Investigations anything that seemed to him interesting21, and Ernest Gellner’s book Words and Things was fiercely critical of Wittgenstein and those who followed him21. Their complaint, roughly, was that describing how words are used turns philosophy into a catalogue and walks away from the question of whether what is said is true.

In the meeting, that objection has teeth. A team can be fluent in all its games, every “happy to discuss” perfectly understood, and still be wrong about the numbers. Knowing which game is being played tells you how to read a remark. It does not tell you whether the remark is right.

Carry this today

What game did each person in that meeting think they were playing?

10

The city budget

John Rawls, 1971

The veil of ignorance

John Rawls’s A Theory of Justice (1971) asks what principles people would choose for their society if they chose from behind a veil of ignorance, knowing no particular facts about themselves: not their talents, class, wealth or values2423. They know general facts about how societies work, but not which place in one they will occupy23. Rawls argued they would choose two principles: equal basic liberties first; then offices and positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity, with economic inequalities arranged to benefit the least advantaged members of society as much as possible, the part he called the difference principle2324.

Applied to the council budget

A town has money for one of four things: resurfacing the main roads, keeping the libraries open on Sundays, restoring an evening bus route, or more parking in the centre. Arguments about budgets are usually arguments between people who know exactly who they are: the commuter with a car, the pensioner who relies on the bus, the parent whose child does homework at the library. Put the veil on and you have to imagine that you might turn out to be any of them, including the one with the fewest alternatives. The question stops being which option you would use and becomes which option you could defend to whoever ends up worst off.

The strongest objection

The economist John Harsanyi argued in 1975 that rational people behind the veil would not focus on the worst-off position at all. Facing uncertainty about who they will be, they would choose to maximise average welfare, which is the principle of utility23. Rawls’s rule, in that view, is a model of extreme caution: it treats the worst outcome as if it were certain.

Applied to the budget, Harsanyi’s chooser might fund the roads, because almost everyone uses them, and accept that the few who depend on the evening bus lose out. Both men agreed on the veil. They disagreed about how a sensible person behaves behind it, and that disagreement is still unresolved.

Carry this today

Would I sign off on this if I didn’t know which seat I’d be sitting in?
