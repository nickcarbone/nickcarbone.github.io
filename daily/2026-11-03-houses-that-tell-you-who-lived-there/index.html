Houses That Tell You Who Lived There

Fifteen landmark European houses built for rich clients between 1894 and 1959, and what each plan, material and doorway reveals about how its owners wanted to live.

A house built for a rich client is a brief turned into rooms. Somebody had to decide where the servants slept, how a business caller was kept away from the children, whether the dining room faced the street or the garden, and whether the neighbours would ever see inside. Those decisions survive in the plan long after the owners have gone, and they say more about the people than the façade does.

This guide goes inside 15 European houses, from a Brussels town house begun in 1894 to an art dealer’s villa outside Paris finished in 1959. Every one is now a museum, a heritage-listed building or exhaustively documented. For each there is the commission, the plan, the materials, what the design signalled in its day, and a short note on what the house would tell a novelist about a character who lived there. The novelist’s notes are the desk’s own interpretation; everything else is sourced.

IIThe plates

Pl. 01

Hôtel Solvay

Brussels, Belgium · 1894–1903

Client
Armand and Fanny Solvay
Architect
Victor Horta
Built
1894–1903
Style
Art Nouveau
Visiting
Open to visit Museum since 2021, privately owned. Unguided individual visits of about 40 minutes with tickets online, and separate guided tours (as of November 2026).1
Privacy 2/5Display 5/5

A street front of reception rooms that open into one another, with family and servants stacked away above and below.

The commission

Armand Solvay was the eldest son of Ernest Solvay,2 whose ammonia-soda process for making soda ash made the family firm a worldwide industrial business.3 Armand took charge of the family firm in May 1894 and married Fanny Hunter on 16 June; four days later he walked the empty plot on the avenue Louise with Victor Horta.2 Horta had ample means, and remembered Armand as a man who prized simplicity.2 The permit came in 1895, the family were living there by the end of 1898, and the last marble-worker’s bill is dated December 1903.24

The plan

Horta kept the conventional Brussels town house, with its carriage entrance and a coach house at the back, and remade everything inside it. The kitchen sits on the ground floor rather than in the basement, which was unusual. The bel étage carries the reception rooms across the street front, the couple’s office, boudoir, bedroom, dressing room and bathroom are on the second floor, and the children and their governess are on the third. Servants lived in the basement and attics and used their own stair; household registers for 1900–1910 record up to 12 people.2 The reception rooms are divided by glass partitions that can be opened to make one space.4

Materials

Twelve species of wood and 23 marbles went into the interiors and furniture. Each room was given its own timber: oak for Armand’s office, burr maple for the boudoir, mahogany for the eldest child and pitch pine for the youngest. A metal frame carries the building, light comes down two wells over the staircases, and there were 166 electric light fittings in the reception rooms alone, along with central heating, a ventilation system drawing fresh air through grilles in the façade, and hot running water.2 UNESCO calls it the most ambitious and spectacular of Horta’s Art Nouveau houses.5

What it said

This was industrial money from modern chemistry, and it chose the newest style and the newest services. The electric light and heating were as much a statement as the marble.

For the novelist

Your character married into a fortune and moved into a house where even the children’s rooms were ranked by wood. They live upstairs from their own parties and a floor above a dozen people they rarely see. They like modern comforts and want the guests to notice them.

Disputed

Sources give 1957 or 1958 for the sale to the couture house that saved it; that family still owns it.46 The staircase glass destroyed in 1944 was restored in 1982, but the light well over the stair, closed after 1958, has not been reopened.2

Pl. 02

The Hill House

Helensburgh, Scotland · 1902–1904

Client
Walter Blackie and family
Architect
Charles Rennie Mackintosh, with Margaret Macdonald Mackintosh
Built
1902–1904
Style
Glasgow Style
Visiting
Open to visit National Trust for Scotland; daily, early January to late December, no booking needed. The house stands inside a protective mesh “Box” due to stay until 2028 (as of November 2026).78
Privacy 4/5Display 2/5

A plain grey exterior and a business library by the front door keep visitors away from a private, highly decorated family interior.

The commission

Walter Blackie ran the Glasgow publishing house Blackie & Son and wanted to live near the city but out of it. The designer Talwin Morris recommended Mackintosh,8 who was commissioned in spring 1902; the Blackies moved in in March 1904.91011 Before drawing anything, Mackintosh spent time with the family to see how they and their staff used a house. Blackie later recalled that the elevations came only after the inside had been settled.98

The plan

An L-shaped house of two storeys and an attic. The library sits next to the front door so Blackie could see business callers there without bringing them into the family’s rooms. The drawing room faces south, the dining room is panelled, and upstairs the principal “White Bedroom” is L-shaped, with the bed set under a lower barrel vault; part of it served as Mrs Blackie’s day room, and she often received guests there.101211 The family’s daughter Ruth remembered two housemaids, a cook and a table maid.13

Materials

Blackie asked for grey roughcast and slate rather than red tile, which he thought wrong under a west of Scotland sky.13 Inside are dark oak shelves in the library, Margaret Macdonald’s light fittings and embroidered panels, and her gesso panel The Sleeping Princess (1908) over the drawing-room fireplace.12 The Portland cement render let water in from the start, and the Trust removed it in 2026 so the walls could dry.1314

What it said

A publisher with modern taste and conservative manners. The outside is sober enough for Helensburgh, and the colour and invention are inside, where only family and invited guests saw them.

For the novelist

Your character keeps work at the front door and pleasure upstairs. They are proud of the books on their shelves because they published many of them. They commission something daring and then ask it to look respectable from the street.

Disputed

Published costs differ: about £5,000 in some accounts, just over £9,500 including furnishings and garden according to a Trust historian.813

Pl. 03

Casa Batlló

Barcelona, Spain · 1904–1906

Client
Josep Batlló i Casanovas
Architect
Antoni Gaudí
Built
1904–1906
Style
Modernisme
Visiting
Open to visit Privately owned museum; open every day, with tickets booked online (as of November 2026).15
Privacy 2/5Display 5/5

The most theatrical street front in the guide; the family lived behind it on the main floor with rent-paying tenants above.

The commission

Josep Batlló was a textile industrialist with mills in Barcelona. In 1903 he bought an existing 1877 apartment building on the Passeig de Gràcia, the avenue where the city’s leading families had settled, and in 1904 hired Gaudí, who talked him out of demolishing it.16 The rebuilding ran from 1904 to 1906.17

The plan

This is an apartment building with the owner on top of the social order but not of the building. The Batllós took the noble floor, over 700 square metres, with a study, a salon whose great window looks onto the avenue, and a dining room opening onto a garden courtyard at the back.1819 The floors above were let as flats.20 Residents came in through a shared lobby; the family’s own lobby lay beyond a grille, up a carved wooden stair.18

Materials

Montjuïc sandstone on the lower front,19 broken ceramic and glass mosaic (trencadís) above, and an iron-and-glass roof over the light well.21 The light well is lined with blue tiles that darken towards the top and pale towards the bottom, with windows that grow larger lower down, so that every floor gets similar light.1822

What it said

On an avenue where rich families competed through their façades, Batlló bought the loudest one available. The rents upstairs paid towards it.

For the novelist

Your character wants everyone on the avenue to know their address and nobody on it to see their dinner. They are a landlord as well as a host, and their tenants pass through a lobby that is not quite theirs.

Disputed

The roof is popularly read as St George’s dragon, a reading the museum promotes; no statement by Gaudí confirming it is known.2219 Sources differ on when the present owners bought the building, 1990 or 1993.2019

Pl. 04

Palais Stoclet

Brussels, Belgium · 1905–1911

Client
Adolphe and Suzanne Stoclet
Architect
Josef Hoffmann and the Wiener Werkstätte
Built
1905–1911
Style
Vienna Secession
Visiting
Exterior only Privately owned and closed to the public; seen only from the avenue. In June 2026 Belgium’s Constitutional Court annulled a Brussels law that could have required open days.2324
Privacy 5/5Display 4/5

Built to show a collection to an invited circle; it has stayed shut to everyone else for more than a century.

The commission

Adolphe Stoclet trained as a railway engineer, came from a family of bankers and became a director of the Société Générale de Belgique.2526 He and his wife Suzanne, daughter of the art dealer Arthur Stevens, met Hoffmann while living in Vienna.2526 UNESCO dates the commission to 1905 and completion of house and garden to 1911, and says the clients imposed no aesthetic or financial limits.27

The plan

It was conceived as a total work of art: building, garden, furniture and objects designed as one.2728 Published descriptions follow a ceremonial route from the street through marble vestibules to a hall two storeys high, a music room with a gallery, and a dining room lined with Gustav Klimt’s mosaic frieze, where the lower walls step out to serve as sideboards for silver.29 Much less is published about the private rooms upstairs.

Materials

The house is clad in marble plaques, which one account describes as white Norwegian marble.3029 Klimt’s frieze is made of marble, ceramic, gilded tiles, enamel, pearls and other semi-precious stones;31 the MAK in Vienna holds his working cartoons.32

What it said

Collectors’ wealth, presented as connoisseurship. The house was a setting for art and for a guest list, and access to it was itself the privilege.

For the novelist

Your character owns things few people will ever be allowed to see, and decides who those people are. Dinner is served under a Klimt. The servants’ part of the house has left almost no trace in the record.

Disputed

Sources disagree on whether the commission came in 1904 or 1905.2527

Pl. 05

Villa Noailles

Hyères, France · 1923–c.1928

Client
Charles and Marie-Laure de Noailles
Architect
Robert Mallet-Stevens
Built
1923–c.1928
Style
Early Modernism
Visiting
Open to visit Owned by the public authorities since 1973 and run as a contemporary art centre; open to the public (as of November 2026).33
Privacy 2/5Display 5/5

A winter house that kept growing to fit a sporting, partying, film-making circle of artists.

The commission

Charles de Noailles was an aristocrat and patron; Marie-Laure, born Bischoffsheim, came from a banking family and became one of the most adventurous art patrons in Paris.34 They married in 1923, and Charles’s mother gave them land on a hill above Hyères. They first asked Mallet-Stevens for a small, simple winter house; the contract dates from 192335 and the first house was finished in November 1925.36

The plan

The house then grew in stages into dozens of rooms: a Salon Rosé lit from above, a covered swimming pool, a gymnasium and a squash court, and a bedroom in an open-air loggia with the bed hung from the ceiling.3637 Gabriel Guévrékian designed a triangular Cubist garden.36

Materials

It looks like reinforced concrete but is mostly brick and stone masonry, with concrete used for beams and cantilevers.3836 Fourteen identical clocks by Francis Jourdain hang through the house, and Theo van Doesburg painted a tiny room for arranging flowers.36

What it said

This was a fortune spent on the avant-garde, and on health and sport. The guests were artists, and Man Ray shot a film there in 1929 with the Noailles’ friends as the cast.37 A mast flew the owners’ colours when they were in residence.33

For the novelist

Your character treats a house as a party that never quite ends. There is a room for flowers, a court for squash and a clock in every room. They sleep outdoors in December because it is good for them.

Disputed

Published counts of the finished house vary, from “something like fifty rooms” in one guest’s memory to about sixty.3539 Le Corbusier is reported to have declined the job before Mallet-Stevens took it.37

Pl. 06

Rietveld Schröder House

Utrecht, Netherlands · 1924

Client
Truus Schröder-Schräder
Architect
Gerrit Rietveld with Truus Schröder-Schräder
Built
1924
Style
De Stijl
Visiting
Open to visit Centraal Museum; small timed groups, booking required (as of November 2026).40
Privacy 3/5Display 3/5

Huge uncurtained windows on a quiet street, but an upper floor its owner could close into private rooms at will.

The commission

Truus Schröder grew up in a wealthy Catholic family and married a lawyer, who died in 1923 and left her with three children.41 Rietveld had already remodelled a room for her in the family’s conventional house. In 1924 they designed a new one together, and she moved in in January 1925.4142

The plan

The ground floor is fairly conventional and held the joint architects’ office. The upper floor was called an attic on the plans to satisfy the authorities; it is essentially one space, which sliding and pivoting panels turn into bedrooms and a living room at night.4342 A corner window opens so that the corner of the room disappears. A speaking tube let Schröder talk to callers at the door without going downstairs.44

Materials

It looks like concrete slabs but is built of brick, steel and some concrete, rendered; the colour scheme of white, greys, black and primary colours is repainted in the original colours.4546

What it said

This status was cultural rather than material. A widow of means turned down the bourgeois villa for a house her son called a slap in the face to Dutch respectability.42

For the novelist

Your character rearranges the walls every evening. She runs a business from the ground floor and talks to visitors through a tube. Rietveld became her partner, and he moved in after his wife died.4144

Disputed

The house is often credited to Rietveld alone; the museum stresses that Schröder co-designed it.41

Pl. 07

Haus Wittgenstein

Vienna, Austria · 1926–1928

Client
Margarethe Stonborough-Wittgenstein
Architect
Paul Engelmann and Ludwig Wittgenstein
Built
1926–1928
Style
Austere modernism
Visiting
Limited access Bulgarian Cultural Institute since 1975; interior seen on guided tours only (as of November 2026).4748
Privacy 4/5Display 3/5

Heavy metal shutters that could seal the ground floor; inside, a sequence of rooms for receiving.

The commission

Margarethe Stonborough-Wittgenstein was the daughter of Karl Wittgenstein, whose iron and steel empire made the family enormously wealthy,49 and the subject of Klimt’s 1905 portrait.50 In November 1925 she commissioned Paul Engelmann, a pupil of Loos. Her brother Ludwig, the philosopher, joined in 1926 and progressively took the design over. The house was built in 1926–28.515253

The plan

Three intersecting blocks.51 A few steps lead up to a central hall that opens directly to the dining room, music room, living room, library and terrace.53 Wittgenstein enforced strict symmetry, sometimes thickening walls to get it, and designed the doors, radiators, handles and plumbing himself.51

Materials

No skirtings or thresholds,51 bare bulbs for light,53 and tall glazed doors in slim metal frames.54 Each ground-floor window has a metal shutter of about 150 kilograms that can be lowered into the floor by a pulley he designed. Wittgenstein reportedly spent a year on the handles and another on the radiators, and had a ceiling raised by 30 millimetres near the end.5556

What it said

Old wealth that wanted to look severe. Margarethe softened it with her own furnishings and objets d’art.52

For the novelist

Your character’s brother designed her house and would not let her hang curtains.53 Their sister Hermine admired it but wrote that she could not have lived in it herself: it was a house for the gods.55

Disputed

How much of the house is Engelmann’s and how much Wittgenstein’s is argued over; Engelmann himself credited the final design to Wittgenstein.5552

Pl. 08

Villa Tugendhat

Brno, Czech Republic · 1928–1930

Client
Fritz and Grete Tugendhat
Architect
Ludwig Mies van der Rohe with Lilly Reich
Built
1928–1930
Style
International Style
Visiting
Open to visit Owned and administered by the City of Brno;57 the interior is seen by guided tour only, often booked months ahead (as of November 2026).58
Privacy 4/5Display 4/5

A closed single storey to the street; a wall of glass to the garden, invisible from outside.

The commission

Grete Löw-Beer and Fritz Tugendhat both came from wealthy Jewish industrial families in Brno. They married in 192859 and knew Mies’s buildings from Germany.60 The plot on the hillside was a wedding present from her father, according to the city’s architecture guide.60 Building began in June 1929 and was finished in 1930.5957

The plan

From the street the house reads as a single storey with an entrance, garage and bedrooms. The main floor below is one open space divided only by free-standing walls and curtains, with a winter garden along one side and a stair to the garden. The kitchen and staff rooms have their own entrance; heating, air conditioning and the window motors are in the basement.5760

Materials

A free-standing wall of onyx from Morocco, a curved screen of macassar ebony around the dining table, Italian travertine and steel columns.5860 Two of the huge living-room panes sink fully into the floor, driven by motors.6157

What it said

Critics argued at once over whether it was luxury or a new way of living. In 1931 the journal Die Form asked whether one could live in it at all, and Grete replied that the family lived there very gladly.62 The Tugendhats emigrated and the house was later confiscated by the Gestapo.63

For the novelist

Your character’s living room has no wall on which to hang a picture. They lower the window into the floor on summer evenings. The street sees a garage.

Disputed

Grete’s 1931 reply survives in translation in most accounts; check the German original before quoting it at length.62

Pl. 09

Villa Müller

Prague, Czech Republic · 1928–1930

Client
František and Milada Müller
Architect
Adolf Loos with Karel Lhota
Built
1928–1930
Style
Raumplan
Visiting
Open to visit Prague City Museum; reopened in autumn 2026 after repairs, with small guided groups (as of November 2026).64
Privacy 5/5Display 3/5

A blank white cube outside, an interior of half-levels where the lady of the house could watch without being seen.

The commission

František Müller co-owned one of Czechoslovakia’s largest construction firms, founded in Pilsen by his father and Vojtěch Kapsa.65 UNESCO’s tentative-list description says the house was meant to advertise the firm’s building skills as well as to be a home.66 Loos designed it with Karel Lhota; it was built in 1928–30.67

The plan

Loos’s Raumplan sets rooms at different levels, each with a ceiling height matched to its importance. From the entrance a few steps rise through a low recess into the living room; the dining room sits on a half-level above it, and the lady’s boudoir above that has a small window looking down into the living room.68 Further up are the library, the master’s study, bedrooms with separate dressing rooms, a Japanese-style summer dining room and a roof terrace facing Prague Castle.6867

Materials

Green and grey Cipollino marble in the living room, mahogany in the dining room and library, lemonwood in the boudoir, pear wood in the bedroom.68 Outside it is a plain white cube with yellow window frames.6869

What it said

A builder’s house, full of costly materials but plain on the outside, showing off craft to people invited in. Visitors who were not admitted further were received in a separate room.64

For the novelist

Your character can watch the drawing room from her boudoir without being seen. After the Communist takeover the house was confiscated, and Milada Müller stayed on in a corner of it; accounts differ on which rooms she was left.7069

Disputed

Sources give 1968 or 1969 for Milada Müller’s death.6570

Pl. 10

Villa Savoye

Poissy, France · 1928–1931

Client
Pierre and Eugénie Savoye
Architect
Le Corbusier and Pierre Jeanneret
Built
1928–1931
Style
International Style
Visiting
Open to visit Bought by the French state in 1962;71 open Tuesday to Sunday (as of November 2026).72
Privacy 3/5Display 4/5

Lifted above a meadow and seen from all sides, with the servants and cars tucked underneath.

The commission

Pierre Savoye co-founded an insurance brokerage;73 Eugénie Savoye wrote the brief in 1928 for a country house near Paris for weekends and entertaining.7475 The final scheme was settled in November 1928, and the house was built in 1929–31.71

The plan

The house sits on thin columns (pilotis). The curved glass wall of the ground floor follows the turning circle of a car, so a driver could pull in under the house, drop the family at the door and carry on to the garage without reversing. The ground floor holds the hall, a three-car garage, the chauffeur’s room, a laundry and servants’ rooms. A ramp rises through the house to the living floor and then outside to the roof solarium.7471

Materials

A reinforced-concrete frame with infill walls, ribbon windows and a roof garden; the living room was painted light blue, pink and white.74

What it said

The plan was shaped by the car. Arriving by motor car was part of the design, and the family were raised a storey above their staff and vehicles.

For the novelist

Your character drives out from Paris on Saturday and leaves the car for the chauffeur under the house. By the mid-1930s Eugénie was writing to Le Corbusier that it rained in the hall and on the ramp.74

Disputed

Eugénie’s letters of 1936 and 1937 are quoted in different versions, and how far the leaks made the house unusable is told differently.747675

Pl. 11

Maison de Verre

Paris, France · 1928–1932

Client
Dr Jean and Annie Dalsace
Architect
Pierre Chareau with Bernard Bijvoet and Louis Dalbet
Built
1928–1932
Style
Machine aesthetic
Visiting
Limited access Privately owned; not a museum, with occasional visits by arrangement (as of November 2026).77
Privacy 5/5Display 3/5

Hidden behind a courtyard, walled in glass that admits light but not eyes; a doctor’s practice and a salon under one roof.

The commission

Jean Dalsace was a gynaecologist and an early advocate of family planning in France; Annie Dalsace came from a prominent family with property money, and her parents bought the old town house on the rue Saint-Guillaume.7879 Chareau, a furniture and interior designer, had already furnished their flat. He designed the house with the Dutch architect Bernard Bijvoet and the metalworker Louis Dalbet; work began around 1928.8081

The plan

An elderly tenant on the top floor would not leave, so the lower floors were removed and the new house built underneath her flat.8078 The ground floor was Dr Dalsace’s surgery, with waiting room and consulting rooms. The first floor has a double-height salon with a wall of books; bedrooms are above.7882 A rotating screen hid the family stair from patients during the day.77

Materials

Walls of glass blocks, exposed black-painted steel, perforated metal screens and studded rubber floors; a suspended rail carried food from the kitchen.82 The translucent walls let in daylight while keeping out the neighbours’ view.83

What it said

A professional household with avant-garde friends. The Dalsaces held salons for painters, writers and musicians a floor above the consulting rooms.80

For the novelist

Your character works downstairs and entertains upstairs, and the patients never see the family. An old woman in the flat above refused to move.

Disputed

Sources give 1931 or 1932 for completion, and disagree on Bijvoet’s share of the design.8381

Pl. 12

Eltham Palace

London, England · 1933–1936

Client
Stephen and Virginia Courtauld
Architect
Seely & Paget
Built
1933–1936
Style
Art Deco
Visiting
Open to visit English Heritage; open to visitors, with opening days varying by season, so check before travelling (as of November 2026).84
Privacy 2/5Display 5/5

A house for large parties joined to a medieval great hall, with up-to-date private suites upstairs.

The commission

Stephen Courtauld came from the Courtauld textile family but did not join the firm; he climbed, collected art and backed Ealing Studios.85 In 1933 he and Virginia took a 99-year lease on the ruined royal palace at Eltham and hired Seely & Paget to build a new house beside the restored medieval great hall.84

The plan

A “butterfly” plan with one wing of living rooms running into the great hall and the other holding dining and service rooms.86 The entrance hall sits under a glass dome, designed by the Swedish designer Rolf Engströmer with inlaid figures in its veneered walls, and the dining room has an aluminium-leaf ceiling and doors inlaid with animals drawn at London Zoo.87 The couple’s ring-tailed lemur, Mah-Jongg, had centrally heated quarters on the first floor.88

Materials

Bird’s-eye maple, onyx and gold mosaic in Virginia’s bathroom, a private telephone exchange, loudspeakers for records, electric clocks and a central vacuum plant.878489

What it said

Inherited textile money that liked comfort and technology and enjoyed entertaining. Guests included Queen Mary.84

For the novelist

Your character keeps a lemur that bites guests. In 1940 incendiary bombs hit the great-hall roof, and the couple left in 1944.8488

Disputed

Sources date the work 1933–35 or 1933–36.8684

Pl. 13

Casa Malaparte

Capri, Italy · 1938–1940s

Client
Curzio Malaparte
Architect
Curzio Malaparte, with mason Adolfo Amitrano (Adalberto Libera consulted)
Built
1938–1940s
Style
Personal rationalism
Visiting
Closed Privately held; not open to visitors (as of November 2026).9091
Privacy 5/5Display 3/5

Reachable only on foot or by sea, but its red mass and roof-stair, seen from the sea and on film, made it famous.

The commission

Curzio Malaparte, the pen name of Kurt Erich Suckert, was a writer and journalist who joined the Fascist party, was expelled in 1933 and sent into internal exile.92 He bought a headland on Capri in 1936; permission to build there came in 1938 with high-level political help.9394

The plan

A long red block on a cliff, with a stair that widens as it rises to a roof terrace screened by a curved white wall.95 The first floor holds a large salone, bedrooms and a study over the sea; a glass-backed fireplace puts the view of the sea behind the flames.9496

Materials

Red-rendered masonry, stone floors, and much of the original furniture.9397

What it said

Malaparte called the house a portrait of himself in stone.94 The status on show was celebrity and access to power rather than inherited money.

For the novelist

Your character built himself a house with a single view and photographed himself in it. Jean-Luc Godard filmed Le Mépris there in the early 1960s.96 Malaparte left the house to China (to the Chinese Writers’ Association, in some accounts), a bequest that never took effect.9291

Disputed

Authorship is the great argument. Adalberto Libera was long credited, but research by Marida Talamona argued that Malaparte and local builders changed Libera’s scheme so much that the house is essentially Malaparte’s.949590

Pl. 14

Villa Mairea

Noormarkku, Finland · 1938–1939

Client
Maire and Harry Gullichsen
Architect
Alvar and Aino Aalto
Built
1938–1939
Style
Nordic organic modernism
Visiting
Open to visit Villa Mairea Foundation; guided tours with advance tickets, all year apart from short winter closures (as of November 2026).98
Privacy 3/5Display 4/5

Public entertaining and art downstairs, opening onto a courtyard; bedrooms and a painting studio upstairs.

The commission

Maire Gullichsen was an heir to A. Ahlström, a family industrial firm with forest and paper interests; her husband Harry ran the company. Maire was a painter and collector who co-founded Artek with the Aaltos in 1935.99 The house was designed from 1937 and completed in 1939.100

The plan

A two-storey house and a separate sauna enclose a garden around a free-form pool. On the ground floor, a few steps lead up from the hall into one large living space that a sliding wall opens entirely to the garden; upstairs are the bedrooms and Maire’s studio.100101 An early scheme had a separate art gallery, later dropped.102

Materials

Teak and pine cladding, stone and render outside; a screen of irregular wooden poles at the stair; steel columns wrapped in rattan or wood strips.100102

What it said

Industrial wealth spent on art and on modern design. Docomomo Finland calls it Aalto’s most complex and opulent private house.103

For the novelist

Your character first saw her finished house in photographs at the New York World’s Fair.99 She paints upstairs while the company entertains below.

Disputed

Aino Aalto’s role is often under-credited; the Foundation credits her with the interiors.104 In July 2026 the house became part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing of Aalto works.105

Maison Louis Carré

Bazoches-sur-Guyonne, France · 1956–1959

Client
Louis and Olga Carré
Architect
Alvar Aalto, with Elissa Aalto
Built
1956–1959
Style
Late Aalto
Visiting
Open to visit Association Alvar Aalto en France; guided tours on weekend afternoons, March to November, booking required (as of November 2026).106
Privacy 3/5Display 5/5

A dealer’s house where the entrance hall is a gallery for clients and the private wing has its own saunas and walled gardens.

The commission

Louis Carré was a Paris art dealer who opened his gallery in 1938 and showed Léger, Dufy, Villon and others.107 He wanted a villa of the highest artistic quality and comfort, and commissioned Aalto in the mid-1950s; the Carrés moved in in June 1959.108109

The plan

The entrance hall, under a vaulted wooden ceiling, has panels for hanging paintings and steps down into the living room. The house divides into an entertaining section and a service section, with the bedrooms on the service side.108 The Carrés had separate bedrooms, each with a sauna and a sheltered garden; four staff rooms are under the raised roof.110108

Materials

Blue slate roof, limestone, rendered brick, Finnish pine for the ceiling vault, which was built by Finnish carpenters.108111 The furniture Aalto designed for the house is protected as a historic object and must stay in it.112

What it said

A house that was also a showroom. Selling pictures and receiving guests were planned into it from the start.108111

For the novelist

Your character hangs the paintings he hopes to sell where the client will stand to take off his coat. Each spouse has their own sauna. Olga Carré lived there until 2002.112

Disputed

Sources differ on when Carré first approached Aalto and on Elissa Aalto’s share of the design.108110

IVMethod and limits

Selection: houses designed for named private clients, built between about 1890 and the present, that are now museums, protected monuments or documented in depth by archives and heritage bodies. The guide stops in 1959 because most later houses of this kind are still private homes, and it does not discuss current private owners, residents or property values.

Privacy and display scores are the desk’s editorial judgement, from 1 to 5, based on the sourced descriptions: how far the plan screens the household from the street, visitors and staff (privacy), and how far the house was designed to be seen, visited or used for entertaining and showing things (display). They are a way of comparing houses, not measurements.

Section diagrams show the documented use of each floor, simplified. They are schematic and not drawn to scale. Visiting information was checked in November 2026 and will change; check the house’s own site before travelling.
