Seventeen decisions on the record, 1494 to 1995

The Decisions That Accidentally Built Our World

Seventeen treaties, standards and committee choices, from Tordesillas to the 160-character text, with who made each one, what they were trying to do, and what followed that nobody planned.

The width of a railway, the length of a text message, the reason Brazil speaks Portuguese: each was settled by a handful of people with a particular problem in front of them. Most of them were solving something small, a timetable, a tax, a jammed typebar, a deadline. What they produced outlived the problem by decades or centuries.

This is a record of seventeen such decisions, set out like the minutes of a meeting. For each one there is the problem, who decided, what else was on the table, why they chose as they did, and what followed. Each item separates what the decision-makers meant to do from what they did not, and 8 of them carry a myth check where the popular story has outgrown the record. Every item ends with a counterfactual. Those are labelled as speculation, because they are.

A few rules for reading it. Dates are given as the sources give them, and where sources disagree the item says so. Figures that change, such as domain income or IPv6 adoption, carry the date they were reported. Numbers in small type link to the sources at the foot of the page.

Five ways a small decision gets big

Sorting the seventeen by how their consequences grew, rather than by subject, shows the same few patterns again and again.

A line on an unseen map

Negotiators fixed a line across territory they had never surveyed, often against a deadline. The line stayed; the reasons for it were forgotten.

An industry's fix made universal

A fix devised by or for one industry (shipping, railways, trucking, grocery retail) was written into a rule or standard that then spread far beyond the industry that devised it.

Someone declined to make a hard call and pointed to a list or to each government instead. The neutral rule produced winners nobody picked.

Item 1

1494

When
7 June 14941,3
Where
Tordesillas, Castile
Decided by
Representatives of John II of Portugal and of Ferdinand and Isabella of Castile and Aragon

BordersA line on an unseen map

A meridian through an ocean nobody had mapped

Resolved The world outside Europe is split between two crowns along a north–south line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands: Portugal to the east, Castile to the west.1,3

The problem

Columbus came back in 1493 claiming lands for Castile, and Portugal, which had spent decades working down the African coast, objected. A papal bull of May 1493, Inter caetera, drew a pole-to-pole line 100 leagues west of the Azores and Cape Verde and gave what lay beyond it to Castile.2,3

What was on the table

Accept the pope's line at 100 leagues, or negotiate directly. John II thought the papal line left him too little sea room on the route round Africa, and the two crowns settled on 370 leagues instead.3

Why they chose it

Both sides wanted a clear rule they could ask the pope to confirm, and the treaty text asks for exactly that.1

What followed3

  1. The line is moved 270 leagues west of the papal one
  2. The eastern bulge of South America falls on Portugal's side
  3. Cabral lands there in 1500 and claims it for Portugal
  4. Brazil becomes the Portuguese-speaking country of the Americas

Meant to

Divide newly found lands and sea routes between Portugal and Castile without war.

Didn't mean to

Nobody in 1494 knew South America's coast poked east of the line. Portugal's claim to Brazil rests on that geographical accident.

In the retelling

The line was a precise boundary.

On the record

Nobody could then measure longitude reliably or agree on the length of a league, and modern estimates of where the line actually ran differ by several degrees. England, France and the Netherlands never accepted it, and by the late 1500s it was routinely ignored at sea.3

Counterfactual (speculative)

Had John II accepted the papal line at 100 leagues, the coast where Cabral landed would probably have fallen in Castile's half, and a Spanish-speaking Brazil is easy to imagine.

Item 2

1818

When
20 October 1818 (extended 15 June 1846)4,6
Where
London; extended in Washington
Decided by
Albert Gallatin and Richard Rush for the United States; Frederick John Robinson and Henry Goulburn for Britain4,5

BordersA line on an unseen map

A straight line across country neither side had surveyed

Resolved From the Lake of the Woods west to the 'Stony Mountains', the boundary between the United States and British North America runs along the 49th parallel. The Oregon Country beyond is left open to both for ten years.4,7

The problem

The 1783 peace treaty described a border running west from the Lake of the Woods to the Mississippi. Drawn on an inaccurate map, the line could not exist: the Mississippi does not reach that far north.9

What was on the table

West of the Rockies the United States wanted the 49th parallel carried to the Pacific, and Britain wanted the Columbia River. Neither gave way in 1818, so the Oregon Country was left to joint occupation, renewed indefinitely in 1827. In the 1840s American expansionists campaigned on '54°40′ or fight'.5,7

Why they chose it

A line of latitude was easy to write down and needed no agreement about rivers, ridges or settlements. The convention itself says joint occupation should not prejudice either side's claims: the hard question was postponed.4

What followed5,6,8

  1. 1818: the parallel is fixed as far as the Rockies
  2. 1846: the Oregon Treaty carries it to the coast, with Vancouver Island left wholly British
  3. The line becomes the spine of an 8,891 km border
  4. Surveyors later mark it on the ground with thousands of monuments

Meant to

Settle the plains section and defer the Pacific coast without fighting.

Didn't mean to

The Northwest Angle: because the line runs due south from the lake's 'most north-western point' to meet the parallel, a piece of Minnesota is cut off from the rest of the United States by water and Canadian land.4,9

Counterfactual (speculative)

If Britain's Columbia River line had won in 1846, much of what is now Washington state would have been British territory. This is speculation, though it was the line Britain actually proposed.5

Item 3

1846

When
18 August 1846 (Gauge Act)46,48
Where
Westminster
Decided by
George Stephenson, whose early lines set the width; the Gauge Commission; Parliament45,47

MeasuresLocked in by everything built on top

The colliery wagon width that became the world's railway

Resolved New passenger railways in Great Britain are built to 4 feet 8½ inches, with 5 feet 3 inches in Ireland, and exceptions for the broad-gauge lines of the south-west and Wales.48

The problem

Early lines picked their own widths. Where gauges met, goods and passengers had to change trains.45

What was on the table

Stephenson's narrow width, inherited from north-eastern colliery wagonways, or Isambard Kingdom Brunel's 7-foot broad gauge on the Great Western Railway, adopted under its 1835 act.44,47

Why they chose it

By 1846 most track was already Stephenson's. The commissioners recommended that the narrower gauge be adopted by law and that broad-gauge lines be brought to it.45

What followed44,48

  1. The Gauge Act fixes the standard in 1846
  2. Broad gauge survives on the GWR for decades
  3. In the weekend of 21–22 May 1892, about 4,200 men convert the last 171 route miles
  4. Stephenson's gauge spreads abroad and becomes the world's most common

Meant to

One network in Britain, on the gauge most of it already used.

Didn't mean to

A width chosen for horse-drawn coal wagons now governs high-speed lines across continents.

In the retelling

The gauge comes from the ruts of Roman chariots.

On the record

Roman ruts were of similar width, as many horse-drawn vehicles were, but nothing links Roman roads to the wagonways the gauge actually came from.47

Counterfactual (speculative)

If Brunel's broad gauge had won, trains might have been wider and steadier; whether the extra cost of the track would have slowed the spread of railways is a fair question with no settled answer.

Item 4

1873

When
Layout developed from 1867; machines on sale by 187450,67
Where
Milwaukee; manufactured in Ilion, New York
Decided by
Christopher Latham Sholes and Carlos Glidden, inventors; James Densmore, their backer; E. Remington & Sons, the manufacturer50,67

Keys and networksLocked in by everything built on top

QWERTY: the layout everyone has a theory about

Resolved The Sholes and Glidden typewriter goes into production with the keyboard that, after many revisions, became QWERTY.

The problem

Building a typewriter that worked and could be sold.

What was on the table

The layout changed many times as the machine was improved. Densmore struck a deal with Remington, a gun and sewing-machine maker, around 1873.50

Why they chose it

This is where accounts part company. The Wisconsin Historical Society says the aim was to separate commonly used letters so the typebars jammed less. The Kyoto researchers Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka argue that the layout was shaped by the needs of telegraph operators transcribing Morse.49,50

What followed49,50

  1. Remington's machines sell slowly, then widely
  2. Teletype adopts the layout for its teleprinters
  3. Computer terminals inherit it from teleprinters
  4. Phones copy it onto glass

Meant to

A machine that sold.

Didn't mean to

A layout outliving every mechanical reason for it. The economist Paul David made QWERTY the textbook case of 'path dependence'; Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis replied that the evidence for better layouts such as Dvorak is weak.51,52

In the retelling

QWERTY was designed to slow typists down.

On the record

It is repeated even by reputable institutions, but no contemporary evidence shows it. The jam-reduction and telegraph explanations are the serious contenders, and they are still debated.49,67

Counterfactual (speculative)

If a different layout had shipped in 1874, David's argument suggests it would have locked in just as firmly. His critics would reply that QWERTY was never shown to be worse, so little would have changed.

Item 5

1883

When
Adopted 11 October 1883; in force 18 November 188328,29
Where
Grand Pacific Hotel, Chicago
Decided by
The General Time Convention, a railway body, on a plan by its secretary William F. Allen28,29

TimeAn industry's fix made universal

The railways set America's clocks, and the law caught up 35 years later

Resolved North American railways run on standard time zones an hour apart, four of them across the United States, Eastern, Central, Mountain and Pacific, from Sunday 18 November 1883.28,31

The problem

Towns kept their own local solar time, leaving the railways juggling more than a hundred local times on their timetables.28

What was on the table

Carry on with each railway's own time, or adopt shared zones. Allen spent months consulting railway and scientific figures before presenting his plan in April 1883.29

Why they chose it

Timetables. Allen said that under the plan 'local time would be practically abolished'.29

What followed29,30,31

  1. The 'day of two noons': cities reset clocks to the new standard
  2. Some authorities resist at first, Chicago's among them
  3. Daily life follows the railways' time anyway
  4. Congress writes the zones into law in the Standard Time Act of 19 March 1918

Meant to

Simpler railway timetables.

Didn't mean to

A private industry agreement became the clock of a continent with no legal force behind it for a generation. The 1918 act also brought in federal daylight saving time, repealed the following year while standard time stayed law.31,32

Counterfactual (speculative)

Without the railways' move, Congress would probably have had to impose zones on a country of local times, a far harder political sell than ratifying a habit people already lived by.

Item 6

1884

When
October 1884
Where
Washington DC
Decided by
41 delegates from 25 nations at the International Meridian Conference, convened by the US government24,27

TimeAn industry's fix made universal

Greenwich wins a vote the ships had already cast

Resolved The meridian passing through the centre of the transit instrument at the Royal Observatory, Greenwich, becomes the world's prime meridian. Passed 22 to 1 (San Domingo against), with France and Brazil abstaining.23,24,25

The problem

Maps and charts counted longitude from many national zero lines, including Paris, Berlin and Washington. There was no shared reference for navigation or timekeeping.25

What was on the table

Paris, Washington, or a 'neutral' meridian, which France proposed, for example through the Canary Islands. The American delegates themselves proposed Greenwich.24

Why they chose it

Most shipping already used it. A survey by the Canadian engineer Sandford Fleming found that about 72 per cent of the world's shipping by tonnage used charts based on Greenwich, against 8 per cent for Paris.25,27

What followed23,25,26

  1. Greenwich becomes the zero of longitude
  2. A 'universal day' starting at Greenwich midnight is adopted
  3. Nations later choose their own offsets from Greenwich one by one
  4. France adopts Greenwich time in 1911, and Greenwich longitude for nautical documents from 1914

Meant to

Ratify the line most navigators already used.

Didn't mean to

The resolutions were not binding, and adoption depended on each government. The conference's fame now attaches things to it that it never did.24

In the retelling

The 1884 conference created the world's time zones.

On the record

It did not. It adopted a prime meridian and a universal day, and said that this should not interfere with local or standard time. Zones came later, country by country.23,25

Counterfactual (speculative)

Had France's neutral meridian won, longitudes on every chart would differ by a fixed offset. Little else would change, which is part of why the majority went with Greenwich.

Item 7

1884

When
15 November 1884 – 26 February 188513,14
Where
Berlin, Wilhelmstraße 77
Decided by
Fourteen states, hosted by Otto von Bismarck. No African ruler or representative took part13,14

BordersA line on an unseen map

The conference that did not draw Africa's borders

Resolved The General Act provides for free trade in the Congo basin, free navigation of the Congo and Niger, a ban on the slave trade there, and rules (Articles 34 and 35) for notifying and 'effectively' occupying new possessions on the African coasts.13,15

The problem

European claims were colliding, above all over the Congo basin and the Niger. The scramble for territory was already under way.13,14

What was on the table

Settle claims one treaty at a time, or agree common rules. Berlin chose rules; the territorial bargaining mostly happened elsewhere.

Why they chose it

The powers wanted to avoid war with each other and to keep the Congo open to their trade. African interests were not represented.

What followed13,14

  1. The Act sets procedure, not maps
  2. Bilateral treaties over the next decades fix most actual borders
  3. The rules legitimise and speed up the partition
  4. Leopold II's Congo Free State is recognised in side agreements

Meant to

A legal framework for European expansion that would keep the powers from fighting each other.

Didn't mean to

The conference's reputation. It is now shorthand for the whole partition, which flattens a longer story of bilateral deals.

In the retelling

Diplomats in Berlin sat round a map and carved up Africa.

On the record

The German Foreign Office's own archive says the conference drew no specific borders. Political scientist Jack Paine has said it 'established little in the way of making states' beyond what became the Democratic Republic of the Congo.13,14

Counterfactual (speculative)

Without Berlin's rules the same powers would almost certainly still have partitioned the continent; the open question is how many conflicts between them the rules prevented.

Item 8

1908

When
24 July 190839,40
Where
Windsor Castle to the White City Stadium, London
Decided by
Lord Desborough, head of the London Olympic organisers; Jack Andrew of the Polytechnic Harriers, who set the course39

MeasuresA stopgap that stayed

Why the marathon is 26 miles and 385 yards

Resolved The Olympic marathon starts on the East Lawn at Windsor Castle and finishes in front of the Royal Box at White City: 26 miles 385 yards, or 42.195 km.39,40

The problem

The marathon had no fixed length. The 1896 race in Athens was about 40 km, and early Olympic marathons varied in length.41

What was on the table

Any route the organisers liked. Starting at Windsor was Desborough's idea to win royal interest; the programme made it subject to the King's consent.39,40

Why they chose it

The 385 yards was the distance from the stadium entrance round the track to a finish below the Royal Box.39

What followed40,41

  1. One London course sets a famous distance
  2. Dorando Pietri collapses on the track, is helped and is disqualified; Johnny Hayes wins in 2:55:18.4
  3. The international athletics federation adopts the distance in 1921
  4. Olympic marathons have used it since 1924

Meant to

A course that started and ended where the royal family could take part.

Didn't mean to

A world standard. Olympedia calls it 'this rather arbitrary distance, which had no particular historical or athletic significance'.40

In the retelling

Queen Alexandra asked for the start at Windsor so the royal children could watch from the nursery.

On the record

Popular histories tell it with 'reportedly'; Olympedia calls the nursery claim certainly false. The better-documented account credits the Windsor start to Desborough. The AIMS historian Hugh Jones adds that the distance 'can only be taken on trust': the start was loosely described and the early miles may have been slightly short.39,40,41

Counterfactual (speculative)

If London had finished at the stadium entrance, the standard might have been a round 26 miles, and generations of runners would have been spared the last 385 yards.

Item 9

1916

When
30 April 1916 (Germany and Austria-Hungary); 21 May 1916 (Britain)36,37,38
Where
Berlin, Vienna, then London
Decided by
The German and Austro-Hungarian wartime governments; in Britain, Parliament

TimeA stopgap that stayed

A wartime coal-saving measure that most of Europe still observes

Resolved Clocks go forward an hour for the summer to make better use of daylight.

The problem

In wartime, fuel used for lighting was fuel not available for the war.36

What was on the table

Nothing, or one of several schemes. In Britain the builder William Willett had campaigned since 1907, when he published The Waste of Daylight at his own expense, proposing four 20-minute steps forward in April and back in September. Bills had stalled; he died in 1915.36,38

Why they chose it

Saving coal. Germany moved first, and Britain followed within weeks.31,36

What followed31,36,37

  1. Germany makes the world's first nationwide daylight saving change
  2. Britain's bill passes on 17 May and clocks go forward on 21 May
  3. The United States follows in 1918
  4. The twice-yearly change outlives the war that justified it

Meant to

A wartime economy measure.

Didn't mean to

Permanence. The emergency rule became an annual ritual, and arguments about abolishing it now recur in parliaments on both sides of the Atlantic.

In the retelling

Willett wanted more daylight for golf.

On the record

The Western Front Association repeats the story that a round cut short by dusk set him thinking. It may be true, but it is an anecdote; his pamphlet made the case on wasted daylight.36,38

Counterfactual (speculative)

Without the war, Willett's idea might have stayed a pamphlet. It had failed in Parliament before 1914, and the fuel crisis was what moved it.

Item 10

1922

When
1922 (DIN 476); 1975 (ISO 216)42
Where
Berlin; then international
Decided by
Walter Porstmann, engineer, and the German standards committee; later ISO42

MeasuresLocked in by everything built on top

A4, and the American sheet that kept its own size

Resolved Paper sizes follow a 1:√2 ratio from a base sheet of one square metre, so each size is the one above halved. A4 is 210 by 297 mm.42,43

The problem

Incompatible paper sizes made stocking paper and copying documents costly.42

What was on the table

Older national formats, or a system on the √2 principle. The idea was old: Georg Christoph Lichtenberg described the ratio in a letter of 25 October 1786, and a French tax law of 1798 defined formats that match later ISO sizes. It then fell out of use until Porstmann revived it.42,43

Why they chose it

Halving a √2 sheet gives the same shape, so enlarging and reducing work cleanly and one series covers everything from posters to postcards.42

What followed42

  1. DIN 476 is adopted in Germany
  2. Belgium follows in 1924, the UK in 1959, France in 1967
  3. ISO 216 makes it the international standard in 1975
  4. Almost every country uses it; the United States and Canada are the main exceptions

Meant to

A rational, interchangeable system of sizes.

Didn't mean to

The American split. In 1921 Herbert Hoover, as Commerce Secretary, standardised 8 by 10½ inches for government use, while a printing-industry committee recommended a 17 by 22 inch base sheet that halves to 8½ by 11. The federal government switched to 8½ by 11 only in 1980. Each choice locked in its own printers, files and envelopes.42

Counterfactual (speculative)

Had the United States adopted ISO sizes when Britain did, an American letter would probably be a sheet of A4, and documents would cross the Atlantic without anyone adjusting margins.

Item 11

1947

When
July–August 1947
Where
New Delhi
Decided by
Sir Cyril Radcliffe, a British lawyer, chairing the Punjab and Bengal boundary commissions; appointed under Lord Mountbatten, the last viceroy10,11

BordersA line on an unseen map

A border drawn in five weeks by a man new to India

Resolved Punjab and Bengal are divided between India and Pakistan by 'contiguous majority areas of Muslims and non-Muslims', taking account of 'other factors'.11

The problem

On 3 June 1947 Mountbatten announced that power would be handed over on 15 August. The two provinces had to be split in the weeks between.11

What was on the table

Each commission had four judges, two nominated by Congress and two by the Muslim League. They deadlocked, so the final decisions fell to Radcliffe.11,12

Why they chose it

Radcliffe was acceptable to both sides because he was a stranger to India and its politics. That same quality meant he worked from census tables and maps, without a field survey, to a deadline he did not set.11

What followed11,12

  1. Radcliffe arrives in early July 1947
  2. The awards are published on 17 August, two days after independence
  3. Millions wake up on independence day unsure which country they live in
  4. Mass migration and killing follow; disputes over rivers and Kashmir outlast the decade

Meant to

A quick, legally defensible division by religious majority, finished before the British left.

Didn't mean to

Estimates of the displaced run from about 10 to 20 million, and of the dead from about 200,000 to two million. None is precise.10,11,12,71

In the retelling

One man with a pencil decided everything.

On the record

He did make the final calls, but inside terms of reference set by Mountbatten and agreed by the parties. How far Mountbatten influenced late changes, notably over Ferozepur, is still argued over by historians.10,11

Counterfactual (speculative)

A later handover date might have allowed a field survey and published awards before independence; whether that would have reduced the violence is unknowable.

Item 12

1956

When
26 April 1956; ISO standard 196853,54,55
Where
Port Newark to Houston; later ISO
Decided by
Malcom McLean, a North Carolina trucking operator; later ISO's freight-container committee53,55

TradeAn industry's fix made universal

A trucker's box that rebuilt world trade

Resolved Load the truck body, not the cargo. The converted tanker Ideal-X sails with 58 containers on deck.54

The problem

Loose cargo was loaded by hand, piece by piece, and was slow and expensive to handle. Levinson notes that shipping goods abroad could cost 15–20 per cent of their value.53

What was on the table

Break-bulk cargo, as before, or boxes. McLean's first boxes were 35 feet, the size of the truck trailers of the day; rival firms used other sizes.54

Why they chose it

Cost. By McLean's reckoning, loading the Ideal-X cost under 16 cents a ton, against $5.83 for conventional methods.54

What followed53,55

  1. McLean sells his trucking firm to go into shipping
  2. Boxes of many sizes compete through the 1960s
  3. ISO publishes standard container dimensions in 1968, with an 8 by 8 foot cross-section
  4. Standard boxes run between ships, trains and lorries worldwide

Meant to

Cheaper coastal shipping in the United States.

Didn't mean to

Globalisation. Economists Daniel Bernhofen, Zouheir El-Sahli and Richard Kneller found the container's effect on trade 'economically large', supporting the view that it was a driver of 20th-century globalisation.56

Counterfactual (speculative)

Without an agreed standard, containers would probably have stayed proprietary systems, a box that fitted one line's ships and cranes and nobody else's.

Item 13

1973

When
Spring 1973; first scan 26 June 197457,59
Where
United States; first scan at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio
Decided by
The US grocery industry's ad hoc committee on a product code, formed in 1970 with help from the consultancy McKinsey; George Laurer of IBM, who designed the winning symbol57,58

TradeAn industry's fix made universal

The grocery committee that chose the barcode

Resolved The industry adopts IBM's design, now the Universal Product Code, as its one standard symbol.58,60

The problem

Checkouts were slow, and stock was counted by hand.

What was on the table

Seven designs were submitted. Among them was RCA's round 'bull's-eye', a descendant of the patent Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver filed in 1949. Laurer thought it smeared when printed.58,59

Why they chose it

Reliability under real printing. Laurer said of the bull's-eye: 'My nature and my training would not allow me to support something I didn't believe in.'58

What followed57,58,59

  1. A pack of Wrigley's gum is scanned at Marsh on 26 June 1974
  2. Retailers and manufacturers print codes on everything
  3. The system grows into the GS1 standards
  4. GS1 says barcodes are now scanned billions of times a day

Meant to

Faster checkouts for American grocers.

Didn't mean to

A global identity system for physical goods, and with it the data on what sells where.

IBM dates the code's official birth to 1 April 1973; GS1 UK dates the committee's choice to 3 April.58,59

Counterfactual (speculative)

If the committee had picked the bull's-eye, the familiar stripes would be rings. More importantly, without a single industry choice, rival symbols could have split American retail between incompatible scanners.

Item 14

1977

When
Around 1977; specified in RFC 791, September 198168
Where
US Defense Department research programme
Decided by
Vint Cerf, then running the internet research programme at the Defense Department, who has taken responsibility for the choice63

Keys and networksA stopgap that stayed

Four billion addresses 'for an experiment'

Resolved Each internet address is 32 bits long, allowing about 4.3 billion addresses.

The problem

An experimental network protocol needed an address size.

What was on the table

Larger addresses were possible, at the cost of overhead on every packet. IPv6, its successor, uses 128 bits.65

Why they chose it

Cerf has said repeatedly that it was enough for what he thought was an experiment: 'It's enough to do an experiment... The problem is the experiment never ended.'63

What followed64,65,66

  1. The experiment becomes the internet
  2. Address-sharing workarounds spread to stretch the supply
  3. IANA hands out its last free blocks on 3 February 2011
  4. IPv6 is standardised; in late March 2026 it first carries just over half of users' connections to Google

Meant to

A workable research network.

Didn't mean to

A global shortage, and decades of engineering to manage it.

Counterfactual (speculative)

Had Cerf chosen 128 bits in 1977, the shortage would probably never have arrived. The cost would have been larger headers on every packet across the slow links of the time.

Item 15

1984

When
October 1984; written up March 199416,17
Where
University of Southern California's Information Sciences Institute
Decided by
Jon Postel and Joyce Reynolds, who managed early internet naming and numbering

Keys and networksJudgement handed elsewhere

The engineer who refused to decide what a country is

Resolved Country domains use the two-letter codes of the ISO 3166 standard. In Postel's later words: 'The IANA is not in the business of deciding what is and what is not a country.'16,17

The problem

The domain name system needed a rule for which places get their own top-level domain, without turning engineers into arbiters of statehood.

What was on the table

Judge each request on its merits, or borrow a list someone else maintains. Postel borrowed ISO's.

Why they chose it

Neutrality. ISO 3166 already listed countries and territories with codes, so disputes about the list were ISO's to handle.17

What followed

  1. Every territory with an ISO code becomes eligible for a domain, however small
  2. Tuvalu's .tv and Anguilla's .ai become valuable to media and AI companies
  3. A code's future becomes tied to geopolitics, as with .io
  4. Domain income becomes a line in small states' budgets

Meant to

A neutral, mechanical rule that kept politics out of the root of the internet.

Didn't mean to

Small places gained a saleable asset. Marketplace reported in December 2023 that Anguilla's .ai earned about $3 million a month, roughly a third of the government's monthly budget, and that Tuvalu's deal for .tv was estimated at up to $10 million a year.19

The rule cuts both ways for .io, the British Indian Ocean Territory's domain. If the territory's ISO code were withdrawn, ICANN's policy starts a retirement process of five years, which can be extended. The UK and Mauritius signed a treaty on the Chagos Archipelago on 22 May 2025; as of early 2026 ratification was still incomplete. The UK, meanwhile, is the visible exception: its domain is .uk, while its ISO code is GB. Early participants recall that .uk began at the UK's own request.18,20,21,22

Counterfactual (speculative)

If IANA had judged statehood itself, every contested territory would likely have become a fight over a line in the root zone file, and the internet's naming body would have been dragged into diplomacy from the start.

Item 16

1985

When
1985; first message 3 December 199261,62
Where
Bonn; first message on the Vodafone network in Britain
Decided by
Friedhelm Hillebrand of the German post office, who chaired GSM's work on non-voice services, with Bernard Ghillebaert70

Keys and networksA stopgap that stayed

160 characters, counted on a typewriter

Resolved A text message on the GSM mobile standard carries up to 160 characters.

The problem

GSM needed a cheap way to send short messages, using a radio channel that was otherwise barely used.62

What was on the table

Longer or shorter. Hillebrand's own account, given to the Los Angeles Times in 2009, is that he typed sample messages on a typewriter at home in Bonn and counted their length.69

Why they chose it

He noted that postcards and telex messages usually ran to about 150 characters, and judged 160 enough for most messages.62

What followed61

  1. SMS goes into the GSM standard
  2. Neil Papworth sends 'Merry Christmas' from a computer to Vodafone's Richard Jarvis on 3 December 1992
  3. Texting becomes a mass habit

Meant to

A side channel for short notices.

Didn't mean to

A new form of writing, with its own abbreviations, shaped by a limit set for a signalling channel.

The typewriter story comes from Hillebrand's own later retelling; the postcard and telex comparison is how he has explained the number since.62,69

Counterfactual (speculative)

A much lower limit might have kept texting a paging service, and a much higher one might have made it a form of email. 160 landed in between.

Item 17

1995

When
1 January 1995 (Kiribati); 30 December 2011 (Samoa)
Where
The central Pacific
Decided by
Each country's own government

TimeJudgement handed elsewhere

The line no treaty fixes

Resolved There is no resolution. The civil date line is not defined by international law; each country picks its own date, and the line goes where those choices put it.33

The problem

Somewhere on the globe, the calendar has to change by a day. The 180th meridian is a convenient place because it runs mostly through open ocean.33

What was on the table

An agreed line, or none. The 1884 conference counted longitude to 180° east and west but left the date line itself undefined. At sea there is a nautical date line set by agreement; on land, governments decide.23,33

Why they chose it

Each country chooses the date that suits its trade and ties.

What followed33,34,35

  1. Kiribati drops a day in its eastern islands at the turn of 1995 so the whole country shares one date
  2. The line bulges far east around Kiribati
  3. Caroline Atoll, uninhabited, is renamed Millennium Island to sell 'first sunrise of 2000'
  4. Samoa skips Friday 30 December 2011 to align with Australia and New Zealand

Meant to

Each move suited the country making it.

Didn't mean to

A global reference line that zigzags and moves when a parliament votes. Samoa had moved the other way in 1892, observing 4 July twice to line up with trade to California.35

Accounts differ on which day Kiribati removed: timeanddate.com gives 1 January 1995, while others, such as History UK, give 31 December 1994.33,72

Counterfactual (speculative)

Had 1884 fixed a date line by treaty, Kiribati and Samoa would presumably have had to negotiate to change their calendars, not simply legislate.
