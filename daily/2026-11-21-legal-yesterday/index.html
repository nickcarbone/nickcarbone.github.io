Be it remembered that the following were once the law, or were said to be

Legal Yesterday

Twenty-six old laws, failed bills and legal legends that governed where people could go, whom they could marry, what they could drink and how they could dress, with the exact words, how they were enforced and what became of them.

17 repealed3 struck down or voided4 still in force1 never enacted1 legend

Most of the laws that shaped ordinary lives were never dramatic. They were forms, thresholds and permits: a forty-day window in which a parish could send a newcomer away14, a count of windows on a house18, a book recording each drinker’s monthly ration of spirits79, a certificate a woman needed before she could wear trousers in Paris36. Each was written to solve an administrative problem. Each decided who could move, work, own, marry or relax, and who could not.

This index gathers twenty-six of them, from the wage freeze England imposed after the Black Death to the dance clause Japan deleted in 2016. Most are dead. Three are still on the books in amended form, one survives only in bits, and two entries are not laws at all: a bill that nearly redefined pi, and a “rule” about wife-beating that no court ever laid down. Each entry gives the wording where an authoritative text exists, translated where needed, and says plainly when it doesn’t. Dates and figures come from statutes, court records, parliamentary papers and historians; where sources disagree, the entry says so.

Patterns repeat. Laws aimed at a problem of the moment, from plague-era labour shortages to wartime drunkenness, outlasted it by generations. Many were enforced through paperwork rather than police: a settlement certificate, a reference book, a motbok, a hiring certificate. And many fell not to a new law but to a court, a constitution or a minister declaring them dead.

How long they lasted

Each bar runs from enactment to repeal, abolition or today. Hatching marks what survived after the main rule ended: Norway’s Jesuit ban, the US exception for wives of Asian men, the surviving sections of the 1882 Act, the 1948 Japanese Act after its dance clause went, and the marriage bar abroad after Britain’s home civil service dropped it. The thin top line on the Red Flag Act is the flag itself. Dots are bills and legends that were never law. Tap a row to jump to its entry.

§ 1 1349 and 1351 England Repealed 1863

WorkRepealedLifespan: 1349–1863

The Ordinance and Statute of Labourers

What it did
After the Black Death, Edward III’s ordinance bound every able-bodied man and woman under sixty, “free or bond”, without land or a trade to serve anyone who asked, at the wages paid in the twentieth year of his reign (1346–47)1. Parliament’s 1351 statute fixed rates down to the job: “a mower of meadows for the acre five pence”. Refusers went to the stocks for three days or more2.
Purpose
The texts say it themselves: survivors were demanding “excessive wages” or choosing “to beg in idleness”1. The law tried to freeze the labour market at its pre-plague price.
Enforcement
Patchy but sometimes heavy. Justices raised £84 7s. 7d. in fines at Colchester in 1352, and excessive wages made up 72 per cent of cases in a Cheshire roll of 1356–59; yet seven of eight manors in Mark Bailey’s sample recorded no cases at all3. No national total of prosecutions exists.
Who it fell on
Labourers, ploughmen and servants; artisans were tied to set rates; stipendiary chaplains were pressed to serve for the customary salary12.
What became of it
Wikipedia, a secondary source, lists the Statute of Artificers (1562 or 1563, depending on the reckoning) among the labour laws that followed, and says the 1351 statute was formally repealed in 18634.
§ 2 1635 Tokugawa Japan Lifted 1866

MovementRepealedLifespan: 1635–1866

The closed-country edict

What it did
A memorandum from the shogun’s senior councillors to the governors of Nagasaki, issued under Tokugawa Iemitsu, barred Japanese ships from sailing abroad, made secret departure a capital crime and ordered death for any Japanese who came home after living overseas56. Most of its other articles concern hunting down Christians and policing foreign trade at Nagasaki6.
Purpose
Historians read it as a measure to shut out foreign, and especially Christian, influence6. The councillors’ own reasons are not stated in the text.
Enforcement
It sat inside a wider anti-Christian campaign that used informers’ rewards and the fumi-e, in which suspects were made to tread on a sacred image6. No reliable count exists of people executed for leaving or returning.
Who it fell on
Sailors, merchants and the Japanese communities already living in South-east Asia, who were cut off from home.
What became of it
There was no formal repeal. After Commodore Perry’s ships forced open Japan’s ports in 1853–546, the shogunate’s foreign ministers told the American legation in May 1866 that “all Japanese who may wish to travel abroad shall be furnished with such a permit”7. Takahiro Yamamoto argues that the new passports belatedly endorsed undercover border-crossing that was already common8.

Sources differ. The University of Pittsburgh’s text, using C. R. Boxer’s translation, heads a closely related edict “June 1636”9; most sources date it 1635.

§ 3 1647 England and Wales Voided 1660

Belief and the bodyStruck down or voidedLifespan: 1647–1660

Parliament's abolition of Christmas

What it did
The Long Parliament abolished Christmas, Easter, Whitsun and other holy days, and gave scholars, apprentices and servants a secular day off on the second Tuesday of each month instead10.
Purpose
Puritan conviction that such feasts had “no warrant in the Word of God”, as the 1645 Directory of Public Worship put it11.
Enforcement
Shops were expected to open on 25 December, and there were Christmas riots in several towns1213. Measures in the 1650s penalised Christmas services11.
Who it fell on
Everyone, but especially the clergy and the apprentices and servants who lost their traditional holidays.
The legends
Oliver Cromwell did not personally ban Christmas; the Cromwell Association calls that “a common myth”11. No law aimed at mince pies has been found. The Law Commission’s point is narrower: the only Christmas on which eating them was illegal was 1644, when 25 December fell on Parliament’s monthly fast day and the fast was ordered kept1213.
What became of it
At the Restoration in 1660 the legislation of 1642–60 was declared null and void11.
§ 4 1662 England and Wales Repealed 1948

MovementRepealedLifespan: 1662–1948

The Settlement Act

What it did
A newcomer to a parish who rented a home worth under £10 a year could, on complaint within forty days, be removed by two justices to the parish where they were last legally settled, if they were judged “likely to be chargeable” to the poor rate14.
Purpose
To decide which parish paid for whose poor relief, and to stop people moving towards parishes with better relief15. The £10 threshold was beyond an ordinary labourer15.
Enforcement
Parishes moved unmarried women who were heavily pregnant across their boundary before the birth, because a child born outside marriage was settled where it was born; employers hired for 364 days so that servants would not gain a settlement by a year’s service15. Removal long outlived the Act: in 1907 more than 12,000 people were still moved from one poor-law union to another in England and Wales16.
Who it fell on
The labouring poor, migrant workers, widows, children and unmarried mothers15.
What became of it
From 1795 newcomers could not be removed until they actually needed relief (pregnant unmarried women excepted)15. The Poor Law Act 1927 repealed most of the 1662 Act and the Statute Law Revision Act 1948 the rest17.
§ 5 1696 England and Wales Repealed 1851

Property and taxRepealedLifespan: 1696–1851

The window tax

What it did
Every inhabited house except cottages paid two shillings a year, with four shillings more for ten to nineteen windows and eight more for twenty or above, from 25 March 169618.
Purpose
William III’s government needed to make up revenue lost to the clipping of coins19.
Enforcement
Householders bricked up windows rather than pay19. When the threshold dropped to seven windows in 1766, the number of houses with exactly seven fell by nearly two-thirds20. In 1845 Dr D. B. Reid’s report on Sunderland blamed “the blocking up of the numerous windows” for the state of the town’s housing, and doctors linked dark, airless rooms to typhus and cholera20.
Who it fell on
Occupiers of houses, and through their rents the tenants of crowded urban tenements, since landlords passed the cost on20.
What became of it
A repeal motion lost by three votes in April 1850; the tax went in 1851, replaced by a duty on inhabited houses2021. Disraeli called the swap only “a partial commutation”21.

Sources differ. Parliament’s own key-dates page says “four shillings for between 10 and 20 windows”; the Act charges four shillings on top of the two-shilling base, six in all.

§ 6 1735 Great Britain Repealed 1951

Belief and the bodyRepealedLifespan: 1735–1951

The Witchcraft Act

What it did
It reversed the old law. The capital witchcraft statutes of 1604 (England) and 1563 (Scotland) were repealed and prosecutions for witchcraft barred; the new offence was pretending to have magical powers, punished by a year in prison without bail and quarterly hours in the pillory22.
Purpose
To end witch trials while still punishing fortune-tellers and others who claimed powers for gain22.
Enforcement
Its best-known use came two centuries later. The Scottish medium Helen Duncan was tried at the Old Bailey from 23 March 1944 and convicted on 3 April23; she served nine months, and the Court of Criminal Appeal upheld the conviction in R v Duncan [1944] KB 71324. Prosecutors are reported to have chosen the 1735 Act because they feared an acquittal under the Vagrancy Act; Winston Churchill called the case “obsolete tomfoolery”23.
Who it fell on
Spiritualist mediums, fortune-tellers and folk healers claiming supernatural powers.
What became of it
Repealed by the Fraudulent Mediums Act 1951, which made it an offence to fake mediumship for reward25.

Sources differ. Duncan is often called the last person convicted under the Act. National Geographic and the ICLR both describe her as the last person imprisoned under it2324, which is the claim we can support.

§ 7 1870 and 1882 England and Wales Dismantled 1882

Marriage and familyStill in force, amendedLifespan: to 1882

Coverture and the Married Women’s Property Acts

What it did
Coverture was common law rather than statute: on marriage a woman’s personal property passed to her husband and she could not hold property in her own name2728. Wealthy families worked round it with marriage settlements28.
Purpose
Blackstone framed it as protection: a wife acted under her husband’s “wing, protection, and cover”26.
How it was dismantled
After a campaign that included an 1856 petition of about 26,000 signatures28, the 1870 Act let wives keep their own earnings27. The 1882 Act, in force from 1 January 1883, let a married woman hold and dispose of property “as if she were a feme sole”29. Historians note working-class women gained little at first28.
Who it fell on
Married women, and in practice their husbands’ creditors.
What became of it
The 1870 Act was repealed by the 1882 Act; most of the 1882 Act has since gone, but two sections, on life insurance for spouses and on property disputes between them, are still in force29.

Note. Blackstone also wrote that “the husband also (by the old law) might give his wife moderate correction”26. See the rule-of-thumb entry for what that did and did not mean.

§ 8 never England (as claimed) Never law

Marriage and familyLegendLifespan: Never law

The ‘rule of thumb’

The claim
That English law once let a husband beat his wife with a stick no thicker than his thumb, and that this is where the phrase “rule of thumb” comes from.
What the record shows
No such rule of law has been found. The story attaches to the judge Sir Francis Buller, who was “reputedly” said to have ruled it and was mocked for it in James Gillray’s 1782 print Judge Thumb, or, Patent sticks for family correction, warranted lawful30. The legal historian Henry Ansgar Kelly traced the legend in 1994. By a University of Oregon summary of his work, he found that Buller gave some credence to a thumb standard in 1782 (possibly 1781), and that two American cases mentioned one: Bradley v State (Mississippi, 1824) referred to it without relying on it, and in State v Rhodes (North Carolina, 1868) a trial judge used it, reasoning the state Supreme Court rejected31. None of this made it a rule of English law. The phrase itself has long meant a rough practical measure31.
What was real
The doctrine of “moderate correction” was real; Blackstone recorded it and wrote that in the reign of Charles II “this power of correction began to be doubted”26. The thumb was the invention.
§ 9 1799 and 1800 Great Britain Repealed 1824

WorkRepealedLifespan: 1799–1824

The Combination Acts

No quotation. We have not found an authoritative transcription of the 1800 Act to quote, so the summary below relies on reference works.

What it did
Made it a crime for workers to combine “to gain an increase in wages or a decrease in hours”, in effect outlawing trade unions32.
Purpose
Passed under Pitt the Younger, whose government was responding to Jacobin activity and feared wartime strikes33.
Enforcement
The Acts formally covered employers’ combinations too, but Britannica notes those clauses “were never in any recorded case put into operation”32. When the framework-knitters’ leader Gravener Henson tried to use the Act against masters in 1811, magistrates would not act34. No count of prosecutions survives.
Who it fell on
Journeymen and workers in Britain’s growing manufacturing trades.
What became of it
Repealed in 1824 after a campaign by the radical tailor Francis Place and the MP Joseph Hume32. A wave of strikes followed, and an 1825 Act allowed unions to exist while creating new offences of intimidation and molestation34.
§ 10 1800 Paris, France Declared void 2013

Belief and the bodyStruck down or voidedLifespan: 1800–2013

Paris's ban on women in trousers

No quotation. The sentence usually quoted (any woman wishing to dress as a man must obtain authorisation from the Prefecture of Police) is a summary used in a 2011 Senate bill, not the ordinance’s own wording, so we do not present it as such35.

What it did
An ordinance of the Paris police prefect, Louis Nicolas Dubois, dated 16 brumaire an IX (7 November 1800), required women who wanted to dress as men to apply to the Prefecture, with a health officer’s certificate3637.
Purpose
In the French government’s 2013 reading, it was mainly a way of keeping women out of certain jobs and roles36.
Enforcement
No count of permits survives in the sources we found. Marie-Rose Astié de Valsayre asked deputies to repeal it in 1887; the athlete Violette Morris defended her right to wear trousers in a 1930 trial36.
Who it fell on
Women in Paris.
What became of it
It was never formally repealed. On 31 January 2013 the Ministry of Women’s Rights, answering a senator’s written question, declared it incompatible with the equality guarantees of the constitution and the European Convention on Human Rights, “implicitement abrogée” and without legal effect36.

Sources differ. A Senate bill and an Assemblée question date it 26 brumaire an IX (17 November 1800) and cite circulars of 1892 and 1909 allowing trousers for women holding a bicycle’s handlebars or a horse’s reins3537. The historian Christine Bard gives 16 brumaire and calls the bicycle exemption “de la légende urbaine”36.

§ 11 1814 Norway Struck out 1851

Belief and the bodyRepealedLifespan: 1814–1851

Norway's constitutional ban on Jews

What it did
Written into the new constitution, the article continued an older exclusion (“fremdeles”: still, as before) and banned Jews from entering the kingdom3839.
Enforcement
In the constitution’s first year Jews were expelled or persecuted in Østfold, Christiania and Bergen; the last known case, in 1844, ended in fines, prison and expulsion39. There were quiet exceptions: Sephardic Jews had been allowed to trade since 1750, and Jewish bankers were let into the capital in 1822 to negotiate state loans39.
Who it fell on
Jews, and through the second clause Jesuits and Catholic religious orders.
What became of it
The poet Henrik Wergeland campaigned for repeal from the late 1830s but died in 184540. Majorities in 1842, 1845 and 1848 fell short of two-thirds; on 13 June 1851 the Storting voted 93–10 to strike the clause3941. The ban on monastic orders went in 1897, and the Jesuit ban only on 1 November 1956, by 111 votes to 3142.

Sources differ. The 1851 change can be dated 13 June (the vote), 21 July (royal sanction) or 24 September (the law lifting the entry ban)3941.

§ 12 1823 England and Wales Repealed 1875

WorkRepealedLifespan: 1823–1875

The Master and Servant Act

No quotation. We have not found an authoritative transcription of the 1823 Act; its replacement’s text is quoted under “What became of it”.

What it did
Under 4 Geo. 4 c. 34 a worker who broke a contract of service, by leaving early or refusing work, committed a criminal offence; an employer’s breach was only a civil matter4344. Workers faced up to three months’ hard labour43.
Purpose
It codified the use of penal sanctions against workers for breach of contract44.
Enforcement
Heavy and routine. Official judicial statistics record more than 10,000 Master and Servant prosecutions a year between 1858 and 1875, and 14,353 in 1875, more than for petty larceny43. Hard labour could mean the treadmill or the crank43.
Who it fell on
Servants, labourers and artisans under contract.
What became of it
An 1867 Act made fines the usual penalty. From 1 September 1875 the Conspiracy and Protection of Property Act repealed the 1867 Act and the older master-and-servant enactments scheduled to it45, and the Employers and Workmen Act made breach a civil dispute between equals: “A dispute under this Act between an employer and a workman may be heard and determined by a court of summary jurisdiction”46.
§ 13 1865 United Kingdom Superseded 1896

Public lifeRepealedLifespan: 1865–1896

The Red Flag Act

What it did
Every road locomotive needed at least three people to work it, one walking sixty yards ahead with a red flag. The limit was four miles an hour in the country and two through any city, town or village; the whistle was not to be sounded “for any Purpose whatever”47.
Purpose
To protect horse traffic from steam traction engines48. It was passed as a temporary measure, due to lapse in 186747.
Enforcement
We found no documented prosecution figures.
Who it fell on
Owners of traction engines and steam carriages, and later the first motorists.
What became of it
The flag went first: an 1878 Act replaced it with a walker at least twenty yards ahead, with no mention of a flag4950. The Locomotives on Highways Act 1896, in force on 14 November 1896, freed vehicles under three tons unladen and set a 14 mph limit51. Motorists celebrated with an “Emancipation Run” from London to Brighton, at whose start Lord Winchilsea destroyed a red flag; the run is commemorated by an annual veteran car run50.

Sources differ. The Veteran Car Club says the 14 mph limit was cut to 12 mph before it took effect50; Wikipedia says local authorities could reduce it to 1251.

§ 14 1873 United States In force, amended

Belief and the bodyStill in force, amendedLifespan: 1873–today

The Comstock Act

What it did
Signed by President Grant on 3 March 1873, the Act for the Suppression of Trade in, and Circulation of, Obscene Literature and Articles of Immoral Use banned mailing obscene matter and anything for preventing conception or procuring abortion5354.
Enforcement
Anthony Comstock was made a special agent of the Post Office days after it passed54. Late in life he claimed 4,000 arrests and 150 tons of books destroyed; those figures are his own53. Margaret Sanger fled abroad in 1914 to avoid prosecution; in 1915 an agent tricked her husband William into handing over her pamphlet Family Limitation, and he was jailed54, for 30 days53.
Who it fell on
Publishers, booksellers, artists and sex educators, and above all women seeking contraception and those supplying it.
What became of it
In 1971 Congress struck the words “preventing conception or” from the mailing ban, but the abortion and obscenity clauses remain in the US Code as of November 202652. A 2022 Justice Department legal opinion concluded the law does not stop the mailing of abortion pills where the sender lacks intent that they be used unlawfully55; in August 2026 the department was reported to be reviewing that reading56.
§ 15 1881 Chicago, United States Repealed 1973 or 1974

Public lifeRepealedLifespan: 1881–1974

Chicago's ‘unsightly’ ordinance

What it did
Fined disabled and disfigured people for appearing in public. Chicago’s was one of several American “ugly laws”5758.
Purpose
It passed with little debate after years of hostile press coverage of disabled beggars; the historian Adrienne Phelps Coco argues its real target was begging and poverty as much as impairment59.
Enforcement
No Chicago arrest figures survive in the sources we found. The last documented arrest under any such law, in Omaha in 1974, was of a homeless man with “marks and scars on his body”; the prosecutor declined to charge but said “the law is still active”57.
Who it fell on
Disabled and disfigured people, especially those begging59.
What became of it
Repealed by the city council in the early 1970s5958.

Sources differ. JSTOR Daily gives the repeal as 197359; Wikipedia and many popular accounts give 197458. We have not found the council record.

§ 16 1894 onwards United Kingdom and Ireland Lifted 1946 to 1973

WorkRepealedLifespan: 1894–1946

The marriage bar

What it did
Women civil servants had to resign when they married, and married women were kept out of established posts. In Britain it was an administrative rule, traced by Foreign Office historians to an 1894 Treasury minute on typists and endorsed by the 1934 Schuster Report61. Ireland wrote it into statute, most recently as section 10 of the Civil Service Regulation Act 195662.
Purpose
Treasury papers of 1947 called married women “a perfect nuisance” and doubted “more than 50% attendance out of them”63.
Enforcement
Of 18 women recruited to the Foreign Service between 1946 and 1952, seven had left by 1954, “largely because of the marriage bar”; Margaret Anstee, recruited in 1948, resigned on marriage in 195261.
Who it fell on
Women in the civil service, and similar bars applied in teaching and at the BBC until 194463.
What became of it
Lifted for Britain’s home civil service in 1946 and for the Diplomatic Service in 19726361. Ireland repealed its statutory bar on 31 July 19736462.

Sources differ. The Diplomatic Service date is often given as 1973; the Foreign Office’s own historians say 197261.

§ 17 1897 Indiana, United States Shelved 1897

Public lifeNever enactedLifespan: 1897, never enacted

Indiana's Pi Bill

What it proposed
A local doctor, Edwin J. Goodwin, believed he had squared the circle; his bill would have given Indiana schools his “discovery” free while other states paid royalties6667. Its arithmetic implied a value of pi of 3.267.
How far it got
It was sent first to the Committee on Canals, then to Education, and passed the House 67–065. In the Senate the Committee on Temperance recommended it; the same day, 12 February 1897, the Senate postponed it indefinitely65. Senator Hubbell was reported as saying the Senate “might as well try to legislate water to run up hill as to establish mathematical truth by law”65.
Who stopped it
C. A. Waldo, a Purdue mathematician who happened to be at the State House lobbying for his university’s budget. “That evening the senators were properly coached,” he recalled some twenty years later6567.
What became of it
Never enacted6566.

Sources differ. The Indiana State Library dates the House vote 6 February 189766; Edington, working from the journals, gives 5 February65. The Indiana Archives call the author Edward Johnston Goodwin67; Edington and the State Library give Edwin.

§ 18 1907 United States Repealed 1922

Marriage and familyRepealedLifespan: 1907–1922

Marrying out of US citizenship

What it did
An American woman who married a foreign national lost her citizenship, whether or not the couple lived in the United States69.
Enforcement
In Mackenzie v. Hare (decided 6 December 1915), the Supreme Court upheld the law against a US-born Californian who had married a foreign national and still lived in the US, holding, as the syllabus puts it, that such a marriage was “tantamount to voluntary expatriation”70. Women who lost citizenship could lose jobs reserved for citizens and face detention or deportation68.
Who it fell on
Women who married non-citizens; after 1922, especially Asian American women68.
What became of it
The Cable Act of 22 September 1922 ended the rule for most marriages but kept it for women who married men “ineligible for citizenship”, in practice Asian men6968. That exception went in 1931; from 2 July 1940 any woman who had lost citizenship through marriage could regain it by taking an oath6869.
§ 19 1906 Canada Struck down 1985

Drink and SundaysStruck down or voidedLifespan: 1907–1985

Canada's Lord's Day Act

What it did
Passed in July 1906 and in force from March 1907, the federal Act restricted trade, work and paid recreation on Sundays7273.
Purpose
The Lord’s Day Alliance persuaded Wilfrid Laurier to introduce it; organised labour backed it as a guaranteed weekly day off73. The Supreme Court later found its purpose was compulsory religious observance74.
Enforcement
Each prosecution needed provincial consent, which made the Act hard to use73. In 1937 a Toronto veteran, Captain Archibald Pither, convicted of buying tobacco on a Sunday, chose a day in jail over a $2 fine: “Overseas I had to fight as hard on Sundays as any other day”72.
Who it fell on
Shopkeepers, workers and Sunday newspaper sellers, and anyone whose Sabbath was not Sunday74.
What became of it
After Calgary police charged Big M Drug Mart over Sunday trading in 1982, the Supreme Court ruled on 24 April 198575 that the Act infringed freedom of conscience and religion under the Charter7674. It was never repealed, simply left out of the 1985 consolidation of federal statutes and listed as “unconstitutional”77.
§ 20 1914 to 1919 Sweden Ended 1955

Drink and SundaysRepealedLifespan: 1914–1955

Sweden's ration book for spirits

What it did
Under the system devised by the physician Ivan Bratt, every purchase of spirits was entered in a personal book, the motbok. It started in Stockholm in 1914 and covered the whole country, under state control, by 191979. A typical entitlement for an eligible man was three litres of spirits a month79.
Purpose
To cut consumption without outright prohibition, which Swedes narrowly rejected in a 1922 referendum80.
Enforcement
Books went to men over 25 with a fixed address and no sign of serious drinking problems; local boards could cut the ration for the unemployed, people with criminal records or those behind on their taxes79. At the end of 1948 the average purchase was 1.82 litres a month80.
Who it fell on
Everyone who drank, unequally: married women normally could not hold a book of their own, and unmarried women got reduced rations after individual assessment79.
What became of it
In 1954 the government proposed abolition, with new rules from 1 October 195578; the motbok ended that year79.
§ 21 1914 and 1921 United Kingdom (England and Wales) Ended 2005

Drink and SundaysRepealedLifespan: 1914–2005

Britain's afternoon closing

What it did
The Intoxicating Liquor (Temporary Restriction) Act, given royal assent on 31 August 1914, let licensing justices cut pub hours on police advice8283. From 1915 a Central Control Board, set up under the Defence of the Realm legislation, went further in munitions and military areas83. The Licensing Act 1921 made the wartime pattern permanent, with fixed permitted hours, variations between London and the country, and a compulsory afternoon break81.
Purpose
“The maintenance of order, or the suppression of drunkenness” among war workers83.
Enforcement
Within four months restrictive orders covered 427 of the 1,000 licensing districts in England and Wales83. A 1915 “No Treating” order made buying a round an offence84. In Carlisle, near the Gretna munitions works, the state took over the pubs and breweries from 1916, and kept many of them until 197184. London convictions for drunkenness fell from 67,103 in 1914 to 16,567 in 191784.
Who it fell on
Drinkers, licensees and, in wartime, munitions workers.
What became of it
Hours were relaxed in stages from the late 1980s; fixed permitted hours ended in England and Wales on 24 November 2005, when the Licensing Act 2003 took full effect8584.

Sources differ. Many accounts credit the Defence of the Realm Act itself with the 1914 limits84; Hansard and Britannica put them in the separate 1914 Act8283.

§ 22 1915 Iceland Ended 1989

Drink and SundaysRepealedLifespan: 1915–1989

Iceland's beer ban

No quotation. We have not found the Icelandic statute text in a primary source, so there is no quotation here.

What it did
After a 1908 referendum, Iceland banned alcohol from 1 January 191586. Spain’s refusal to buy Icelandic fish unless Iceland bought Spanish wine forced the first exception; spirits came back after a 1933 referendum. Beer stronger than 2.25 per cent stayed illegal8687.
Purpose
Temperance, with a nationalist edge. The historian Stefán Pálsson links beer with the Danish lifestyle Icelanders were then trying to shed88.
Enforcement
Home brewing and smuggling were widespread86. In 1985 a minister banned bars from mixing spirits into legal low-alcohol pilsner to fake the real thing86.
Who it fell on
Drinkers and bars.
What became of it
The beer ban ended on 1 March 1989; AFP reported that more than 340,000 cans were bought on the first day87. Retail sales remain a state monopoly87.

Sources differ. Wikipedia dates the Spanish pressure to 1921 and legal wine to 192286; some accounts give 1921 for both.

§ 23 1935 Ireland Repealed 1979

Marriage and familyRepealedLifespan: 1935–1979

Ireland's ban on contraceptives

What it did
Banned the sale and import of contraceptives, defined as “any appliance, instrument, drug, preparation or thing, designed, prepared, or intended to prevent pregnancy”89. Offenders faced a fine of up to £50, six months in prison, or both90. Using contraception was not itself an offence91.
Enforcement
Customs enforced the import ban. They seized spermicidal jelly that Mary McGee, a 27-year-old mother of four for whom another pregnancy was dangerous, had ordered from England91.
Who it fell on
Everyone in Ireland seeking contraception, and above all women for whom pregnancy carried medical risk91.
What became of it
On 19 December 1973 the Supreme Court ruled 4–1 in McGee v Attorney General that the import ban was unconstitutional91. The Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 repealed section 17 but allowed contraceptives only “bona fide” for family planning92; condoms and spermicides could be sold to over-18s through pharmacies, doctors and family-planning services from 1985 and were dropped from the legal definition of a contraceptive in 19939394.
§ 24 1945 and 1952 South Africa Repealed 1986

MovementRepealedLifespan: 1945–1986

South Africa's pass laws

No quotation. We have not found authoritative transcriptions of section 10 of the 1945 Act or of the 1952 Act to quote; the repealing Act’s long title is quoted under “What became of it”.

What it did
Pass laws, which Britannica defines as laws requiring non-white South Africans “to carry documents authorizing their presence in restricted areas”95, were older than apartheid. Section 10 of the Natives (Urban Areas) Consolidation Act 1945, as amended in 1952, let a Black person stay in a town for no more than 72 hours unless they qualified by birth and residence there, or by long continuous work or lawful residence96. The misleadingly named Natives (Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents) Act 1952 replaced local passes with a single reference book recording employment and fingerprints95, under a 1952 Act97.
Purpose
“Influx control”: keeping Black South Africans out of urban areas reserved for whites except as needed for labour9596.
Enforcement
Vast. The sociologist Michael Savage counted more than 17,745,000 Africans arrested or prosecuted under pass laws between 1916 and 198496; Britannica says the total passed 17 million95. At Sharpeville on 21 March 1960 police fired on a crowd protesting the passes, killing 69 people96.
Who it fell on
Black South Africans, and especially migrant workers95.
What became of it
The Abolition of Influx Control Act, “to repeal the laws relating to influx control in respect of Black persons”, came into force on 1 July 1986 and repealed the whole 1945 Act98. The 1952 Act was repealed by the Identification Act, No. 72 of 198699.

Sources differ. Wikipedia says the system was repealed “retroactive on April 23, 1986”96; the Government Gazette gives assent on 24 June and commencement on 1 July 198698. We follow the Gazette.

§ 25 1948 Japan Dance clause cut 2016

Public lifeStill in force, amendedLifespan: 1948–today

Japan's no-dancing law

What it did
The Act on Control and Improvement of Amusement Business (Act No. 122 of 1948), passed during the American occupation, treated venues that let customers dance as regulated amusement businesses, subject to its licensing and business-hours rules101102103.
Purpose
To regulate the hours and locations of amusement businesses, keep minors out and protect public morals101.
Enforcement
Largely ignored for decades, it was revived around 2010 after a brawl at an Osaka club103. Police arrested Masatoshi Kanemitsu, owner of the Osaka club NOON, in April 2012. He was acquitted, the Osaka High Court upheld the acquittal in January 2015, and the Supreme Court dismissed the prosecutors’ final appeal in June 2016104105.
Who it fell on
Club owners, DJs and dancers.
What became of it
The 2015 amendment, in force from 23 June 2016, removed dancing from the regulated categories and created a new licence for late-night venues serving alcohol102100. The 1948 Act itself remains in force.
§ 26 1958 People's Republic of China In force, reformed

MovementStill in force, amendedLifespan: 1958–today

China's household registration

What it did
Adopted by the Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress on 9 January 1958, the regulations registered every household as rural or urban and made moving to a city conditional on official permission106.
Purpose
Article 1 cites social order, citizens’ rights and “socialist construction”106. In practice, until the 1980s rural residents could barely get non-farm work in cities107.
Enforcement
Hundreds of millions of workers migrated from the countryside in the three decades to 2014 without urban registration107.
Who it fell on
Rural-registered citizens and internal migrants.
What became of it
Reformed, not repealed. In July 2014 the State Council announced reforms to relax registration limits, under which, nominally, the rural and urban labels would go108107, and a 2024 plan relaxed registration limits “except in certain megacities”109. We have found no repeal of the 1958 regulations as of November 2026.
