Annotated register · 15 entries · 1916–2011

The Typeface Register

Fifteen typefaces that a railway, a ministry, a bank committee or a software company chose for everyone else: who drew each one, what the brief demanded, and where you still read it.

15typefaces
5chosen by a committee or standards body
77sources

Most people never choose the type they read most. A railway manager chose it in 1913, a bankers' committee in 1958, a German highway research institute at the end of the 1970s, a software company in 2007. The letters on a motorway sign, the numbers along the bottom of a cheque and the two lines of chevrons under a passport photo were each fixed by a brief, a test and a decision, often recorded in a regulation or a standards document that almost nobody reads.

This register takes fifteen of those decisions, in order of the year the face was drawn or adopted. Each sheet gives who drew the letters, who chose them, when, what the brief asked for, and where the face is still read. Some of the familiar stories do not survive the paperwork: the New York subway did not start out in Helvetica, the Times face it replaced was never called Times Old Roman, and Britain's road-sign alphabet was drawn against the committee's advice.

Where your device has one of these typefaces installed, the sheet shows a line set in it. Johnston, DIN 1451, Highway Gothic, E-13B, Transport and FE-Schrift are not normally installed on phones or computers, so those sheets are text only.

Reg.TR-01

Wayfinding · 1916

Johnston

Every sign, map and poster on Transport for London's network

Drawn by
Edward Johnston, calligrapher, helped by his former pupil Eric Gill 2,4
Chosen by
Frank Pick, for the London Underground 1,2
Adopted
Commissioned 1913; capitals finished June 1916 2
Brief
Lettering for posters and notices that busy passengers could read across a crowded platform 3

Johnston was not a type-cutter. TfL's own archive describes him as a calligrapher who drew the whole alphabet by hand, delivering the capitals in June 1916 and the lower case and numerals the following month; a complete set was not run off by an Underground printer until June 1917, when the letters began appearing on posters and notices 2. Eric Gill, his friend, neighbour and former pupil, assisted, and was paid ten per cent of the fee 4.

Sources disagree on when the work began. TfL and its archive date the commission to 1913 1,2; the Design Museum gives 1916 3. What is not in doubt is that the face outlived the company that ordered it. After a review by typographic consultants in 1979, Eiichi Kono redrew it between 1979 and 1983 as New Johnston 2.

For the centenary in 2016 Monotype drew Johnston100 for TfL, adding the # and @ that a 1916 alphabet never needed and new hairline and thin weights for screens 1,5. TfL says the face now spells out words across its entire network 1. The Tube alone carried an estimated 1,216 million journeys in 2024/25, a provisional figure 6.

Reg.TR-02

Page and document · 1928

Gill Sans

LNER timetables and posters, British Railways stations, Penguin covers

Drawn by
Eric Gill, for the Monotype Corporation 7,8
Chosen by
Stanley Morison, Monotype's typographic adviser; then the London and North Eastern Railway 7,9
Adopted
Released by Monotype 1928; LNER standard by the early 1930s 8,9
Brief
A commercial sans serif built on the capitals Gill had painted on a Bristol bookshop 7

LONDON AND NORTH EASTERN RAILWAY · Flying Scotsman

The face began as a shop sign. Gill painted sans-serif capitals on the fascia of Douglas Cleverdon's bookshop in Bristol, and Morison, who saw them, ordered a typeface based on those letters 7. Monotype issued it in 1928, and for its first decades recommended it for advertising and display rather than running text 7.

Its biggest early customer was a railway. The London and North Eastern Railway made Gill Sans the type of its posters, timetables and publicity; the Science Museum Group holds a 1934 booklet, An Account of the LNER Type Standardization, printed in it 9, and a replica of the Flying Scotsman headboard, credited to Gill and dated to the train on 21 November 1932 10. Penguin used it on its covers from 1935 11.

Reg.TR-03

Page and document · 1932

Times New Roman

The Times from 1932; Windows; Microsoft Word's default until 2007; US diplomatic paper

Drawn by
Victor Lardent of The Times's advertising department, under Stanley Morison, with Monotype's drawing office 12,13
Chosen by
The Times, on a memorandum from Morison considered by a committee of the paper 13
Adopted
First issue set in it 3 October 1932 12
Brief
A sturdier, more economical text face to replace the paper's spindly 19th-century type 12,15

The Times, Monday 3 October 1932

It started with an insult. In 1929 The Times asked Monotype to buy advertising space, and Morison replied that he would rather pay the paper £1,000 to keep its hands off a Monotype advertisement, because its printing was so poor and its type so dated. The paper hired him as typographic adviser, and in October 1930 he proposed an entirely new typeface 13.

Lardent's drawings are lost, and the credit is more shared than the usual two names suggest. Dan Rhatigan, a former Monotype type director, argues that much of the work belongs to the company's drawing office 13. The German National Library's summary of the brief is the one Morison set: clear, legible and economical with space 12. The paper appeared in it on 3 October 1932, and the paper kept the face to itself for a year 12.

The Times itself moved on to Times Europa in 1972 15, but by then the face belonged to everyone. It shipped with Windows from version 3.1 64 and was the default in Word until Calibri replaced it in 2007 69. In December 2025 the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, ordered 14-point Times New Roman back onto all department paper, reversing a 2023 switch to Calibri 29,32.

On the record

There was no face called Times Old Roman. The type The Times used before 1932 was a Monotype modern, and the phrase came later, from a 1932 Monotype Recorder cover 14.

Reg.TR-04

Wayfinding · 1936

DIN 1451

German road signs, house numbers, postmarks and, until 2000, number plates

Drawn by
No single designer: a committee chaired by Ludwig Goller, a Siemens engineer 16,17
Chosen by
The German standards committee, today the Deutsches Institut für Normung 16,18
Adopted
Issued as a standard in 1936; a preliminary version dates from 1931 16,19
Brief
“A common, easily and clearly legible type” for technology and transport 16

DIN 1451 is a typeface written as a technical standard. Its letters derive from lettering used by the Prussian state railways early in the century, and the committee that turned it into a norm was chaired by Ludwig Goller, who led the standards body's work on technical drawing and signage from 1925 16,17,18. The German National Library gives the brief as a type whose letters could be told apart at a distance 16. Goller's employer, Siemens, put it in its own logo in 1936 17.

Fonts in Use dates the first definition to 1931 and the confirmed standard to 1936 19; the library and the foundry Fontwerk give 1936 16,18. Adolf Gropp revised the road-sign version for the Federal Highway Research Institute; Fontwerk dates the revision to 1981, Fonts in Use to 1980 18,19. It stayed on German number plates until FE-Schrift (TR-12) completely replaced it in 2000 65.

Because it was a public standard rather than a product, foundries could draw their own versions. FontShop's FF DIN came out in 1995 and carried the engineering alphabet into corporate branding 19.

Reg.TR-05

Wayfinding · Late 1940s

Highway Gothic

US interstate and highway signs; also airfield signs and school buses

Drawn by
Usually credited to the engineer Theodore W. (Ted) Forbes 25,26; FHWA's own reports name no designer
Chosen by
The federal roads agency, now the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) 20
Adopted
Published as the Standard Alphabets in the late 1940s; sources give 1948 26 and 1949 25
Brief
One national set of letters that sign shops could reproduce by hand 20

Its real name is the Standard Alphabets for Highway Signs; “Highway Gothic” is a nickname 26. The alphabets come in a range of widths, series B to F plus the wider E(Modified), and FHWA notes that the narrowest, series A, was dropped as unworkable in manual fabrication 20. The federal sign manual requires E(Modified) on freeway and expressway guide signs, and the alphabets also turn up in specifications for airfield signs and vehicles such as school buses 20.

The face's one serious challenger came from a design firm. Clearview, by Donald Meeker, Chris O'Hara and James Montalbano, was tested in Pennsylvania in the 1990s 23,24, and FHWA granted it interim approval on 2 September 2004 20,22. In January 2016 FHWA withdrew that approval, citing “significant confusion and inconsistency” in sign design and fabrication and the font's “poorer performance in certain applications” 21. Congress then forced a partial climbdown: in March 2018 FHWA reinstated Clearview only for agencies already approved, and said it made no recommendation or endorsement 22. FHWA counts 26 states that had received approval 21.

The plain government alphabet has had a second life in design. Tobias Frere-Jones's Interstate, drawn for Font Bureau in the mid-1990s and now in MoMA's collection, is built on it 25.

Reg.TR-06

Page and document · 1955

Courier

Typewriters, screenplays, and US diplomatic paper until 2004

Drawn by
Howard “Bud” Kettler, IBM type designer in Poughkeepsie 27
Chosen by
IBM, for its typewriters 27
Adopted
1955 27
Brief
A monospaced typewriter face, later adapted to the Selectric type ball 27

INT. WRITERS' ROOM - NIGHT. Page one, minute one.

Kettler designed Courier in 1955 for IBM's type-bar typewriters, and it was later adapted to the golf-ball Selectric 27. According to the screenwriter John August, IBM never sought exclusive rights in the design, which is why it could spread across the industry and remains royalty-free 28.

Film is where Courier became law in practice. Screenplays are expected in 12-point Courier because a fixed face at a fixed size makes a page roughly a minute of screen time, so producers can compare drafts by page count 28. When August commissioned a better-looking replacement, Courier Prime, released in January 2013, it kept Courier's metrics exactly so that page counts would not change 28.

Courier New was the US State Department's type until 2004, according to Axios and The Washington Times, when the department moved to Times New Roman 29,33.

Reg.TR-07

Wayfinding · 1957

Helvetica

New York City subway signs, officially since 1989

Drawn by
Max Miedinger, commissioned by Eduard Hoffmann of the Haas foundry, Münchenstein, as Neue Haas Grotesk 34
Chosen by
For the subway: the New York City Transit Authority, after Unimark's 1970 standards; made official by the MTA's 1989 sign manual 36
Adopted
Face finished 1957 34; official subway face December 1989 36
Brief
For Haas: a sans serif to compete with a popular rival 34. For the subway: legibility from any angle 36

Uptown & The Bronx · 59 St–Columbus Circle

Hoffmann commissioned Miedinger in autumn 1956, and the design was finished in summer 1957 as Neue Haas Grotesk 34. Stempel, Haas's parent company in Frankfurt, felt it could not sell a face under another foundry's name. Paul Shaw records that a Stempel manager, Heinz Eul, suggested “Helvetia”, the Latin name for Switzerland, and Hoffmann changed it to Helvetica 35.

The subway story is where most retellings go wrong. The Transit Authority hired Unimark International in 1966 on MoMA's recommendation, with Bob Noorda leading the sign study, but Unimark's 1970 Graphics Standards Manual specified Standard Medium, the American name for Akzidenz-Grotesk, as offering “the easiest legibility from any angle” 36. Helvetica first appears officially in a 1980 revision, signs in it went up at the 63rd Street stations in October 1989, and a sign manual by Michael Hertz Associates made Helvetica Medium the official face that December 36.

Shaw notes that to most people the two faces look indistinguishable, which is how the myth took hold 36. He has since expanded the research into a book for MIT Press 37.

On the record

Massimo Vignelli later said the black signs were chosen because of graffiti. Shaw found that legibility tests began in 1972–73 and that Vignelli was not involved in that decision 36.

Reg.TR-08

Machine-read · 1958

E-13B

The account line along the bottom of cheques in North America and much of the world

Drawn by
Developed for magnetic reading in the Bank of America programme that produced ERMA, with Stanford Research Institute and General Electric 39; no individual designer is credited
Chosen by
The American Bankers Association, on the unanimous recommendation of its equipment manufacturers' committee 38
Adopted
December 1958; ABA Publication 147 issued April 1959 38
Brief
Ten digits and four symbols that a machine could read magnetically, at speed, through stamps and smudges 38,40

The American Bankers Association began work on machine-readable cheques in 1954. In November 1958 the committee of office-equipment makers it had convened unanimously recommended E-13B, and the ABA adopted it the next month, publishing the specification in April 1959 38. The machines that would read it came out of Bank of America's ERMA project, built first by Stanford Research Institute and then re-engineered by General Electric. When ERMA was shown off in September 1959, GE's spokesman was Ronald Reagan, and the system read ten cheques a second 39.

The characters are odd-looking for a reason: they are read by the magnetic signal of the ink, not by their outline, which is why the set is only ten numerals and four symbols 38,40. ISO still maintains the print specification 40. France went a different way with CMC-7, a set built from vertical strokes, standardised by AFNOR in 1964 41.

Cheques are declining but nowhere near gone. The Federal Reserve counted 9.2 billion cheque payments in the US in 2024, down from 17.0 billion in 2015 42.

Reg.TR-09

Wayfinding · 1965

Transport

Road signs across the United Kingdom; a variant on GOV.UK

Drawn by
Jock Kinneir and Margaret Calvert 43,44
Chosen by
The Anderson Committee on motorway signs, then the Worboys Committee on all other roads, for the Ministry of Transport 43,46
Adopted
In law from 1 January 1965 47
Brief
Signs a driver can read at speed; the committee asked for no new letterform at all 44

The Anderson Committee, set up in 1957 under Sir Colin Anderson, chairman of P&O, needed signs for Britain's first motorway, and Kinneir's trial signs went up on the Preston bypass, opened in 1958 43,46. Calvert has said the committee told Kinneir not to create a new alphabet, arguing that the German autobahn lettering would do; the pair ignored it 44. The result borrowed from Akzidenz-Grotesk and took the curled tail of its lower-case l from Johnston 44,45. The Worboys Committee then extended the system to every other road, and the Traffic Signs Regulations made it law from 1 January 1965 43,47.

It did not go unopposed. The letter-cutter David Kindersley argued in The Times and on the BBC for an all-capitals serif face. The Road Research Laboratory found his letters had a 3% legibility advantage, but only with very tight spacing, and the advantage vanished on Anderson-style layouts 46.

Calvert also drew the pictograms. The girl leading a boy on the school-crossing sign was based on photographs of herself as a child, and the cow on the farm-animal warning was based on Patience, from a relative's farm 45,48,49. “Style never came into it,” she told Design Week 45. A redrawn New Transport is the typeface of GOV.UK 48.

Reg.TR-10

Machine-read · 1968

OCR-B

The two machine-readable lines at the foot of every passport photo page

Drawn by
Adrian Frutiger 50,51
Chosen by
The European Computer Manufacturers Association; later ISO and the International Civil Aviation Organization 50,52,53
Adopted
Designed 1968 51; ISO recommendation 1969, standard 1976 52; ICAO passports from 1980 54
Brief
Characters a scanner can read that people can also read comfortably 50,51

P<GBRSPECIMEN<<TYPE<REGISTER<<<<<

OCR-A, the American optical-recognition alphabet of 1966, worked for machines and was hard on people. The European Computer Manufacturers Association commissioned Frutiger to rework it, and his OCR-B of 1968 was made to be scanned by machines and read by people 50,51. ISO first recommended it in 1969 and fixed it as ISO 1073-2 in 1976 52.

The passport is where most people meet it. ICAO's work on machine-readable travel documents began in 1968, and the first edition of Doc 9303 in 1980 was the basis for the first machine-readable passports, from Australia, Canada and the United States 54. The current standard is blunt: “Machine readable data shall be printed in OCR-B type font, size 1, constant stroke width characters” 53.

ICAO's key directory says more than a billion electronic passports are in circulation 55. That counts only chipped passports; every passport made to the standard carries the OCR-B zone.

Reg.TR-11

Wayfinding · 1975

Frutiger

Charles de Gaulle Airport; the NHS; Swiss road signs in a variant

Drawn by
Adrian Frutiger 56,58
Chosen by
Commissioned for the signs at the new Paris airport at Roissy 58
Adopted
Commission 1968; signs installed 1975 58
Brief
Univers's clarity, softened with organic, almost calligraphic nuances, legible at a distance 56,58

Terminal 1 · Arrivées · Correspondances

Frutiger was asked in 1968 for the signs of the airport being built at Roissy, which opened in March 1974; the signs were completed in 1975 58,59. His Guardian obituary describes him starting by cutting test letters from paper with scissors and pasting them onto yellow foil 57.

It became a typeface for everyone only after it had worked on signs. Sources differ on the release: the Guardian says Linotype issued it the following year, 1976 57; Monotype's own history says converting it to phototypesetting took two more years 58.

It now carries health care in England, where the NHS identity says its fonts are Frutiger and Arial and that only these can be used 60. Swiss road signs have used a version, ASTRA-Frutiger, since 2003 56,61.

Reg.TR-12

Machine-read · 1980

FE-Schrift

German number plates, and plates in a number of other countries

Drawn by
Karlgeorg Hoefer 65
Chosen by
The Federal Highway Research Institute (BASt) 65
Adopted
Designed 1978–80; on plates from 1994, sole plate face from 2000 65
Brief
Letters that are hard to alter and easy for machines to read 65

The name is the brief: fälschungserschwerende Schrift, the typeface that makes forgery harder. DIN 1451's geometry had a weakness that Erik Spiekermann describes plainly: with tape or paint an E became an F and a 3 became an 8 66. Hoefer's answer, according to the German National Library, was an alphabet in which every letter has its own form instead of being built from the parts of others, so that none can be turned into another 65.

It went onto plates in 1994 and replaced DIN 1451 completely in 2000 after a transition 65. It is one of the few typefaces read as much by cameras as by people.

Designers have never loved it. PRINT quoted Spiekermann's verdict that while nobody could turn one letter into another, now they all look forged in the first place 67.

Reg.TR-13

Screen · 1982

Arial

Windows since 3.1; the second NHS font; countless forms and office documents

Drawn by
Robin Nicholas and Patricia Saunders, Monotype 62
Chosen by
Monotype, on a contract for IBM laser printers; then Microsoft, for Windows 62,63
Adopted
Drawn 1982 62; bundled with Windows 3.1, April 1992 64
Brief
A sans serif with exactly Helvetica's character widths, so documents could swap between them without reflowing 63

Arial matches Helvetica's widths, letter for letter.

Monotype won the contract to supply fonts for IBM's first big laser printers, and Arial was drawn on the company's own Monotype Grotesque, adjusted to match Helvetica's proportions 62,63. Because the widths matched, software could substitute one for the other automatically 63.

The decision that made it ubiquitous was Microsoft's. When Windows 3.1 made TrueType its font format, Microsoft went with Arial rather than license Helvetica; Apple, Mark Simonson notes, chose Helvetica and paid Linotype 63. Windows 3.1 shipped on 6 April 1992 with Arial among its fonts 64.

Simonson's 2001 essay “The Scourge of Arial” called it “little more than a shameless impostor”, while conceding that on a screen it might as well be Helvetica 63.

On the record

Arial is often called a copy of Helvetica. It copies Helvetica's widths, but its letterforms come from Monotype Grotesque 62,63.

Reg.TR-14

Page and document · 2007

Calibri

Microsoft Office's default from 2007 to 2023; US State Department 2023–25

Drawn by
Lucas de Groot, for Microsoft's ClearType Font Collection 68
Chosen by
Microsoft 68,69
Adopted
Design began 2002; default from 2007 68,69
Brief
A sans serif that would take advantage of ClearType rendering on screens 68

Calibri was Office's default from 2007 until Aptos.

Calibri came out of a set of system fonts Microsoft began commissioning in 2002 to exploit ClearType rendering 68. De Groot's own account is that he submitted a version with rounded stroke ends while warning Microsoft that they looked ugly in ClearType and not to choose it; Microsoft chose it, and an improved rasteriser later rendered the rounded tops smoothly 68. Microsoft says it has been the default “for all things Microsoft” since 2007, replacing Times New Roman 69.

In April 2021 Microsoft unveiled five commissioned candidates to succeed it: Tenorite, Bierstadt, Skeena, Seaford and Grandview 69. Bierstadt, by Steve Matteson, won and was renamed Aptos, after Matteson's favourite unincorporated town in Santa Cruz, California; Microsoft says it reaches hundreds of millions of users 70. The US State Department adopted Calibri in January 2023, citing accessibility 30, and dropped it in December 2025 29,31.

Calibri also had a part in Pakistani politics. In 2017 a joint investigation team concluded that a trust declaration dated February 2006 linked to Maryam Nawaz was fabricated, relying on a forensic finding that Calibri was not commercially available before 31 January 2007 71. De Groot told AFP that beta versions existed from 2004 but were hard to get 72. In July 2018 an accountability court convicted her in the Avenfield case, a verdict the Islamabad High Court set aside on 29 September 2022 73.

Reg.TR-15

Screen · 2011

Roboto

Android's system typeface; Google's Material Design

Drawn by
Christian Robertson, Google 74,75
Chosen by
Google 74
Adopted
Android 4.0 Ice Cream Sandwich, 2011; redrawn 2014 74,75
Brief
One family for every screen, “from watches to desktops, televisions to cars” 75

Android 4.0 · Ice Cream Sandwich · 2011

Roboto shipped in 2011 with Android 4.0, under an open-source licence 74. Its reception among type critics was poor; Typographica's review, as quoted by John Gruber, asked whether it was “a four-headed Frankenstein” 76.

Google redrew it for Material Design. Robertson's 2014 post says almost every glyph was tweaked: R and K redrawn, dots and punctuation rounded, O and C rounded out on their sides so the face feels less condensed, and spacing reworked 75. In May 2015 Google turned it into a full open-source project, and the glyph count rose from about 13,000 to more than 40,000 74.

Apple took the opposite route on its own phones, with San Francisco: drawn first for the small screen of the Apple Watch and moved onto the iPhone and Mac with iOS 9 and OS X El Capitan 77.

How this register was made

Each sheet draws on the commissioning body's own record where one is public (TfL's archive, FHWA reports, ICAO's Doc 9303, the ANSI cheque standard, UK statutory instruments, Microsoft's and Google's announcements), checked against museum collections, the German National Library's type histories and design historians, chiefly Paul Shaw on the New York subway and Dan Rhatigan on Times New Roman. Where sources give different dates, the sheet says so. Usage figures carry the year they describe.

The specimen lines use only fonts already on your device or, for Roboto, Google's own release of it. They are there to show the letters, not as a substitute for the originals, several of which are owned by transport authorities and are not sold.
