Atlas · 30 systems · 24 places · 6 problems

The Everyday Infra­structure Atlas

Thirty ferries, funiculars, trams, walkways, stations and markets that make 24 places easier to live in, with what each one solves, how residents use it and how to use it yourself.

By Nick Carbone · 27 October 2026

Every city has its sights. The things that make a place easy to live in tend to be less photogenic: a boat that leaves every 15 minutes, a lift up a hill, a heated bridge between two office blocks, a market you walk through on the way to the train. Visitors use them once and remember the view. Residents use them twice a day and would notice at once if they stopped.

This atlas collects 30 of them in 24 places, grouped by the problem each one solves. Every entry gives what the system is for, how the people who live there use it, where it came from and how to use it yourself, with a link to the operator or the public body responsible. A final section compares how different cities answered the same problem.

Two rules shaped the list. Each system had to be part of everyday life, carrying commuters, students or shoppers and not only sightseers, and each had to be documented by its operator, a public body or reputable reporting. Superlatives are attributed to whoever makes them.

Figures change, so each one carries its year. Status was checked in October 2026; several of these systems, from Valparaíso's ascensores to Wuppertal's Schwebebahn, close often enough that it is worth checking before you set out.

Six problems

The entries fall into six groups. The numbers are how many of each are in the atlas.

  • 6

    Water in the way

    Where a river, harbour or strait splits the city and bridges are few, far or jammed.

  • 6

    Hills in the way

    Where the people live up a slope and the jobs, shops and port are at the bottom.

  • 4

    Streets to share

    Where there is no room for another road, so transport shares the street or goes over the river.

  • 6

    Walking made easier

    Where weather, gradients or water make the walk or ride the hard part of the trip.

  • 3

    Stations as neighbourhoods

    Where the station becomes the place you park the bike, buy the milk or meet the city.

  • 5

    Markets on the way home

    Where fresh food sits on the commute instead of at the end of a car journey.

01 Ferries and water buses

Şehir Hatları ferries

Istanbul, Turkey since 1851

40 million passengers in 2023 (operator's figure)1

The problem
The Bosphorus splits Istanbul between Europe and Asia. The ferry between Kadıköy or Üsküdar on the Asian shore and Karaköy or Eminönü on the European one has been the water link between the two halves since the 19th century12.
How residents use it
Commuters pay with the İstanbulkart, the same card they use on buses and the metro, tapping it at a turnstile on the pier3. The operator says it carried 40 million passengers in 20231.
How it came about
Şirket-i Hayriye was founded in 1851 to run steam ferries on the Bosphorus, and today's municipal operator traces its line back further, to a state ferry administration of 1844. The Şehir Hatları name dates from 1937; Şirket-i Hayriye was nationalised in 1945 and the service passed to the Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality in 2005, by the operator's account2.
Use it yourself
Karaköy to Kadıköy is the classic commuter crossing, about 20 minutes on the water4.

02 Ferries and water buses

Star Ferry

Hong Kong, China (Hong Kong SAR) since 1888

44,289 passengers a day on average in 2025 (company figure)5

The problem
Victoria Harbour separates Hong Kong Island from the Kowloon peninsula. Until the first cross-harbour road tunnel opened in 1972, boats were the only way over; the MTR followed in 19806.
How residents use it
How essential the crossing was became clear in 1966, when a fare rise of five cents set off a hunger strike and then riots67. In 2025 the company averaged 44,289 passengers a day5.
How it came about
Dorabjee Naorojee Mithaiwala began crossings in 1888 with a single wooden steamer, the Morning Star, and in 1898 the business, by then owned by Sir Catchick Paul Chater, became the Star Ferry Company. Ridership peaked in 1969 at 54 million, about 148,000 a day. The Central terminal moved to Pier 7 in November 2006, and the government's franchise now runs to 31 March 20336.
Use it yourself
Tap an Octopus card or buy a token from the machines on the pier; coins are no longer taken at the turnstile7.

03 Ferries and water buses

Vaporetti and traghetti

Venice, Italy since 1881

Four bridges over the Grand Canal (Euronews, 2025)8

The problem
Venice's historic centre has no roads, and only four bridges cross the Grand Canal8. Water buses do what buses do elsewhere, and the traghetto, a gondola that simply goes from one bank to the other, does the work of a pedestrian crossing.
How residents use it
In 2016 the city trialled priority boarding for residents at eight vaporetto stops after commuters complained of being crowded off by visitors9. Traghetto gondoliers describe their crossings as designed for residents, who in 2025 paid €0.70 against €2 for visitors8.
How it came about
The first steam vaporetto, the Regina Margherita, reached the Grand Canal in June 1881; gondoliers struck for 48 hours that November in protest10. The present operator, ACTV, was founded in 197811. There were about 30 traghetto crossings in the 1950s; now there are just over half a dozen, and often only two to four are working at once12.
Use it yourself
To cross like a Venetian, take the traghetto from the Pescaria fish market at Rialto to Santa Sofia, and stand for the ride, as locals traditionally do12.

04 Ferries and water buses

IJ ferries

Amsterdam, Netherlands since 1308

About 29 million crossings in 2025 (Noord district executive's figure)13

The problem
The broad IJ separates the old centre from Amsterdam-Noord. A bridge for cyclists and pedestrians further east, the Oostbrug, is not due to be built until 203113; until then the ferries carry the load.
How residents use it
Noord's district executive wrote in 2026 that commuters and students cross every day and rush-hour boats are full13. In September 2024 residents told NH Nieuws they had not seen the morning crush at Buiksloterweg so bad in ten years14.
How it came about
A crossing here, the Buiksloterveer, is documented from 130815. The city made the ferry free for everyone in 191216, and it still is. The lines were renumbered F1 to F9 in January 202117.
Use it yourself
The docks are on the water side of Centraal, behind the station. The Buiksloterweg boat needs no ticket, takes bikes and runs through the night18.

05 Ferries and water buses

Staten Island Ferry

New York, United States since 1905

More than 16 million passengers a year on a 5.2-mile route (NYC DOT)19

The problem
Staten Island has no subway link to Manhattan20. The ferry is the direct transit connection across New York Harbor, between St. George and Whitehall at the foot of Manhattan19.
How residents use it
On a weekday five boats make 117 trips and carry about 45,000 people, with a boat every 15 minutes in the rush hour, according to the city's transport department19.
How it came about
The City of New York has run the ferry since 19051920. The fare was abolished in 19972122, and cars were barred after the attacks of 11 September 200122. The newest Ollis-class boats, each carrying 4,500 passengers, entered service in 2022 and 202319.
Use it yourself
No ticket and no booking: walk into Whitehall Terminal, beside the South Ferry and Whitehall Street subway stations, and wait for the doors. Boats run 24 hours a day, every day of the year19.

06 Ferries and water buses

Sydney Ferries

Sydney, Australia since 1853

Parramatta River line: 27 km and 18 wharves23

The problem
The harbour and the Parramatta River cut Sydney into peninsulas and bays. From Manly or the river suburbs, the ferry to Circular Quay goes straight across water that roads have to go round.
How residents use it
The F1 line to Manly alone logged about 6.75 million trips in 2025 by Opal card data24. Ferries take the same Opal ticketing as the city's trains and buses23.
How it came about
Ferry trips to Manly began in 1853, and a regular service was running by 185524. The four big Freshwater-class Manly ferries entered service between 1982 and 198824. Operations have been contracted out since July 2012, and run by Transdev Sydney Ferries since July 201923.
Use it yourself
Tap on and off at the wharf with an Opal card or a contactless bank card. Every line but the F10 starts from Circular Quay23.

07 Funiculars and cable cars

Ascensores

Valparaíso, Chile since 1883Partly running

About 30 built; 7 running in October 2025 (BioBioChile)2526

The problem
Valparaíso is a narrow strip of flat port backed by steep cerros, the hills where much of the city lives. The ascensores, short funiculars, connect the hillside neighbourhoods to the shops, offices and port below25.
How residents use it
BioBioChile reported in 2025 that hill residents had long relied on the ascensores as their main cheap transport, and that with most of them closed, older and sick people had been largely stuck at home for years26. Chile's heritage council still describes them as a transport system in use27.
How it came about
The first, Ascensor Concepción, opened on 1 December 1883 and was originally counterbalanced with water2728. About 30 were eventually built, though accounts vary2526. The historic quarter they serve was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003, and in 2012 the state agreed to buy ten of them2529.
Use it yourself
Start with Concepción, whose lower station is up Pasaje Elías, an alley off Calle Prat by the Reloj Turri clock28, and check which ascensores are running that week: closures are frequent26.

08 Funiculars and cable cars

Metrocable Line K

Medellín, Colombia since 2004

3,000 passengers an hour in each direction; about 9 minutes end to end30

The problem
The informal barrios of Medellín's north-east climb slopes that buses serve slowly. Before Line K, residents of Santo Domingo Savio could spend more than two and a half hours commuting each way31.
How residents use it
Line K carried about 46,000 passengers a day in 2019, and some 96 million in its first 15 years32. A 2012 study in the American Journal of Epidemiology found homicides fell faster in neighbourhoods near the line than in comparison areas, though other public works arrived at the same time33.
How it came about
Line K opened on 7 August 2004 with four stations: Acevedo, Andalucía, Popular and Santo Domingo30. The metro company calls it the world's first urban aerial cable built for mass public transport32, and its success led to more Metrocable lines across the city31.
Use it yourself
Board at Acevedo, where the cable car connects directly with Metro Line A3031. The line closes for maintenance from time to time, most recently from 17 September to 2 October 2026 for its annual overhaul34.

09 Funiculars and cable cars

Mi Teleférico

La Paz and El Alto, Bolivia since 2014

10 lines; more than 640 million passengers by September 2025 (company figure)35

The problem
La Paz lies in a deep canyon between roughly 3,200 and 4,100 metres, with El Alto on the plateau above. Cable cars go straight up the slope, saving riders up to an hour against the road, according to the builder, Doppelmayr3637.
How residents use it
About 200,000 people a day used the network in 2024. An Inter-American Development Bank estimate cited by EFE puts the average time saving at 22%, and one El Alto vendor told the agency her commute had fallen from about an hour to 10 or 15 minutes37.
How it came about
The state company was created by decree on 23 April 201438, and the Red Line opened on 30 May that year, followed by the Yellow and Green lines before the year was out3940. Guinness World Records listed it as the most extensive urban cable car network in the world in 201840. The tenth line, the Silver, opened in March 20193539.
Use it yourself
Start at Estación Central, the old railway station, and ride the Red Line up to 16 de Julio in El Alto, where you can change to the Blue and Silver lines39.

10 Funiculars and cable cars

Carmelit

Haifa, Israel since 1959

6 stations on about 1.8 km4142

The problem
Haifa climbs steeply from the port and lower town up the slopes of Mount Carmel. The Carmelit, an underground funicular, climbs 274 metres between its bottom and top stations, joining downtown, Hadar and Carmel Center41.
How residents use it
It is part of the city's ordinary public transport: Haifa's day and week tickets cover it, and single rides are paid with Israel's national Rav-Kav card43. After it reopened in 2018 it was carrying about 4,000 passengers a day41.
How it came about
The line opened on 6 October 19594144. It closed for renovation from 1986 to 1992, and again after a fire at the bottom station in February 2017; it reopened on 4 October 2018 with new cars and a new cable from Doppelmayr Garaventa4143. Guinness World Records lists it as the shortest operating underground system42.
Use it yourself
Ride from Downtown (the old Paris Square station) up to Carmel Center, about eight minutes43, then walk down through Hadar to see the slope you have just climbed.

11 Funiculars and cable cars

Wellington Cable Car

Wellington, New Zealand since 1902

More than 1 million passengers a year (Wellington City Council)45

The problem
It links the flat business district on Lambton Quay with the hill suburb of Kelburn, rising 120 metres over a 609-metre route46. It was built because developers wanted workers to be able to live on the empty hills close to town47.
How residents use it
Wellington City Council describes it as a public transport link used by commuters, students and visitors45. Its Salamanca stop serves Victoria University of Wellington46.
How it came about
The Kelburn and Karori Tramway Company opened the line on 22 February 1902, and it carried about 425,000 trips in its first year4748. Annual passengers passed one million in 1912, and electricity replaced steam for the winding gear in 1933. The rebuilt line, with Swiss equipment, reopened in October 19794648.
Use it yourself
Board at the bottom of Cable Car Lane, off Lambton Quay. The top station sits beside the Botanic Garden and the free Cable Car Museum49, so plenty of riders go up and walk down through the garden4650.

12 Funiculars and cable cars

Polybahn

Zürich, Switzerland since 1889

100 seconds from Central to the Polyterrasse5152

The problem
The university and the ETH, Switzerland's federal institute of technology, sit on a terrace above the old town. The Polybahn climbs 41 metres on 176 metres of track, at an average gradient of 23%, from Central, a short walk from the main station, to the Polyterrasse51.
How residents use it
Students call it the Student Express, and it carries up to two million passengers a year52.
How it came about
It opened on 8 January 1889, worked by water ballast. It went electric in 1897 and was rebuilt in 1996. The bank UBS owns it, and the city transport company VBZ runs it51.
Use it yourself
Ordinary Zürich zone 110 tickets are valid51. The ride takes 100 seconds5152.

13 Trams and a hanging railway

Kusttram

Belgian coast, Belgium since 1885

67 stops on about 67 km (Guinness gives 68 km)535455

The problem
It strings together the towns along Belgium's whole North Sea coast, from De Panne near the French border to Knokke-Heist near the Dutch one along a narrow, built-up strip53.
How residents use it
With 67 stops, De Lijn says there is always one near you55, which is why it works as the coast's local transport spine as much as a seaside ride. It carried about 15 million passengers in 201653.
How it came about
The first section, between Ostend and Nieuwpoort, opened in 1885 as part of Belgium's national light-railway network5356. The Flemish operator De Lijn took it over in 199153. Guinness World Records lists it as the longest tram route in the world54.
Use it yourself
Buy a ticket in the De Lijn app, which also tracks the tram live and tells you when to get off; day and multi-day tickets suit hopping between towns55.

14 Trams and a hanging railway

Melbourne trams

Melbourne, Australia since 1884

250 km of double track and about 1,700 stops; the operator calls it the world's largest operational tram network57

The problem
Trams move people along the city's wide street grid and inner suburbs. Most of the network, 74%, shares road space with other traffic57.
How residents use it
Trams made 160.2 million trips in 2024–2558. They take myki, the state's standard ticket, except inside the free zone in the city centre59.
How it came about
Horse trams began in 1884 and cable trams in 1885; electric trams have run continuously since 190658. Since 1 January 2015, trips within the city centre and Docklands have been free59.
Use it yourself
A trip entirely inside the Free Tram Zone needs no ticket. If it starts or ends outside, touch on with your myki as usual59.

15 Trams and a hanging railway

Hong Kong Tramways

Hong Kong, China (Hong Kong SAR) since 1904

About 138,000 trips a day in 2025, on 165 passenger trams60

The problem
The north shore of Hong Kong Island is a dense, narrow ribbon between the harbour and the hills. The trams run its length, from Kennedy Town to Shau Kei Wan60.
How residents use it
The trams carried about 138,000 passenger trips a day in 2025, according to the Transport Department60. People call them the ding ding, after the double bell they ring to warn pedestrians61.
How it came about
Electric service began on 30 July 1904, and the first double-deckers arrived in 191261. The French group RATP Dev has owned the company since 201061. In 2021 Guinness World Records recognised the fleet as the largest of double-deck trams in service6162.
Use it yourself
Pay as you get off. The fare is flat however far you go, and Octopus cards have been accepted since 200161.

16 Trams and a hanging railway

Schwebebahn

Wuppertal, Germany since 1901

13.3 km and 20 stations6364

The problem
Wuppertal is a long, narrow valley city. Instead of competing for street space, the Schwebebahn hangs from a steel frame, mostly about 12 metres above the river Wupper, and then above a valley road to Vohwinkel63.
How residents use it
It is ordinary public transport, run by the municipal utility WSW, and carries about 80,000 passengers on a weekday6365 and roughly 20 million a year66.
How it came about
The city chose Eugen Langen's suspension system in 1894. Emperor Wilhelm II took a test ride on 24 October 1900, and the first section opened on 1 March 1901; the line was complete by 19036364. On 12 April 1999 a train fell into the Wupper, killing five people, the line's only fatal accident63. After a power rail fell in November 2018 it was shut for almost nine months6365.
Use it yourself
It sits inside the regional tariff, so ride it end to end, Oberbarmen to Vohwinkel, as an ordinary trip, and check WSW for closures first: a fallen power rail shut it for most of 2019, and a long renewal closure is planned from 20326366.

17 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

Central–Mid-Levels Escalator

Hong Kong, China (Hong Kong SAR) since 1993

About 99,000 pedestrian trips a day (late 2024)67

The problem
The flats of Mid-Levels sit on a steep hillside above Central, where direct roads are impractical. A covered chain of escalators and walkways lifts pedestrians about 135 metres over roughly 800 metres, from Queen's Road Central to Conduit Road68.
How residents use it
It runs for commuters: downhill from 6 to 10 a.m. for the morning trip to work, then uphill until midnight. The Transport Department counted about 99,000 pedestrian trips a day in late 202467.
How it came about
It opened on 15 October 1993, at a final cost of HK$240 million against the HK$100 million approved in 1990. A government audit in 1996 found it had not reduced traffic congestion68. Daily use has nonetheless risen, from 34,000 trips in 1996 to about 99,0006768.
Use it yourself
Mind the direction: before 10 a.m. it only goes down, so going up means the stairs alongside6768.

18 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

Comuna 13 escalators

Medellín, Colombia since 2011

About 12,000 residents served (Alcaldía de Medellín)69

The problem
Residents of Las Independencias, on a hillside in Comuna 1370, faced a climb of hundreds of steps, likened by ElevatorWorld to climbing a 28-storey building. The escalators cut a half-hour walk to about five minutes71.
How residents use it
The city says about 12,000 residents benefit and use them on daily routes69. When they opened, Semana met elderly and disabled residents who had been kept at home by the stairs70.
How it came about
The system opened at Christmas 2011, in six double sections with walkways and new public space70. It has since become one of the city's biggest visitor attractions, with nearly two million visitors in 2025, and in 2026 the city carried out its first full renewal72.
Use it yourself
Treat it as what it is, a working public facility run by the city: it is sometimes stopped for safety, so be ready to use the stairs69.

19 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

RÉSO

Montreal, Canada since 1962

About 32 km of corridors (City of Montreal; Tourisme Montréal says 33 km)7374

The problem
Montreal winters are hard and its summers hot. The city describes its indoor pedestrian network as cool in summer and warm in winter, a way to cross downtown whatever the weather73.
How residents use it
The city says nearly 500,000 people use it every day73, linking metro stations with offices, universities and shops.
How it came about
The first link opened in 1962, between Central Station and the shopping concourse under Place Ville Marie; the urbanist Vincent Ponte is credited with the idea. The metro, opened in 1966, extended it, and Complexe Desjardins in 1974 started a second core. It was rebranded RÉSO in 2004, a play on réseau, network75.
Use it yourself
Download the city's map of the network before you go and look for the RÉSO logo on signs. Much of it is indoor corridor and mall rather than tunnel7375.

20 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

Skyway System

Minneapolis, United States since 1962

About 9.5 miles of skyways linking around 80 blocks7677

The problem
Enclosed bridges at second- or third-floor level let people cross downtown out of the winter cold and the summer heat. They were also meant to keep downtown shops competitive with the new suburban malls78.
How residents use it
The main users are downtown office workers. Governing reported in 2022 that use fell after the pandemic and some property managers closed their skyways77.
How it came about
The developer Leslie Park built the first skyway in 1962; his Seventh Street bridge of 1963 is the oldest still in use79. The IDS Center, completed in 1973, was the first major building designed with skyways on all four sides, which tied the network together78. Each bridge is built and owned by the buildings it joins, so it grew piecemeal76.
Use it yourself
Hours vary by building, because the bridges are private. Go on a weekday in office hours and start from the IDS Center's Crystal Court, following the overhead maps7680.

21 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

+15 Skywalk

Calgary, Canada since 1969

86 bridges and more than 16 km of walkways (City of Calgary)81

The problem
Calgary's winters are long and cold. The +15 lets people walk between offices and shops on enclosed, heated bridges about 15 feet above the street, away from snow and traffic8182.
How residents use it
CBC reported 10,000 to 20,000 pedestrians a day in the busy winter season, though one downtown resident told it he prefers to walk on the street83.
How it came about
A city planning architect, Harold Hanen, designed the system between 1966 and 1969. New developments had to connect, and got extra floor space in return; where a building could not, its developer paid into a city fund that built the missing links82. The first bridge went up in 196981. Critics have long argued it drains life from the pavement below83.
Use it yourself
Download the city's map: bridges belong to different buildings, and some close earlier or at weekends. The city's general hours in 2026 were 6 a.m. to 9 p.m. on weekdays and 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. at weekends81.

22 Escalators, skyways, tunnels and bike bridges

Cykelslangen and Inderhavnsbroen

Copenhagen, Denmark since 2014

21,600 cyclists on an average weekday on Inderhavnsbroen (September 2023)84

The problem
Copenhagen's harbour splits the city, and crossings for bikes and people on foot were few or awkward. Cykelslangen replaced a staircase that cyclists and pedestrians had to share85; Inderhavnsbroen gave a direct crossing from Nyhavn to Christianshavn86.
How residents use it
City counts put Inderhavnsbroen at 21,600 cyclists on an average weekday in September 202384. On one day in 2015, 20,700 cyclists used Cykelslangen85.
How it came about
Cykelslangen, the Cycle Snake, designed by Dissing+Weitling, opened on 28 June 2014 and curls 200 to 220 metres, depending on the source, over the water from Kalvebod Brygge to Havneholmen8587. Inderhavnsbroen was due in 2013, but its first backer went bankrupt and a construction error left the two halves not meeting; it finally opened on 7 July 201688. Its moving sections needed costly repairs within six years89.
Use it yourself
Ride Cykelslangen from Kalvebod Brygge towards the Fisketorvet side. On Inderhavnsbroen, expect a sharp bend in the middle where the two halves meet8687.

23 Stations that do more than trains

Stationsplein bike parking

Utrecht, Netherlands since 2017

12,500 spaces; the city calls it the largest bike park of its kind in the world90

The problem
Utrecht Centraal is the biggest station in the Netherlands, and so many people arrive by bike that racks spilled across the square. The garage puts them underground, steps from the platforms9091.
How residents use it
It is built for people who cycle to their train: the first 24 hours are free, you check in with the national OV-chipkaart, it is staffed, and it never closes. One level is kept for subscribers90.
How it came about
Construction began in November 2014. The first part opened in August 2017 and the second, completing it, on 19 August 2019. Ector Hoogstad Architecten designed it; the city, the rail-infrastructure company ProRail and the national railway NS run it together9092.
Use it yourself
Cycle straight in on the one-way lane, follow the digital signs that count free spaces on each row, and park on levels +1 or −1 for the day. Cargo bikes go to a separate garage nearby90.

24 Stations that do more than trains

Hauptbahnhof and ShopVille

Zürich, Switzerland since 1970

About 449,800 passengers a day in 2025, the most of any Swiss station93

The problem
Swiss law broadly bans Sunday work, so most shops shut on Sundays. An exemption for businesses in major public-transport hubs makes the main station the place where Zürich does its Sunday shopping9495.
How residents use it
Around 190 shops, restaurants and services open every day of the year, so the station works as a neighbourhood shopping centre. SBB lists minimum hours (2026) of 9 a.m. to 9 p.m. on weekdays and 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. at weekends9596.
How it came about
The station opened on 9 August 1847 as the terminus of the Spanisch-Brötli-Bahn; the present hall dates from 187193. ShopVille, the arcade beneath the station and square, opened on 1 October 1970 to what the Swiss National Museum calls a minor sensation97. It grew again when the Löwenstrasse platforms opened in 20149398.
Use it yourself
Plan Sunday errands around the station, where the exemption applies94. Check SBB's page for holiday hours96.

25 Stations that do more than trains

Nørreport station square

Copenhagen, Denmark since 2015

More than 250,000 people pass through each day, according to the architects99

The problem
Nørreport is where S-trains, regional trains, the metro and buses meet. Before its redesign the square was clogged with traffic and thousands of bikes left wherever they fell. The new plan pushed cars to one side and gave bikes a place100101.
How residents use it
Many people arrive by bike and switch to train or metro, so the square is laid out around sunken bike beds for about 2,100 to 2,500 bicycles, depending on the source99100101.
How it came about
The station opened on 1 July 1918 in the tunnel between Østerport and the central station, and the metro arrived in 2002102. The architects COBE won a 2009 competition to rebuild the square with Gottlieb Paludan Architects; COBE gives 2015 as the year it was completed99. The bike beds sit 40 cm below the street and double as rainwater basins in a cloudburst99100.
Use it yourself
Park in a bike bed, take the stairs down to the trains or the metro, and if you need groceries, Torvehallerne is across the road102103.

26 Food markets tied to transit

Reading Terminal Market

Philadelphia, United States since 1892

76 merchants and over 6 million visits a year (APA, 2014)104

The problem
When the Reading Railroad built its terminal at 12th and Market, it gathered the street markets on the site into one hall beneath the tracks, which kept fresh-food trading downtown104. It now sits beside SEPTA's Jefferson Station105.
How residents use it
The American Planning Association says most shoppers live in the Philadelphia region, with tourists about a quarter. Stalls sell groceries as well as lunches, and SNAP food benefits are accepted104.
How it came about
The market opened in 1892 beneath a train shed that opened the following year; the shed became a National Historic Landmark in 1976106107. By the late 1970s only 23 merchants were left104. The last train left the terminal on 6 November 1984, and the Pennsylvania Convention Center Authority bought the market in 1990105107.
Use it yourself
Take SEPTA Regional Rail to Jefferson Station, next door. The Pennsylvania Dutch merchants generally trade Monday to Saturday, so avoid Sundays if you want them105.

27 Food markets tied to transit

Torvehallerne

Copenhagen, Denmark since 2011

More than 5 million visits a year (owner's figure)108

The problem
When the vegetable market left Israels Plads for Valby in 1958, central Copenhagen lost its everyday fresh-food market109. Torvehallerne put one back on the same square, beside the city's busiest commuter interchange110.
How residents use it
Torvehallerne's own figures, cited by TV 2 Kosmopol, put its customers at about 100,000 a week, mostly Copenhageners doing their shopping108.
How it came about
The Grønttorvet vegetable market opened on the site in 1889109. In 1997 Iselin C. Hermann and Hans Peter Hagens proposed new market halls in an article in Politiken; residents gathered more than 2,000 signatures, and the city council backed the plan in 2001110. The first developer went under in the 2008 financial crisis, and the property company Jeudan finished the two glass halls, which opened in September 2011109.
Use it yourself
Get off at Nørreport and cross the street103.

Markthal

Rotterdam, Netherlands since 2014

About 8.5 million visitors in the year to October 2024111

The problem
It puts a covered food market, 228 homes and an underground car park on one central site beside the Blaak rail and metro station, with glass walls to keep Rotterdam's weather out112113.
How residents use it
A 2017 study found about half of visitors came from the Rotterdam area. The architects' own ten-year review notes that greengrocers and flower stalls have been squeezed out by rents in favour of chains and takeaways, weakening its role as a daily market111. There is a supermarket on the first underground level112.
How it came about
Designed by MVRDV and opened by Queen Máxima on 1 October 2014, it is a horseshoe arch of flats over a hall about 120 metres long and 40 metres high112114. The inside of the arch carries an 11,000 m² artwork, Horn of Plenty, by Arno Coenen and Iris Roskam114. In 2020 MVRDV's Winy Maas said it had come to resemble a station hall, and called for a return to the market idea111.
Use it yourself
Take metro line A, B or C to Blaak: the station is directly beside the hall113.

29 Food markets tied to transit

Great Market Hall

Budapest, Hungary since 1897

Three levels under one iron roof115

The problem
Budapest built central covered halls to bring wholesale and retail food trading under one roof. This one stands at the Pest end of Liberty Bridge, and a railway track and tunnel were built to bring goods in115116.
How residents use it
We Love Budapest's 2024 review found the ground floor and basement still serve real shopping, for fish, meat, cheese and produce, sometimes cheaper than supermarkets, while the upper floor mostly serves tourists117.
How it came about
Samu Pecz's design was chosen from nine entries, building began in 1894, and the hall opened on 15 February 1897115116118. Badly damaged in the Second World War, it was later listed as a protected monument, was closed as unsafe in 1991, and was restored in the 1990s with its Zsolnay ceramic roof tiles put back115116118.
Use it yourself
Take metro M4 or trams 2, 47 or 49 to Fővám tér119. For everyday shopping go on a weekday morning, use a side entrance and stay downstairs117.

Municipal markets

Barcelona, Spain since 1848

43 markets and 1,880 stalls and shops at the end of 2023120121

The problem
Barcelona keeps fresh-food shopping in its neighbourhoods through a city-run network of covered markets spread across every district120121.
How residents use it
The markets recorded 70.6 million visits in 2023 and held 30.3% of the city's fresh-food trade, according to the markets institute's annual report121.
How it came about
Santa Caterina, built in 1848, was the city's first covered market; Enric Miralles and Benedetta Tagliabue's rebuild reopened in 2005122. Sant Antoni, opened in 1882, closed in 2009 for a restoration that kept remains of the city wall and the Roman Via Augusta found beneath it, and reopened in May 2018123124.
Use it yourself
Use the market in whichever neighbourhood you are staying in; the city lists them all on its markets site. Sant Antoni still holds its Sunday book market120123.

Same problem, different answers

Set side by side, systems that solve the same problem show the choices cities made.

Paying for the crossing

Two of the six ferries are free. Amsterdam made its IJ ferry free in 191216; New York abolished the Staten Island fare in 199721. The others charge through the same card a commuter uses on the bus or the metro, the İstanbulkart, Octopus or Opal, so the boat becomes one more leg of the journey to work3723. Venice goes further and prices the traghetto differently for residents and visitors8.

Three ways up a hill

The oldest funiculars date from 1883 to 1902: Valparaíso's first in 1883, Zürich's Polybahn in 1889, Wellington in 1902254851. Haifa put the idea in a tunnel in 195941. Cable cars came later, when Medellín showed in 2004 that the technology could carry a city's commuters32, and La Paz and El Alto built ten lines from 201439. Outdoor escalators are the third answer, for slopes that are steep, short and crowded: Hong Kong's in 1993, Comuna 13's in 20116870. All three need constant upkeep, and Valparaíso shows what happens when it stops26.

Under the street or over it

Montreal went underground; Minneapolis and Calgary went up a floor. What matters more is who owns the links. In Minneapolis each bridge belongs to the buildings it joins, so hours vary and some skyways shut after the pandemic7677. Calgary wrote connection into its planning rules, trading extra floor space for bridges and running a fund to fill gaps82. In both cities critics say the street below loses out7783.

Where to put 10,000 bikes

Utrecht and Copenhagen faced the same problem, thousands of bicycles at the station door, and answered it in opposite ways. Utrecht dug a 12,500-space garage under the square90; Nørreport sank shallow bike beds into the square itself and left it open99. Zürich shows a different kind of station advantage: a legal exemption that makes it the place to shop on a Sunday94.

Next to the station is not always enough

Philadelphia's market was built into a railway terminus, and Budapest's hall had its own freight track; Rotterdam and Copenhagen placed their new halls beside busy stations104110113115. A station brings crowds, but not always shoppers. The Markthal's own architects say rising rents have pushed out greengrocers in favour of takeaways111, while Barcelona's district-by-district network still holds almost a third of the city's fresh-food trade121.
