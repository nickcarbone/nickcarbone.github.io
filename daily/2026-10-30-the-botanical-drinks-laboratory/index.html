The Botanical Drinks Laboratory

A working reference for building complex, low-sugar drinks from herbs, spices and peel: what twelve botanicals contribute, how extraction changes them, and three prototype recipes to start from.

Twelve specimens · three extractions · three prototypes

Herbarium of the drinks cabinet

Rosemary, gentian root, coriander seed, lemon peel.

Mounted for extraction, not for show.

Det. Daily desk, 30 October 2026

Take the sugar out of a soft drink and most of what is left tastes thin. Sugar does several jobs at once: it adds weight in the mouth, rounds off acid, and hides bitterness. A drink without it has to earn its interest some other way, and the materials for that are already in the spice rack and the herb bed.

The tools are older than soda. Bitterness is closely watched by the body: humans carry about 25 different bitter-taste receptors38. A little gentian goes a long way, and the US caps quinine in carbonated drinks at 83 parts per million39. Acidity, from citrus juice or hibiscus, gives a drink its edge. Aroma, which carries much of what we call flavour, comes from volatile oils in seeds, leaves and peel. Pungency, from ginger and pepper, supplies the warmth that sugar’s body usually provides. Salt, used in tiny amounts, does something surprising: in a 1997 study in Nature, sodium suppressed bitterness far more than it suppressed sweetness, letting the pleasant tastes through40.

This lab manual covers twelve botanicals that between them pull all of those levers, the chemistry of getting their flavour out and into water, a pairing tool to suggest combinations and starting ratios, three prototype recipes with batch and cost calculators, and a tasting sheet for writing down what happened.

AromaVolatile oils from seed, leaf and peel. Lost to heat and air.
BitternessGentian, citrus pith. Small doses, big effect.
AcidityLemon and lime juice, hibiscus. The drink’s edge.
PungencyGinger and pepper. Warmth in place of body.
SaltA pinch per batch. May quieten bitterness.

Part oneTwelve specimens

What each botanical brings, the compounds behind it, and where to start.

Each sheet below shows the main aroma or taste compounds as a range. Where a pharmacopoeia or an EU regulation sets limits for the essential oil, those are the figures; they describe what a compliant oil must contain, not an average. Where only individual studies exist, the range is what those studies measured, and plants vary with variety, climate and harvest. The bars run from 0 to 100 per cent.

The “start at” dose is the desk’s suggestion for a single botanical steeped in 500 ml of water, scaled from the European Medicines Agency’s peppermint-tea dose of 1.5–3 g of dried leaf in 100–150 ml of water4 and adjusted for each ingredient’s strength. They are starting points for tasting, not tested recipes. Gentian is dosed by the pinch for a reason that turns up in the extraction section.

Part twoGetting the flavour out

Water, alcohol and oil each take different things from a plant.

Water is a poor solvent for aroma

Most of the aroma in these botanicals sits in essential oils, which are, as the name suggests, oily. In 1993 researchers measured how much of 31 such compounds (monoterpenes) would dissolve in water. The hydrocarbons, such as limonene and pinene, managed less than 35 parts per million. Compounds carrying an oxygen atom, the alcohols and ketones, dissolved one or two orders of magnitude better24.

That gap explains a lot of kitchen experience. Limonene, the main compound in citrus peel oil, dissolves to about 14 mg per litre25; menthol to around 450 mg26; linalool, coriander’s main note, to about 1,600 mg27. Coriander and mint give up their aroma to water readily. Citrus zest steeped in water gives a faint, slightly bitter liquid, while the same zest in vodka smells intensely of lemon.

Heat drives aroma off

Essential-oil compounds boil at between 150°C and 300°C, yet distillers extract them with steam at about 100°C. Oil and water that don’t mix each contribute their own vapour pressure, so the pair boils below either liquid alone and the oil comes over with the steam28,44. A simmering pan is a crude still. What rises off it, and fills the kitchen with the smell of ginger or rosemary, is flavour leaving the drink. Covering the pan, keeping the water just off the boil, and adding zest and tender leaves after the heat is off all keep more of it in the drink.

Cold extracts a different drink

Heat does extract more, and it extracts the harsh things fastest. The best data come from tea. A Japanese review of cold-brewing research, using hot brewing (80°C for two minutes) as its benchmark found that cold water (about 10°C, one hour) pulled out only around a fifth of the main bitter, astringent catechin, EGCG, half the caffeine, and most of the softer EGC. The savoury amino acids came out well even in near-freezing water29. A second study found cold-brewed green tea less bitter and astringent, and tasters preferred it30. Herbal infusions have not been studied as closely, so treat the parallel as a guide: cold steeping tends to give a rounder, less tannic drink, at the cost of time.

Time and size

Longer steeping keeps extracting, and the later fractions are the astringent ones: a ten-minute tea infusion carried significantly more polyphenols than a shorter one, and an electronic tongue registered it as more astringent43. Cutting and grinding expose more surface, so extraction runs faster, but grinding also warms the material. Spice-processing studies record volatile-oil losses of about 37 per cent for nutmeg and 17 per cent for cinnamon from the heat of grinding alone31. Crack seeds just before use, and crack rather than powder them.

Alcohol, and the cloud in the glass

Alcohol dissolves the oily aroma compounds that water rejects, which is why tinctures and bitters are made with spirit. It also explains a familiar bit of bar theatre. Pour water into ouzo or pastis and it turns milky: anethole, the anise compound, stays dissolved while there is enough alcohol, and falls out as tiny droplets as the alcohol is diluted. Scientists call it the ouzo effect32. Expect the same faint haze if you top a strong alcohol tincture of peel or juniper with soda.

Oil infusions need care

Fresh herbs, garlic or ginger sitting in oil create the airless, low-acid conditions in which Clostridium botulinum can grow and make its toxin, and contamination can’t be seen or smelled34. The US National Center for Home Food Preservation says garlic-in-oil kept at room temperature risks botulism; refrigerate it at 4°C or below and use it within four days, or freeze it33. Health Canada does not recommend relying on refrigeration alone for long storage34. Oil-based extraction has its uses in cocktails, but for the drinks in this guide water and, if you want, alcohol do the work.

How bitter is gentian?

The European Pharmacopoeia measures bitterness as a dilution: the bitterness value is the reciprocal of the most dilute solution that still tastes bitter, with quinine hydrochloride as the reference at 200,00022. Germany’s Commission E set a minimum of 10,000 for gentian root, and its main bitter compound, gentiopicroside, has a value of about 12,00021. A root at 10,000 is still bitter, under the pharmacopoeia’s test, at one gram in ten litres. So the 0.4 g of root in the spritz base below, diluted to the glass, sits at about one part in 4,000: two or three times the threshold if the root were fully extracted. A cold steep extracts less, so taste the base and add a few minutes’ hot steep for the gentian if it needs more spine.

Acid in numbers

Lemon juice carries about 1.44 g of citric acid per fluid ounce, roughly 48 g per litre; lime juice slightly less, 1.38 g35. Both sit at around pH 2 to 2.636. The recipes and matrix here use 15–50 ml of juice per 500 ml of liquid, which is a sensible range to taste across.

Part threeThe pairing matrix

Pick a base and a direction. The matrix suggests partners and starting weights.

The suggestions follow two rules. First, each direction has jobs to fill: a bitter-citrus drink needs a citrus aroma, a bitter element and a supporting spice. Second, among the botanicals that could fill a job, the matrix prefers one that shares an aroma compound with your base: coriander and thyme both carry linalool; rosemary, juniper and coriander share α-pinene; rosemary, cardamom and peppermint share 1,8-cineole; lemongrass and lemon peel share citral. Every overlap listed comes from the compound figures in part one.

Shared compounds are a reasonable heuristic, not a law. A large 2011 analysis of 56,498 recipes found that North American and Western European cooking tends to combine ingredients that share flavour compounds, while East Asian cooking tends to avoid doing so37. Both approaches produce good food. Treat the matrix as a place to start tasting.

Weights are per 500 ml of water. The base is used at its full starting dose; partners at 40–60 per cent of theirs.

  1. Combine peel, zest, coriander and gentian with the cold water in a jar.
  2. Steep in the fridge for 12–16 hours.
  3. Strain through a fine sieve, pressing the peel. Stir in the lemon juice and salt.
  4. To serve: 50 ml base over ice, topped with 150 ml soda water. Express a strip of grapefruit peel over the glass.

Why it works. Grapefruit pith supplies naringin bitterness; coriander’s linalool links the two citrus aromas; gentian makes the bitterness firm rather than sharp. The cold steep is chosen for the same reason cold-brewed tea is less astringent than hot. If you take regular medication, check the grapefruit caution below first.

Why it works. Covered steeping keeps in the volatile compounds that would otherwise leave with the steam. The pinch of salt is an experiment worth making: in laboratory tests, sodium suppressed bitterness more than sweetness, though at much higher concentrations than this, so taste a glass with and without.

  1. Simmer the ginger, cardamom and pepper in the water, covered, on the lowest heat for 20 minutes.
  2. Take off the heat, add the lime zest and steep 10 minutes more.
  3. Strain, pressing hard on the ginger. Stir in the lime juice, and the sugar if using.
  4. Dilute 1 part concentrate to 4 parts soda water, or hot water in winter.

Why it works. Simmering forms zingerone from the fresh root’s gingerols, rounding the heat. The zest goes in off the heat so its limonene is not boiled away. Fifteen grams of sugar across twelve drinks is just over a gram each.

Keep them cold, keep them short

None of these is preserved. Fresh plant material, water and little sugar or acid make a perishable liquid, and there are no tested recipes for shelf-stable versions of infused syrups; extension services advise refrigeration only42. The desk’s working rule is to refrigerate from the moment of straining and finish a batch within three days, and never to store any of them at room temperature. That rule is caution, not a measured shelf life.

Before you startThe main cautions

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children. The European Medicines Agency does not recommend medicinal preparations of rosemary (under 12s), juniper and gentian (under 18s) in these groups, mostly for lack of safety data2,17,21. A culinary sprig is a different dose from a medicinal one, but if in doubt leave them out.

Peppermint and reflux. The EMA advises people with heartburn or reflux to avoid peppermint leaf tea, people with gallstones to take care, and does not recommend it for children under four4.

Ginger. The EMA advises avoiding medicinal ginger in pregnancy as a precaution, and notes a possible interaction with anticoagulants whose clinical relevance is unknown12.

Grapefruit and medicines. Grapefruit can change how some prescription drugs are absorbed; the FDA lists Seville oranges, pomelos and tangelos as possibly having the same effect11. Check before using them if you take regular medication.

Juniper and kidneys. The EMA says juniper should not be used in severe kidney disease17.

Cinnamon. Left out of the twelve on purpose: cinnamon and cassia contain coumarin, for which the tolerable daily intake used in Europe is 0.1 mg per kilogram of body weight41. Regular strong cinnamon infusions can add up.

Storage. Refrigerate everything, make small batches, and avoid oil infusions of fresh plants unless you will freeze them or use them within days33.
