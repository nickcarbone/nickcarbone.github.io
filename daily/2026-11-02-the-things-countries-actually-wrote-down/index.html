Explorer · constitutions and founding laws

The Things Countries Actually Wrote Down

Fifty-one clauses from constitutions and founding laws, quoted in their own words, with what they meant, what became of them, and the famous lines that were never written at all.

51 entries · 29 countries · 120 sources · about 30 minutes to read in full

Constitutions are usually quoted in fragments, and the fragments drift. A line from a letter becomes a constitutional principle; a statute is remembered as a charter; a repealed clause is cited as if it still bound anyone. This explorer goes back to the documents. Each entry quotes the provision in the words of an official text or a named translation, then says when it was written, what has happened to it since and what it reveals about the country that wrote it.

Some are famous and still at work: Germany putting human dignity before every institution, Japan renouncing war, Ecuador giving nature rights it can enforce in court. Some are startling for being there at all: a minaret ban added by referendum, a king with a retirement age, a republic that still names a dissolved party as forbidden.

Others are history, kept here because they explain the present. Norway's celebrated 1814 constitution barred Jews from entering the country. The United States protected the Atlantic slave trade until 1808, and in 1861 Congress passed an amendment that would have shielded slavery from federal interference. Ireland gave up a territorial claim in exchange for peace.

The last group is the lines people think are there and are not, or that sit somewhere other than where they are usually placed. Filter by country, subject, era or status, search the text, or set two entries side by side.

How to read the status tabs

  • In force Current law in the country's text, as of 2 November 2026. (34)
  • In the text, not applied Still printed, but overridden by courts or waiting on a law that never came. (3)
  • Repealed or lapsed Removed, replaced or expired. Quoted text is struck through. (7)
  • Never ratified Proposed and approved by a legislature, but never adopted. (1)
  • Misquoted or misattributed A famous line that is not where people think it is, with what the text does say. (6)

Translations: where a country publishes an official English text it is used. Otherwise the translation is named under each quotation, and two short passages from Spanish and German are the desk's own, marked as such. Different translations of the same clause can differ a good deal; where that matters, the entry says so.

§1 Germany Art 1(1) 1949 In force

Basic Law (Grundgesetz) · Rights

The first sentence of post-war German law

Human dignity shall be inviolable. To respect and protect it shall be the duty of all state authority.

Official English translation, Federal Ministry of Justice.

Context. The Parliamentary Council adopted the Basic Law on 8 May 1949 by 53 votes to 12, four years to the day after the German surrender; it came into force on 24 May. There was no referendum: two-thirds of the state parliaments approved it, though Bavaria's voted no.

What it tells you. Dignity comes before democracy, federalism or any institution. Courts treat it as the value from which the other rights flow, and it is one of the principles the Basic Law forbids anyone to amend (see Article 79(3)). An older translation, “The dignity of man is inviolable”, still circulates.

§2 Germany Art 20(4) 1968 In force

Basic Law (Grundgesetz) · Duties

A legal right to resist, added in 1968

All Germans shall have the right to resist any person seeking to abolish this constitutional order if no other remedy is available.

Official English translation.

Context. This was not in the 1949 text. The Bundestag added it in 1968 as part of the emergency constitution (Notstandsverfassung), a package that gave the state wider powers in a crisis and drew large protests. The right of resistance was the counterweight.

What it tells you. A state wrote into its own constitution that citizens may resist those who try to overthrow it. The limiting phrase matters: it applies only “if no other remedy is available”, and it protects the constitutional order rather than licensing resistance to unpopular laws.

§3 Germany Art 79(3) 1949 In force

Basic Law (Grundgesetz) · State power

The eternity clause

Amendments to this Basic Law affecting the division of the Federation into Länder, their participation in principle in the legislative process, or the principles laid down in Articles 1 and 20 shall be inadmissible.

Official English translation.

Context. Weimar's constitution could be hollowed out by the procedures it contained; the 1933 Enabling Act was passed through them. The authors of the Basic Law put a short list of principles beyond the reach of any majority, however large.

What it tells you. Some things cannot be amended even unanimously: human dignity, democracy, the rule of law, the social and federal state. It is often misdescribed as making the first twenty articles untouchable. It protects the principles in Articles 1 and 20 and the federal structure, not every word of those articles.

§4 Germany Art 146 1990 In force

Basic Law (Grundgesetz) · State power

A constitution that plans for its own replacement

This Basic Law, which since the achievement of the unity and freedom of Germany applies to the entire German people, shall cease to apply on the day on which a constitution freely adopted by the German people takes effect.

English as quoted by International IDEA from the official translation; German text from the Unification Treaty.

Context. In 1949 the Basic Law was meant as a provisional framework for West Germany until the country was reunited. Reunification in 1990 came by the other route, accession under Article 23, and the Unification Treaty reworded Article 146 to say the Basic Law now applies to the entire German people.

What it tells you. Germany has never held the vote this article anticipates, so the Basic Law still describes itself as replaceable. Fringe movements such as the Reichsbürger misread it as proof that Germany has no valid constitution.

§5 Italy Art 1 1948 In force

Constitution of the Italian Republic · State power

A republic founded on work

Italy is a democratic Republic founded on labour.

Official English edition, Senato della Repubblica.

Context. The Constituent Assembly wrote the constitution after the 1946 referendum ended the monarchy. Work, rather than the nation, a dynasty or a faith, became the stated foundation.

What it tells you. The opening line makes the citizen as worker the basis of the state. It sits at the head of a text that goes on to recognise every citizen's right to work.

§6 Italy Provision XII 1948 In force

Constitution, Transitional and Final Provisions · Historical bargains

A named party, banned by the constitution

It shall be forbidden to reorganise, under any form whatsoever, the dissolved Fascist party.

Official English edition, Senato della Repubblica.

Context. The provision is put into effect by the Scelba Law of 1952. The state used that route to dissolve the neo-fascist groups Ordine Nuovo in 1973 and Avanguardia Nazionale in 1976, according to Il Post. A second paragraph allowed temporary voting bans on Fascist leaders for up to five years; that part has lapsed.

What it tells you. Few constitutions ban a specific historical party by name. Italy's does, and the ban is still in force, which is why debates about dissolving later far-right groups turn on whether they amount to reorganising it.

§7 Italy Provision XIII 1948 Repealed or lapsed

Constitution, Transitional and Final Provisions · Historical bargains

The royal family kept out of the country

Access and sojourn in the national territory shall be forbidden to the former kings of the House of Savoy, their spouses and their male descendants.

Official English edition, Senato della Repubblica.

Context. The first paragraph barred all members and descendants of the House of Savoy from voting or holding office; the second, quoted here, barred the former kings, their spouses and male descendants from entering Italy. Constitutional Law no. 1 of 2002 declared that both paragraphs had exhausted their effects from 10 November 2002.

What it tells you. It is often said the provision was repealed. Strictly, the first two paragraphs stopped having effect but were left in the text, and the third, which transferred the family's Italian assets to the state, was untouched.

§8 Norway Original Art 2 1814 Repealed or lapsed

Constitution of 17 May 1814 · Religion and identity

Europe's most liberal constitution, with a religious ban

Jesuits and monastic orders are not permitted. Jews are still prohibited from entry to the Realm.

English rendering by Aud V. Tønnessen; other translations vary.

Context. The Eidsvoll constitution was among the most democratic of its day, yet Article 2 kept a ban on Jews entering the country. Norway's government lists the repeals: the Jewish clause in 1851, monastic orders in 1897, and the Jesuits only in 1956. Quisling's regime reinstated the ban on Jews in 1942; it fell again at liberation in 1945.

What it tells you. The clause shows how a constitution celebrated for its liberties could carry an exclusion for nearly four decades, and how much longer a ban on a Catholic order survived. The Jesuit clause outlasted the Second World War by a decade.

§9 Ireland Art 41.2 1937 In force

Bunreacht na hÉireann (1937) · Duties

The woman in the home, kept by referendum

In particular, the State recognises that by her life within the home, woman gives to the State a support without which the common good cannot be achieved.

English text, Electoral Commission referendum booklet.

Context. The next sentence commits the state to ensure that “mothers shall not be obliged by economic necessity to engage in labour to the neglect of their duties in the home.” On 8 March 2024 the government asked voters to replace both sentences with a gender-neutral clause on family care. They refused by 73.93% to 26.07%, on a turnout of 44.35%.

What it tells you. The government campaigned for the change and lost heavily, so the 1937 text therefore remains. It is often misquoted as saying a woman's “place” is in the home; it does not use that word.

§10 Ireland Original Art 2 1937 Repealed or lapsed

Bunreacht na hÉireann (1937) · Historical bargains

A territorial claim traded for peace

The national territory consists of the whole island of Ireland, its islands and the territorial seas.

Original English text, as reproduced by CAIN, Ulster University.

Context. Article 2 claimed Northern Ireland as part of the national territory. Under the Good Friday Agreement, voters approved its replacement on 22 May 1998 by 1,442,583 votes to 85,748. The new text took effect on 2 December 1999 and speaks instead of the “entitlement and birthright of every person born in the island of Ireland” to be part of the Irish nation.

What it tells you. It is one of the clearest cases of a country rewriting its own definition of itself as part of a peace settlement: a claim to land became a statement about people.

§11 Switzerland Art 72(3) 2009 In force

Federal Constitution (1999) · Religion and identity

Six words added by popular vote

The construction of minarets is prohibited.

Official English text, Federal Office of Justice.

Context. A citizens' initiative put the ban to a national vote on 29 November 2009. It passed with 1,535,010 votes to 1,134,440 (about 57.5%) and a majority of cantons, on a turnout of 53.76%. The European Court of Human Rights declared two challenges inadmissible in 2011.

What it tells you. Swiss direct democracy lets voters write very specific rules into the constitution itself, which is how a planning matter came to sit in the federal charter.

§12 Switzerland Art 25bis (1893) 1893 Repealed or lapsed

Federal Constitution of 1874 · Religion and identity

The first popular initiative was a slaughter ban

Das Schlachten der Thiere ohne vorherige Betäubung vor dem Blutentzuge ist bei jeder Schlachtart und Viehgattung ausnahmslos untersagt.

Original German. Desk translation: “The slaughter of animals without prior stunning before bleeding is forbidden without exception for every method of slaughter and kind of livestock.”

Context. On 20 August 1893 Swiss voters approved the country's first federal popular initiative with 60.1% of the vote; whether its 11½ cantons were a majority was disputed at the time. The ban, aimed in practice at Jewish ritual slaughter, left the main text in 1973 when animal protection became a federal matter, but survived as a transitional provision until an animal protection law replaced it.

What it tells you. The first time Swiss citizens used their power to amend the constitution directly, they used it against a religious minority's practice. The ban on slaughtering mammals without stunning continues in federal law.

§13 Netherlands Art 120 1848 In force

Constitution of the Kingdom of the Netherlands · State power

Judges may not test laws against the constitution

The constitutionality of Acts of Parliament and treaties shall not be reviewed by the courts.

English translation, Constitute Project.

Context. Dutch courts may not strike down an Act of Parliament for breaching the constitution. They can, and do, test laws against binding international treaties such as the European Convention on Human Rights. Proposals to lift the ban have been made repeatedly; a cabinet bill to allow review for classic rights went to the Council of State in June 2026, according to Parlement.com.

What it tells you. In one of Europe's oldest democracies the constitution is enforced by Parliament itself rather than by judges. Any change needs two readings, the second by a two-thirds majority after an election, so the ban remains in force as of November 2026.

§14 France Art 16 1789 In force

Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) · Rights

A definition of a constitution, still law

Any society in which no provision is made for guaranteeing rights or for the separation of powers, has no Constitution.

English translation, Conseil constitutionnel.

Context. Adopted on 26 August 1789, the Declaration is not a museum piece. The preamble of the 1958 constitution refers to it, and the Conseil constitutionnel treats it as part of the body of constitutional law it enforces.

What it tells you. The article sets a test that many documents called constitutions would fail. A text that creates institutions but guarantees no rights or concentrates all power is, on this definition, not a constitution at all.

§15 France Art 66-1 2007 In force

Constitution of 4 October 1958 · Rights

Abolition, made constitutional

No one shall be sentenced to death.

English translation, Conseil constitutionnel. French: “Nul ne peut être condamné à la peine de mort.”

Context. France abolished the death penalty by statute in 1981. In February 2007 Parliament, meeting as a Congress at Versailles, wrote the ban into the constitution; the Death Penalty Information Center reported the vote as 828 to 26. The constitutional law is dated 23 February 2007.

What it tells you. Putting abolition in the constitution means a future government could not restore executions with an ordinary law.

§16 Sweden Whole ordinance 1766 In force

Freedom of the Press Ordinance (1766) · Rights

The right to read the government's papers, from 1766

No single clause to quote. See the note below.

No short English passage verified; summary from the Riksdag's own history.

Context. The ordinance of 2 December 1766 abolished censorship except for theological writing and gave the public access to official documents, including the right to copy them from the archives. Its author is usually given as Anders Chydenius, a priest from what is now Finland. Gustav III curtailed it after his 1772 coup; its successor, the Freedom of the Press Act of 1949, is one of Sweden's four fundamental laws today.

What it tells you. The Riksdag calls it the first of its kind in the world and UNESCO's information centre calls it the world's first government-sponsored declaration of press freedom; those are claims made by the institutions concerned. Public access to official documents remains a constitutional right in both Sweden and Finland.

§17 United Kingdom Chapter XXIX 1297 In force

Magna Carta (1297 statute) · Rights

Still on the statute book, in the 1297 version

NO Freeman shall be taken or imprisoned, or be disseised of his Freehold, or Liberties, or free Customs, or be outlawed, or exiled, or any other wise destroyed; nor will We not pass upon him, nor [condemn him,] but by lawful judgment of his Peers, or by the Law of the Land.

Text as published on legislation.gov.uk.

Context. The Magna Carta in force in England and Wales is not the 1215 charter but Edward I's 1297 confirmation. Every chapter was repealed between 1828 and 1969 except chapter I, chapter IX on the liberties of London and chapter XXIX, along with a closing saving clause.

What it tells you. People quote “clause 39” from 1215 as living law. The law actually in force is chapter XXIX of the 1297 statute, which combines the old clauses 39 and 40 and ends: “We will sell to no man, we will not deny or defer to any man either Justice or Right.”

§18 Hungary National Avowal 2011 In force

Fundamental Law of Hungary (2011) · Religion and identity

A constitution that opens with a medieval king

We are proud that our king Saint Stephen built the Hungarian State on solid ground and made our country a part of Christian Europe one thousand years ago.

English translation for information, Ministry of Justice.

Context. Parliament adopted the Fundamental Law on 18 April 2011 and it came into force on 1 January 2012, replacing the heavily amended communist-era constitution of 1949. Its preamble, the National Avowal, is written in the first person plural and moves through Saint Stephen, Christianity and the nation's forebears before it reaches rights.

What it tells you. Rights arrive only after this account of national and Christian history, so the preamble tells readers how the rest of the text is meant to be read.

§19 Portugal Preamble 1976 In force

Constitution of the Portuguese Republic (1976) · Historical bargains

Socialism, still in the preamble of an EU member

The Constituent Assembly affirms the Portuguese people's decision to defend national independence, guarantee citizens' fundamental rights, establish the basic principles of democracy, ensure the primacy of a democratic state based on the rule of law and open up a path towards a socialist society…

Official English text, Assembleia da República (Seventh Revision, 2005).

Context. The constitution was written two years after the Carnation Revolution of 1974 ended the dictatorship. Seven revisions have stripped out most of its revolutionary economics, but the preamble's “path towards a socialist society” survives. In May 2026 Chega filed a revision bill that would delete it; the process was paused in June with new deadlines for proposals running to December 2026.

What it tells you. Preambles are hard to touch because they record the moment of founding. Portugal's has outlived the economic programme it once introduced.

§20 United States Art I, s9, cl1 1787 Repealed or lapsed

Constitution of the United States (1787) · Historical bargains

A protected slave trade, with an expiry date

The Migration or Importation of such Persons as any of the States now existing shall think proper to admit, shall not be prohibited by the Congress prior to the Year one thousand eight hundred and eight…

National Archives transcription.

Context. The clause never says “slave”. It barred Congress from banning the Atlantic slave trade for twenty years, and Article V shielded it from amendment until 1808. Congress passed a ban on 2 March 1807, effective from 1 January 1808, the first day it could.

What it tells you. It is the plainest example in the text of a bargain struck to keep the slave states in the Union. The words remain in the Constitution but have had no effect since 1808.

§21 United States Art I, s9, cl8 1787 In force

Constitution of the United States (1787) · Offices and terms

No titles, and no gifts from kings

No Title of Nobility shall be granted by the United States: And no Person holding any Office of Profit or Trust under them, shall, without the Consent of the Congress, accept of any present, Emolument, Office, or Title, of any kind whatever, from any King, Prince, or foreign State.

National Archives transcription.

Context. The second half, the foreign emoluments clause, was the basis of lawsuits against President Trump during his first term. The Supreme Court ordered two of them dismissed as moot in January 2021, so, as the Congressional Research Service notes, they produced no definitive ruling on what the clause means.

What it tells you. A separate Titles of Nobility Amendment passed Congress in 1810 and was never ratified. A conspiracy theory holds that it was secretly ratified as a “missing 13th Amendment”; the National Archives says it was not.

§22 United States 27th Amendment 1992 In force

Constitution of the United States · Offices and terms

Proposed in 1789, ratified in 1992

No law, varying the compensation for the services of the Senators and Representatives, shall take effect, until an election of Representatives shall have intervened.

National Archives transcription.

Context. Congress proposed James Madison's amendment on 25 September 1789, and it stalled. In 1982 Gregory Watson, a University of Texas student, wrote a paper arguing it was still live, received a C, and began a letter-writing campaign. Michigan's ratification on 7 May 1992 was thought to supply the 38th state; the Archivist certified the amendment on 18 May. The grade was changed to an A in 2017.

What it tells you. It established that an amendment with no deadline can be ratified centuries later, an argument now used about other old proposals, including the Corwin Amendment.

§23 United States Corwin Amendment 1861 Never ratified

Proposed amendment (1861) · Historical bargains

The amendment that would have protected slavery

No amendment shall be made to the Constitution which will authorize or give to Congress the power to abolish or interfere, within any State, with the domestic institutions thereof, including that of persons held to labor or service by the laws of said State.

Text as quoted in Illinois and Rhode Island legislative resolutions and by the National Archives.

Context. Congress passed it in the last days before Lincoln's inauguration: the Senate's 24 to 12 on 2 March 1861 was exactly the two-thirds needed. It was sent to the states with no deadline. Ohio, Maryland and Illinois later rescinded their ratifications, and Rhode Island did so in June 2026; the Rhode Island legislature noted that this leaves Kentucky as the only state whose ratification stands.

What it tells you. It would have become the Thirteenth Amendment. The Thirteenth that was actually ratified, in 1865, abolished slavery instead. Because there is no deadline, it is technically still before the states, as Rhode Island's 2026 resolution itself notes.

§24 United States 18th Amendment, s1 1919 Repealed or lapsed

Constitution of the United States · Rights

Prohibition, and the only amendment ever repealed

After one year from the ratification of this article the manufacture, sale, or transportation of intoxicating liquors within, the importation thereof into, or the exportation thereof from the United States… for beverage purposes is hereby prohibited.

National Archives transcription.

Context. Ratified on 16 January 1919, it was repealed by the 21st Amendment on 5 December 1933, whose first section reads in full: “The eighteenth article of amendment to the Constitution of the United States is hereby repealed.” The 21st is also the only amendment ratified by state conventions rather than legislatures.

What it tells you. Prohibition never banned drinking or possession; it banned making, selling and moving alcohol “for beverage purposes”. Both amendments remain printed in the Constitution, one cancelling the other.

§25 United States (Arkansas) Art 19, s1 1874 In the text, not applied

Constitution of Arkansas (1874) · Religion and identity

A religious test that cannot be enforced

No person who denies the being of a God shall hold any office in the civil departments of this State, nor be competent to testify as a witness in any Court.

Text as quoted by Religion News Service and the Freedom From Religion Foundation.

Context. In Torcaso v. Watkins (1961) the US Supreme Court struck down a Maryland requirement that officeholders declare belief in God, which made all such tests unenforceable. PolitiFact counts seven state constitutions that still contain one: Arkansas, Maryland, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee and Texas.

What it tells you. Texts outlive the law. These clauses cannot be applied, but removing them needs a state constitutional amendment, and attempts in Arkansas, such as one in 2009, have not succeeded.

§26 United States (Texas) Art 1, s1 1876 In force

Constitution of Texas (1876) · State power

“Free and independent”, inside the Union

Texas is a free and independent State, subject only to the Constitution of the United States, and the maintenance of our free institutions and the perpetuity of the Union depend upon the preservation of the right of local self-government, unimpaired to all the States.

Texas Legislative Council.

Context. The clause is often cited as evidence that Texas reserved a right to leave. It says the opposite: Texas is “subject only to the Constitution of the United States” and it speaks of “the perpetuity of the Union”. In Texas v. White, decided at the December 1868 term, the Supreme Court called the Union “indestructible” and Texas's ordinance of secession “absolutely null”.

What it tells you. The Texas State Law Library calls the right to secede a “common misunderstanding”: Texas received no special terms when it joined the Union.

§27 Mexico Art 83 1917 In force

Political Constitution of the United Mexican States (1917) · Offices and terms

One six-year term, never again

El ciudadano que haya desempeñado el cargo de Presidente de la República, electo popularmente, o con el carácter de interino o sustituto, o asuma provisionalmente la titularidad del Ejecutivo Federal, en ningún caso y por ningún motivo podrá volver a desempeñar ese puesto.

Official Spanish. Desk translation: “The citizen who has held the office of President of the Republic, whether popularly elected or as interim or substitute, or who provisionally assumes the Federal Executive, may in no case and for no reason hold that office again.”

Context. No re-election was a rallying cry of the revolution against Porfirio Díaz's long rule. The article has been reformed several times, most recently in 2014. A 1927 reform briefly allowed a non-consecutive second term, for Álvaro Obregón; the lifelong bar was restored in 1933 and has been kept since. A 2014 reform allowed legislators to seek consecutive re-election; a 2025 reform reverses that from the 2030 elections.

What it tells you. The ban covers interim and substitute presidents as well as elected ones, and it is lifelong, not just consecutive.

§28 Costa Rica Art 12 1949 In force

Political Constitution of Costa Rica (1949) · State power

The country that abolished its army

The Army as a permanent institution is proscribed. For the vigilance and conservation of the public order, there will be the necessary forces of police.

English translation, Constitute Project. Spanish: “Se proscribe el Ejército como institución permanente.”

Context. On 1 December 1948, after a short civil war, José Figueres struck a wall of the Bellavista barracks with a sledgehammer to mark the army's abolition. The barracks has housed the National Museum since 1949, the year the constituent assembly wrote the ban into Article 12.

What it tells you. The ban is not absolute: the same article allows military forces to be organised under a continental agreement or for national defence, always under civilian control. Costa Rica has kept police forces but no standing army for more than 75 years.

§29 Brazil Art 14, §1 1988 In force

Constitution of the Federative Republic of Brazil (1988) · Duties

Voting as a constitutional duty

Voter registration and voting are: I - compulsory for persons over eighteen years of age; II - optional for: a) the illiterate; b) those over seventy years of age; c) those over sixteen and under eighteen years of age.

English translation as quoted by the Comparative Constitutions Project.

Context. The 1988 constitution was written after two decades of military rule. It made voting compulsory for adults, while extending the optional vote to 16 and 17-year-olds and to people who cannot read. The electoral court says absent voters who give no justification face a fine, and registration can be cancelled after three missed elections.

What it tells you. Many countries compel voting by ordinary law; Brazil puts the duty in the constitution itself, alongside an unusually young optional voting age.

§30 Ecuador Art 71 2008 In force

Constitution of Ecuador (2008) · Nature and land

Rights for nature itself

Nature, or Pacha Mama, where life is reproduced and occurs, has the right to integral respect for its existence and for the maintenance and regeneration of its life cycles, structure, functions and evolutionary processes.

English translation, Constitute Project.

Context. The Rachel Carson Center calls Ecuador's 2008 text the first national constitution to recognise rights of nature. The clause has teeth: in the Los Cedros case of 10 November 2021 a seven-judge majority of the Constitutional Court revoked mining permits in a protected cloud forest, finding violations of nature's rights.

What it tells you. Nature becomes a rights-holder rather than property, and any person, community, people or nation may call on the authorities to enforce its rights. The Andean term Pacha Mama sits in the legal text alongside the Spanish.

§31 Cuba Art 4 2019 In force

Constitution of the Republic of Cuba (2019) · State power

A system declared permanent

The socialist system that this Constitution supports is irrevocable.

English translation, Constitute Project.

Context. The idea predates the 2019 constitution. In June 2002, after statements by the US government that May, Cuba amended its 1976 constitution to declare socialism “irrevocable” and that “Cuba shall never return to capitalism”. The 2019 text kept it, and Article 229 says the irrevocability may never be amended. Article 4 also gives citizens a right to armed combat, when other means are not available, against anyone trying to overthrow the order.

What it tells you. It is an eternity clause in the service of a single economic system, the mirror image of the German provision that protects democracy itself.

§32 Liberia Art 27(b) 1986 In force

Constitution of the Republic of Liberia (1986) · Rights

Citizenship defined by race

In order to preserve, foster and maintain the positive Liberian culture, values and character, only persons who are Negroes or of Negro descent shall qualify by birth or by naturalization to be citizens of Liberia.

Official English text, as reproduced by the Citizenship Rights in Africa Initiative.

Context. Liberia was founded in the 19th century by settlers from the United States, many of them formerly enslaved. The clause covers both birth and naturalisation, so long-term residents who are not of African descent can never become citizens. In March 2026 the former president Ellen Johnson Sirleaf called it a “racist provision”; in April the House began debating a referendum package that would remove it.

What it tells you. Removing it needs two-thirds of both houses and two-thirds of voters in a referendum. As of the latest reports found, in April 2026, no amendment had passed.

§33 Bhutan Art 5(3) 2008 In force

Constitution of the Kingdom of Bhutan (2008) · Nature and land

A forest quota, for all time

The Government shall ensure that, in order to conserve the country's natural resources and to prevent degradation of the ecosystem, a minimum of sixty percent of Bhutan's total land shall be maintained under forest cover for all time.

Official English text, via Constitute Project.

Context. Bhutan's government reported forest cover of 69.7% in its 2023 National Forest Inventory, down from 71% in 2015 but well above the constitutional floor.

§34 Bhutan Art 9(2) 2008 In force

Constitution of the Kingdom of Bhutan (2008) · Duties

Gross National Happiness, carefully worded

The State shall strive to promote those conditions that will enable the pursuit of Gross National Happiness.

Official English text, via Constitute Project.

Context. The constitution was signed on 18 July 2008, as Bhutan moved from absolute to constitutional monarchy. Gross National Happiness was already the country's stated development philosophy; the constitution made it a principle of state policy.

What it tells you. The wording is often summarised as a constitutional mandate for happiness. It promises less and is more interesting: the state must “strive to promote” the conditions in which people can pursue it.

Constitution of the Kingdom of Bhutan (2008) · Offices and terms

A king with a retirement age

Upon reaching the age of sixty-five years, the Druk Gyalpo shall step down…

§36 Japan Art 9 1946 In force

Constitution of Japan (1946) · State power

War renounced, and never amended

Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes.

Official English translation, House of Representatives.

Context. The second paragraph adds that “land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained.” Japan nonetheless has the Self-Defense Forces. In 2014 the cabinet reinterpreted Article 9 to permit limited collective self-defence, and laws putting that into effect came into force in March 2016. The constitution, in force since 1947, has never been amended.

What it tells you. The meaning of Article 9 has changed through interpretation rather than amendment. Revision needs two-thirds of both houses and a referendum; as of April 2026 the governing bloc had pledged to move towards it but lacked two-thirds in the upper house.

§37 India Art 17 1950 In force

Constitution of India (1950) · Rights

A social practice abolished by name

“Untouchability” is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden. The enforcement of any disability arising out of “Untouchability” shall be an offence punishable in accordance with law.

English is an authoritative text of the Constitution; wording via constitutionofindia.net.

Context. The Constituent Assembly adopted the article on 29 November 1948, when it was draft Article 11, and rejected an amendment to define the term. Parliament gave it force with the Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955, renamed the Protection of Civil Rights Act in 1976. The word appears in quotation marks in the official text.

What it tells you. Most constitutional rights restrain the state. This one also targets the conduct of private individuals, making a centuries-old social practice a crime.

A constitutional duty to be scientific

It shall be the duty of every citizen of India— … to develop the scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform;

Wording via constitutionofindia.net.

§39 South Africa Postamble 1993 Repealed or lapsed

Interim Constitution (Act 200 of 1993) · Historical bargains

The bargain that made amnesty possible

These can now be addressed on the basis that there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation.

Original English, as quoted by the Constitutional Court in AZAPO v President (1996).

Context. The closing section, headed “National Unity and Reconciliation”, required that amnesty be granted for politically motivated offences of the conflict, with a cut-off date before 6 December 1993. The interim constitution was replaced when the 1996 Constitution came into force on 4 February 1997.

What it tells you. The text is repealed, but the institution it made possible, the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, shaped how South Africa and many other countries have handled the aftermath of conflict.

§40 South Africa s9(3) 1996 In force

Constitution of the Republic of South Africa (1996) · Rights

Sexual orientation, named in a bill of rights

The state may not unfairly discriminate directly or indirectly against anyone on one or more grounds, including race, gender, sex, pregnancy, marital status, ethnic or social origin, colour, sexual orientation…

Official English text, via the Constitutional Court.

Context. The protection first appeared in the interim constitution, in force from 27 April 1994, and was carried into the 1996 text. South Africa's Constitutional Court says the country was the first to enshrine gay rights in its constitution; the legal scholar David Bilchitz, writing for International IDEA, makes the same claim for the 1993 text.

What it tells you. The clause became the basis for the court's later rulings, including on same-sex marriage. Including sexual orientation in the founding list of protected grounds was a deliberate break with apartheid-era law.

§41 Lebanon Art 95 1990 In the text, not applied

Constitution of Lebanon (1926, amended 1990) · Historical bargains

A promise to dismantle the system it describes

The Chamber of Deputies that is elected on the basis of equality between Muslims and Christians shall take the appropriate measures to bring about the abolition of political confessionalism according to a transitional plan.

English text, Constitutional Council of Lebanon. Translations differ: Constitute Project renders it as eliminating “political sectarianism”.

Context. The article was rewritten in 1990 under the Taif Agreement that ended the civil war. It shares out parliament equally between Muslims and Christians while ordering a national committee, chaired by the president, to propose how to abolish religious quotas in politics. The Heinrich Böll Stiftung reported in 2023 that no such committee had ever been formed.

What it tells you. The constitution entrenches power-sharing by religion and, in the same breath, describes it as temporary. More than three decades on, the transitional plan has not begun.

A river made a legal person, by statute

Te Awa Tupua is a legal person and has all the rights, powers, duties, and liabilities of a legal person.

Official text, New Zealand Legislation.

§43 Nepal Art 4, explanation 2015 In force

Constitution of Nepal (2015) · Religion and identity

A secular state with a qualifier

For the purposes of this Article, “secular” means religious, cultural freedoms, including protection of religion, culture handed down from the time immemorial.

English text hosted by WIPO Lex. Constitute Project's unofficial translation differs in wording.

Context. Nepal was officially a Hindu kingdom until 2006, when parliament declared it secular; the monarchy was abolished in 2008. What it tells you. A single definitional sentence can change what a headline word means. Nepal's “secular” is not the French or American version, and translations disagree about how to render it.

The State shall guarantee equal access to opportunities for public service and prohibit political dynasties as may be defined by law.

§45 China Art 79 2018 In force

Constitution of the People's Republic of China (1982) · Offices and terms

A term limit deleted in 2018

The president and the vice president of the People's Republic of China shall have the same term of office as that of the National People's Congress.

English translation, State Council.

Context. Until 11 March 2018 the article added that the president and vice-president should serve no more than two consecutive terms. The National People's Congress removed the limit in a package of amendments approved by 2,958 votes to 2, with 3 abstentions, according to the Congressional Research Service.

What it tells you. The change applied to the state presidency only; the Communist Party's general secretary, the more powerful post, had never had a constitutional term limit. It shows how much a constitution can change by deleting a single clause.

§46 United States “Separation of church and state” 1802 Misquoted or misattributed

Not in the Constitution · Religion and identity

The famous phrase is Jefferson's, not the Constitution's

Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof;

What the First Amendment actually says. National Archives transcription.

Context. The phrase comes from Thomas Jefferson's letter of 1 January 1802 to the Danbury Baptist Association, which said the First Amendment built “a wall of separation between Church & State”. What it tells you. Courts have used Jefferson's metaphor to interpret the First Amendment, but the words themselves appear nowhere in the Constitution.

§47 United States “Life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness” 1776 Misquoted or misattributed

Not in the Constitution · Rights

The phrase is in the Declaration, not the Constitution

…that among these are Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.

From the Declaration of Independence. National Archives transcription.

Context. The line is from the Declaration of Independence of 1776, a statement of reasons for separation from Britain, not a governing document. The Constitution's Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments protect “life, liberty, or property”.

What it tells you. Happiness does not appear in the Constitution's text. The swap from happiness to property is one of the more telling differences between a revolutionary declaration and a working legal framework.

Bolivia's Mother Earth rights are statutes

Not in the Texas Constitution · State power

New States, of convenient size, not exceeding four in number, in addition to said State of Texas, and having sufficient population, may hereafter, by the consent of said State, be formed out of the territory thereof…

§50 United Kingdom “Britain has no constitution” — Misquoted or misattributed

No single document · State power

Britain has a constitution; it is not written in one place

The United Kingdom does not have a codified constitution.

House of Commons Library, The United Kingdom constitution: a mapping exercise.

Context. The House of Commons Library quotes the Cabinet Manual: “There is no single document that describes, establishes or regulates the structures of the state”. The constitution is spread across statutes such as the surviving chapters of Magna Carta, court decisions and conventions.
