Explainer · six dossiers

How Economies Remember

What economists have found about why some places stay richer or poorer for centuries, what that evidence can and can’t prove, and six regions where the past still shows in today’s incomes.

By Nick Carbone · 26 November 2026 · about 17 minutes

Somewhere in the Peruvian Andes there is a line on the map that nobody alive drew. On one side of it, Spanish colonial officials once required villages to send a share of their men to the silver mines of Potosí and the mercury mines of Huancavelica. On the other side, they did not. The system ended in 1812. Two centuries later, households on the conscripted side consume about a quarter less, and their children are measurably more likely to be stunted.12

That finding, published by the economist Melissa Dell in 2010, belongs to a body of research sometimes called the economics of persistence. Its question is old: why are some places rich and others poor, and why does the ranking change so slowly? Its method is newer. Instead of comparing whole countries, which differ in a thousand ways at once, researchers hunt for borders, routes and accidents that split otherwise similar places into two groups, then measure what happened to each group over hundreds of years.

The results can be startling. Medieval trading ports, colonial land laws, a railway laid across East Africa and the kind of plough a society once used all appear to leave marks on incomes, schooling and even attitudes that can be read today. The research also has serious, unresolved problems, which this piece sets out after the case files.

Part one

Four ways the past gets into the ground

The studies are varied, but most of them trace a historical event through one of a few channels. Think of them as the kinds of rock a borehole can pass through.

Layer · trade

Routes and ports

Trade creates places where strangers have reasons to cooperate, and the rules they invent to do it can outlast the trade itself. Saumitra Jha studied towns in India that were medieval ports on the Indian Ocean, where Muslim merchants held particular advantages in shipping and Hindu and Muslim traders depended on each other.17

Those ports were more ethnically mixed, yet between 1850 and 1950 they were five times less prone to Hindu–Muslim riots than other towns, and from 1950 to 1995 they were still half as prone. Jha links the difference to local institutions that grew up to support trade between the communities.17

Layer · institutions

Colonial rules

One of the most cited papers in the field is Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson’s 2001 argument that Europeans set up very different colonies depending on whether they could survive in them. Where settlers died in large numbers, they were more likely to build extractive institutions; where they could settle, they built institutions that protected property.13

Using historical death rates among soldiers, bishops and sailors as their lever, the authors estimated that institutions explain about three-quarters of the income gaps between former colonies.13 That number has been fiercely contested (see part three). Nathan Nunn, working on a different channel, found a robust negative relationship between the number of people taken from an African country in the slave trades and its economic performance today.15

Layer · infrastructure

Lines and junctions

Transport decides where people gather, and once they have gathered, the gathering becomes its own reason to stay. Cities that grew up at a portage or a railhead keep their people after the river traffic stops or the trains run less often.

Economists call this path dependence: an advantage that has disappeared still decides the map, because the houses, roads, firms and workers are already in place and it would cost too much to coordinate a move somewhere else. Two of the dossiers below, on the American fall line and on Kenya, are about exactly this.57

Layer · farming

What was planted, and how

The Danish economist Ester Boserup argued that societies which farmed with the plough, a heavy tool needing upper-body strength, came to see field work as men’s work. Alberto Alesina, Paola Giuliano and Nathan Nunn tested the idea in 2013.16

Descendants of societies that traditionally used the plough have lower rates of female participation in work, politics and entrepreneurship today, and hold attitudes more favourable to gender inequality. The pattern shows up even among the American-born children of immigrants, which suggests that something cultural travelled with the families.16

Dossier 01 · forced labour

The mita line

Southern Peru · the boundary of the colonial mining draft
≈25%lower household consumption in districts once subject to the mita
≈6 ptshigher prevalence of stunted growth in children
Deep layer
From 1573 to 1812, the Spanish required about 200 communities in the region to send one-seventh of their workers to the mines. In any given year, around 3 per cent of all adult men in Peru were conscripted.2
How it was carried
Dell finds that the mita districts historically had fewer large landowners and lower educational attainment. Today they are less integrated into road networks, and residents are substantially more likely to be subsistence farmers. Her reading is that the effect ran through land tenure and the provision of public goods.1
Why it’s convincing
The comparison is made across the mita boundary itself, between places that are otherwise alike in geography and altitude, so it does not rest on comparing the Andes with the coast.1
Method: regression discontinuity at a colonial border

Dossier 02 · extraction that built things

Java’s sugar factories

Java, Indonesia · the Dutch Cultivation System
94sugar-processing factories built by the Dutch from the 1830s
+1 yrmore schooling, on average, near the old factories
2×railway density within 1 km of a factory, compared with places 5–20 km away
Deep layer
From the 1830s to the 1870s the colonial government forced villages to grow sugar cane for 94 factories. At one point in the mid-nineteenth century, sugar provided a third of the Dutch government’s revenues and 4 per cent of Dutch GDP.4
How it was carried
The factories needed roads, railways and processing, and those stayed. Areas near the old factories are today 25 to 30 percentage points less agricultural, more industrialised and better educated than similar places that could have hosted a factory but did not. Villages forced to grow cane also have more village-owned land and more schools.34
Why it matters
This is the awkward counterpart to the mita. Both were coercive colonial systems; one left places poorer and the other, on these measures, left them richer. Dell and Olken’s point is that what the extraction built locally matters as much as the coercion.3
Method: spatial regression discontinuity around factory catchments

Dossier 03 · path dependence

The fall line

Southern and Mid-Atlantic United States · Richmond, Augusta, Columbus, Macon
c.1855commercial river traffic at Augusta and Columbus has essentially ended, a few decades after the railways arrived
Deep layer
The fall line is the boundary between the Piedmont and the coastal plain, marking the last falls or rapids on a river before it reaches the Atlantic or the Gulf of Mexico. Boats had to stop there and goods were hauled overland, so commerce and services gathered at the portage. Where the falls could power machinery, manufacturing followed.56
How it was carried
Canals, locks and then railways in the early and middle 1800s removed the need to unload at the falls.6 Hoyt Bleakley and Jeffrey Lin show that the historical portage sites nonetheless remain important concentrations of people, long after their original advantages became obsolete.5
Why it’s convincing
The falls are a fact of geology, and they stopped mattering for transport long ago. That makes them a cleaner test than most: anything that persists cannot be explained by the falls still being useful.
Method: natural experiment from geography

Dossier 04 · cities that outlived their reason

The Uganda Railway

Kenya · Mombasa to Nairobi to Kisumu on Lake Victoria
5 → 247localities over 2,000 people, 1901 and 2009
1896–1901main line built from the coast to Lake Victoria
Deep layer
When construction began, Mombasa was Kenya’s largest town, with about 15,000 inhabitants. Nairobi began as a railway depot in 1899. Branch lines followed until 1930; none was built after that.8
How it was carried
Rémi Jedwab, Edward Kerby and Alexander Moradi find that the railway decided where European settlers went, and the settlers decided where the main cities were at independence. After 1963 the railways declined, through mismanagement and the spread of sealed roads, and the settlers left. The cities stayed, and those places remain more urban and developed today.78
Why it matters
The authors argue the persistence is not explained by today’s roads. The railway towns kept growing because their early start coordinated where later investment went, which is path dependence in a developing country rather than in nineteenth-century America.8
Method: comparison of rail and non-rail locations over time

Dossier 05 · who collected the rent

Landlord districts

India · Bengal, Bihar, Orissa and parts of Madras against the cultivator districts
23%higher average wheat yields in non-landlord districts
40%lower infant mortality in non-landlord districts
Deep layer
Land revenue made up 60 per cent of the British government’s revenue in India in 1841. In some regions the British collected it through landlords, whose obligations were fixed “in perpetuity” under the Permanent Settlement of 1793. Elsewhere, following arguments made by Sir Thomas Munro in Madras, they dealt directly with individual cultivators.10
How it was carried
Abhijit Banerjee and Lakshmi Iyer find that former landlord areas had significantly lower agricultural investment and productivity after independence, and lower investment in health and education. They argue the difference probably arose because the old institutions led to different policy choices.9
Why it’s convincing
The choice of system was not random, so the authors use timing: districts that came under British revenue control between 1820 and 1856 were much more likely to get the cultivator system, for reasons that had to do with fashions in colonial administration rather than the land itself.10
Method: instrumental variable from the date of conquest

Dossier 06 · civic memory

The free cities

Northern Italy · the medieval communes
+25%more voluntary associations in cities that were once free city-states
≥50%of the North–South gap in social capital attributed to the South’s lack of free cities
Deep layer
In the eleventh and twelfth centuries, many northern Italian cities won self-government. The study takes 1176, when the Lombard League defeated the Emperor Frederick I, as one reference date and 1300, when the movement was at its height, as another. In the South, Norman rule prevented city-states from forming.1112
How it was carried
Luigi Guiso, Paola Sapienza and Luigi Zingales test an idea of the political scientist Robert Putnam: that self-government bred a civic culture passed down through families. They measure it today with the number of non-profit organisations, the presence of an organ-donation association and the rate of cheating in a national maths exam, and find former free cities score better on all three than similar cities nearby. Schoolchildren in former free cities also report stronger beliefs in their own ability to change things.12
Why it’s debated
Culture is the hardest channel to pin down, because it overlaps with income, education and politics. The authors use features such as whether a city had a bishop in the year 1000 as instruments for later self-rule.11
Method: within-North comparison with historical instruments

Part three

What the borehole can’t tell you

Persistence studies have become a fixture of the top economics journals, and their critics are mostly economists too. The problems they raise are specific.

  1. The data underneath can be thin

    David Albouy re-examined the settler-mortality figures behind the 2001 colonial-origins paper. He found that 36 of the 64 countries in the sample were assigned death rates taken from other countries, often on mistaken or conflicting evidence, and that rates from labourers, bishops and soldiers, often on campaign, had been combined in a way that favoured the hypothesis. Once he controlled for those issues, the key relationship lost robustness and the estimates became unreliable.14 The original authors published a reply disputing his critique, and the argument has not been settled.20

  2. Maps flatter statistics

    Places close to each other tend to resemble each other on almost everything. Morgan Kelly, an economist at University College Dublin, argued in 2019 that many persistence regressions suffer severe spatial autocorrelation, which can inflate their apparent significance by orders of magnitude. In a striking test, he found that replacing the historical variable with random spatial noise often fitted the data as well or better.18 In a later paper he proposed judging each historical variable by whether it beats synthetic noise with the same spatial pattern, and applied the test to twenty published studies.19 Kelly’s critique does not say every finding is wrong. It says the stated precision of many of them cannot be taken at face value.

  3. A correlation over centuries is still a correlation

    The best studies, such as the mita and Java papers, compare places separated by a line drawn for reasons unrelated to their prospects. Most of the field cannot do that, and has to argue that nothing else explains the pattern. Banerjee and Iyer, for example, use the timing of British conquest because the choice of land system was not random.10 Each of those arguments can be challenged.

  4. Knowing that is easier than knowing why

    Even a clean result says little about the mechanism. The Kenyan cities could persist through buildings, networks of firms or simply people’s expectations about where others will be. The Italian result could be culture, or the institutions that culture produced. The authors generally offer a preferred story; the data often fit several.

  5. Memory is not destiny

    The same coercive colonial project left Peruvian districts poorer and Javanese ones richer. Persistence is a tendency, measured on average across many places, and plenty of places break it. A study showing that history weighs on a region today is also a study of how much else must have mattered to account for everything history does not explain.
