01

Why sound is the weak link

What makes audio harder to check than pictures

On 5 June 2023, listeners to Mir, a Moscow-based public broadcaster, in three Russian regions bordering Ukraine heard what sounded like Vladimir Putin announcing that Ukrainian forces had crossed the border, that martial law was in force and that residents should evacuate deeper into Russia.2122 The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, said the broadcast was a hack. Within hours the story was being told around the world as a "deepfake Putin". Nobody had shown that it was.22

That episode, which this manual works through in detail in section 04, contains most of the problems a newsroom faces with audio during a breaking event. A real channel carried a false message. The main confirmation came from the people with the most reason to deny it. And the most widely repeated label for what had happened ran ahead of the evidence.

Audio is harder to verify than pictures for plain practical reasons. A voice carries no landmarks to geolocate, no shadows to measure and no street signs to read. A clip can be a few seconds of speech laid over a still photograph, which is how a fabricated conversation spread in Slovakia two days before its 2023 parliamentary election.2425 And the cost of a convincing imitation has collapsed. In January 2023 Microsoft researchers described VALL-E, a speech model that could mimic a voice from a three-second sample; Microsoft did not release it publicly.3

Detection software will not settle the question for you. Treat it as one opinion among several.

Automated detectors exist, and a desk under pressure will be tempted to paste a clip into one and take the score. A 2025 guide for journalists from Columbia University's Tow Center found that detection systems often fail on fakes made with techniques absent from their training data, and cited a 2023 review concluding that the ability of audio deepfake detectors to generalise "is still poor".4 Britain's Full Fact, when it rated a fake clip of London's mayor in 2024, said it does not cite AI-detection tools at all, because they have not been consistently reliable.5

What does work is the discipline verification desks have used for years on pictures and text, applied to sound. First Draft, the verification non-profit, reduced it to five questions.1

Q1

Provenance

Is this the original recording, or a copy, clip or re-upload?

Q2

Source

Who recorded it, or which channel aired it?

Q3

Date

When was it recorded or broadcast?

Q4

Location

Where was it captured, or where was it transmitted?

Q5

Motivation

Why was it made, and why is it reaching you now?

The five pillars come from First Draft's guide Verifying Online Information (2020).1 The first four match the checklist Claire Wardle set out in the European Journalism Centre's Verification Handbook, which also warns that dating content is "one of the most difficult elements of verification".2

02

Eight procedures

Run them in order; stop and hold if one fails

P1Provenance

Find the first copy

  • Work back from the clip you were sent to the earliest upload you can find. Reposts lose context, trim the start and end, and get re-captioned.
  • Search for distinctive phrases from the audio, and run reverse image searches on any picture or video frame attached to it. The Verification Handbook recommends reverse image search for exactly this: finding out whether the material was posted before.2
  • Check platform timestamps against time zones before concluding that a clip predates an event. Wardle notes that YouTube upload times are shown in US Pacific time, which can make an upload appear to come before the thing it shows.2

A clip with a still image and burnt-in subtitles, like the Slovak fake, gives you almost nothing to trace except the account that posted it first.24 That account is the lead.

P2Source

Reach the person who pressed record

  • The goal, in Wardle's words, is to "identify the original uploader and get in touch with them".2
  • Ask where they were standing, what they could see and what device they used. Direct questions often reveal that someone did not make the recording themselves.2
  • For a recording that claims to capture a known speaker, compare it with verified recordings of that person: tone, accent, pace and whether the content is in character.6

An eyewitness who cannot describe their surroundings, or whose account changes between calls, is telling you something about the recording.

P3Channel

Confirm the channel was what it claims to be

  • For a broadcast, check that the station really transmits on that frequency at that hour. International shortwave schedules change twice a year, for a summer and a winter season, and are coordinated through the High Frequency Co-ordination Conference, a non-profit association that is a member of the ITU's radiocommunication sector.7 Broadcasters publish their own schedules too.
  • Listen independently. Networks of remote receivers such as KiwiSDR and WebSDR let anyone tune a receiver in another country from a browser, free of charge.9 The receiver at the University of Twente in the Netherlands has been publicly available since April 2008 and covers 0 to 29 MHz.8
  • Remember what reception does and does not prove. Clear reception at one site is weak evidence of where a transmitter is: one Radio World columnist got his best reception of a Spanish broadcaster on a receiver in Newfoundland.9
  • Use the professionals. BBC Monitoring has translated and analysed foreign broadcasts since 26 August 1939 and covers media from about 150 countries in up to 100 languages.10 Big newsrooms now run their own verification units; BBC Verify launched in May 2023 with about 60 journalists.29

A genuine frequency or a genuine channel can still carry false content. In the Mir case the station was real and the message was not.22

P4Date

Check the clock

  • Convert every time you are given to UTC before comparing it with anything else.
  • If the audio comes with pictures, the sun can date them. Bellingcat's method compares the ratio of an object's height to its shadow's length with the sun's position on a simulator such as SunCalc. It needs a known date and place, a shadow on level ground, and a check of the shadow's direction, because each length occurs once in the morning and again in the afternoon. Give a time window, not a minute.11
  • Weather records, and other uploads from the same place and day, can confirm or rule out a claimed date.2
  • For a high-stakes recording, ask a forensic audio lab about electrical network frequency (ENF) analysis, explained below.
P5Sound

Listen like an examiner

  • Listen several times on good headphones before you open any tool. Investigators quoted by IJNet point to unnatural pauses, odd speech patterns and shaky pronunciation as signs of synthetic speech.6 Full Fact cited unnatural pauses and an odd turn of phrase in the fake Sadiq Khan clip.5
  • Open the file in a spectrogram view. Free software such as Audacity shows one, with level meters.6 Look for abrupt changes in the background, which can mark an edit.
  • The investigator Lawrence Abu Hamdan of Earshot notes that inserted audio may leave irregular breaks in the background electromagnetic interference.6
  • If you run a detector, find out what it was trained on and when it was last updated, and record its result as one input.4 A score of "70 per cent human" does not tell you what was altered.

Your ears and a spectrogram will catch crude edits. They will not reliably catch a good synthetic voice. When it matters, bring in a media-forensics specialist.6

P6Pictures

Check what came with the sound

  • Combat "footage" is often from a video game. Bohemia Interactive, maker of the military game Arma 3, lists the signs: deliberately low resolution, an exaggerated shaky camera, night scenes that hide detail, little or no sound, stiff human movement, on-screen game interface, smoke and fire that break into separate cloudlets, and vehicles or insignia that do not fit the conflict.14
  • Game footage often has little or no real sound. A clip with dramatic audio and no ambient noise deserves a second look.
P7Confirm

Call the people who would know

  • Ring the person heard on the recording, the broadcaster that carried it, and the police, fire or emergency service for the place it describes. Check their official channels for statements.
  • Treat every confirmation and denial as a claim with an interest behind it. Attribute it by name. The Kremlin's statement that the Mir broadcast was a hack remains the main official account; regional officials said the same.22
  • Emergency services post on social media too, and false information spreads fast during emergencies. The Verification Handbook's rule is to treat content as wrong until you have verified it.2
P8Publish

Publish with the doubt showing

  • Say what you checked, what you could not check, and who says what. "The Kremlin said the broadcast was a hack" is verifiable. "A deepfake Putin" was not.
  • Do not rebroadcast unverified audio in full on air. Describe it, or play a short excerpt clearly labelled as unverified.
  • Keep notes of every step and timestamp. You may need them for a correction, or for a regulator.
03

The verification flowchart

One page for the desk. Prints on A4.

START · P1

Have you found the earliest copy?

Original upload, broadcaster's own stream or recording, with its timestamp in UTC.

HOLDKeep tracing. Treat the clip as unverified and say so if you mention it.
P2

Can you identify and reach whoever recorded or aired it?

Their account of where, when and on what device holds together.

HOLDAttribute only to the account that posted it. Do not present it as eyewitness material.
P3

Does the channel check out?

The station is on that frequency at that hour; independent reception or the broadcaster's own output matches.

FLAGPossible spoof or hijack. Contact the broadcaster before going further.
P4

Does the time check out?

Time zones, daylight, weather and other uploads agree with the claimed date.

FLAGPossibly old or misdated material. Search for earlier versions.
P5 · P6

Does the sound, and any picture with it, hold up?

No audible edits or synthetic tells; spectrogram consistent; pictures are not game footage.

ESCALATESend to a forensic specialist. Detector scores are not a verdict.
P7

Have the people involved confirmed or denied it?

The speaker, the broadcaster, the emergency services. Every answer attributed.

CAUTIONNo response is not confirmation. Report what you know and what you do not.

Verified

Every step passed, independently. Publish, still showing your working.

Partly verified

The channel or time is confirmed, the content is not. Publish only with clear attribution and stated doubt.

Not verified

Any step failed. Hold, or report that unverified audio is circulating and say what is known about it.

Desk check sheet

Tick each step as it is done. The sheet suggests a status; the editor decides.

Steps completed

04

Case study: the Mir broadcast

5 June 2023 · Belgorod, Rostov and Voronezh regions, Russia

The broadcast that day reached television and radio outlets of Mir, a public broadcaster headquartered in Moscow, in three regions on the Ukrainian border.22 The voice, presented as Putin's, said Ukrainian forces had entered the Kursk, Belgorod and Bryansk regions, that martial law was being imposed there, that residents should "evacuate deep into Russia", and that a general mobilisation would follow.21 Here is how the flowchart handles it, using only what was public at the time and soon after.

  1. Earliest copy. The material reached the wider world as clips posted by listeners and viewers, and through reports attributed to the journalist Ksenia Sobchak and the Telegram channel 7x7.21 A desk outside Russia had second-hand copies and no broadcaster's own recording.
  2. Who aired it. The channel was real and identifiable: Mir's regional outlets.22 That passes the source test for the channel and tells you nothing yet about the speaker.
  3. Does the channel check out? This is where the flowchart flags it. A national emergency address that surfaces only on regional outlets in three regions is a warning sign in itself. Officials in Belgorod and Voronezh blamed hackers trying to "sow panic".21
  4. Does the time check out? The broadcast went out on Monday 5 June and the address was presented as current. No conflict, and no help.
  5. Does the sound hold up? Logically, the fact-checking company, found the footage "reasonably well executed", with small oddities such as wrinkles that seemed to disappear, and said there was not enough information to confirm that AI had been used.22 A trained impersonator or an edit of old speeches would have looked much the same to a newsroom. This step stays open.
  6. Confirmed or denied? Peskov told state media that hackers had aired the message and that the government had taken control of the affected broadcasters.22 The Kremlin denied the recording was authentic.21

Outcome on the flowchart: partly verified. The broadcast happened, on real channels, and the Kremlin said it was a hack. The content was false on the Kremlin's account. Who made it, and how, was not established. Logically called a Ukrainian state actor or a sympathetic third party the likely source, without proof.22

The accurate headline was available from the first hour: Russian regional broadcasts hijacked with fake Putin address, Kremlin says. The word "deepfake" added a technical claim nobody had checked.

It was not a one-off. Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty reported that a week later, on Russia Day, stations in the Stavropol and Krasnodar regions broadcast air-raid sirens and shelter warnings, and that intrusions of this kind were becoming common in regions near Ukraine.23 For a desk, a repeat pattern is a reason to slow down, because a second hijack is easier to believe than the first.

05

Case files

Five more incidents, and what each teaches

File A · Live TV hijack

The Zelensky "surrender"

When
16 March 2022
What
Hackers broke into the live broadcasts of Ukraine 24 and the Today channel to push a fake message in which President Volodymyr Zelensky appeared to tell Ukrainians to lay down their arms.20
Tells
A poorly made deepfake: Lead Stories noted that "Zelenskyy's neck is grossly out of proportion" compared with genuine images.19
Response
Zelensky posted his own video calling it a provocation; Meta removed the fake for breaking its manipulated-media policy.1920 Ukraine's Land Forces had warned on 2 March of a heightened risk of deepfakes.19

LESSONA prior warning and a fast, direct denial from the subject can travel faster than any forensic analysis.

File B · Election audio

The Slovak "rigging" call

When
28 September 2023, two days before the parliamentary election, during the pre-election moratorium.25
What
A fabricated conversation about electoral fraud between Michal Šimečka, leader of Progressive Slovakia, and Monika Tódová, a journalist at Denník N, played over a still image with subtitles.24
Debunk
Both denied it. AFP flagged it the same day, citing experts who found "numerous signs of manipulation" in the voices and speech patterns.2425 It began on Telegram and spread on Facebook and by email.24
Status
The creator has not been identified. Police twice declined or halted the case; after a complaint by Tódová, a third investigation was opened.25

LESSONTiming is part of the evidence. A clip released when the subjects are least able to respond is a reason for more caution.

File C · Phone audio

The New Hampshire robocall

When
21 January 2024, two days before the state's presidential primary.26
What
Calls using an AI-cloned voice imitating President Joe Biden, including his phrase "What a bunch of malarkey", urged thousands of residents not to vote in the primary. The calls were spoofed to appear to come from a local political figure.2726
Findings
The FCC proposed a $6 million fine against the political consultant Steve Kramer in May 2024 and imposed it that September; Lingo Telecom, which carried the calls, agreed to pay $1 million.2627 Kramer said he made the calls as a warning about AI. In June 2025 a New Hampshire jury acquitted him of all 11 voter-suppression and 11 impersonation charges.28

LESSONPhone audio has a carrier chain. The FCC's case set out the chain of companies that placed the calls.26

File D · Leaked "recording"

The Sadiq Khan clip

When
November 2023, ahead of a pro-Palestinian march planned for Armistice Day weekend; recirculated on TikTok in April 2024.5
What
A voice resembling the Mayor of London's appeared to dismiss Remembrance weekend and to claim "I control the Met Police".5
Findings
Full Fact found no evidence it was genuine and judged it likely to be a deepfake, citing unnatural pauses and an odd turn of phrase. The Metropolitan Police said the recording was not genuine and that it did not constitute a criminal offence.5

LESSON"Likely a deepfake" is an honest verdict. Proving synthesis is often impossible; proving there is no evidence of authenticity is not.

File E · Misattributed footage

When the war was a video game

2011
ITV's documentary Gaddafi and the IRA presented footage of a helicopter being attacked as an IRA attack. It came from the game ARMA 2. ITV blamed deadline pressure and "human error"; a compliance officer had raised concerns that producers did not follow up. Ofcom ruled the programme materially misleading.16
2022
A clip of the "Ghost of Kyiv", a supposed Ukrainian ace, shooting down a Russian jet spread on 25 February. It was made in Digital Combat Simulator and first posted to YouTube labelled as a simulation.17 On 30 April Ukraine's air force asked people to stop spreading fake news about the legend.18
2022
On 28 November Bohemia Interactive said Arma 3 clips were being passed off as footage from Ukraine and other conflicts, and that for every video taken down, "ten more are uploaded each day". It now works with fact-checkers including AFP and Reuters.1415

LESSONThe original upload often says exactly what the clip is. Provenance (P1) would have caught every one of these.

Glossary

Terms used on the desk

Chronolocation
Working out when an image or recording was made from evidence inside it, such as shadows, weather or visible events.11
Deepfake
Audio or video generated or altered by machine learning to show someone saying or doing something they did not. Use the word only when synthesis has been shown; otherwise say "fake" or "unverified".
ENF analysis
Electrical network frequency analysis: dating and checking the integrity of a recording by matching the mains hum it captured against a log of grid frequency.1213
HFCC
High Frequency Co-ordination Conference: a non-profit association that coordinates international shortwave schedules for each summer and winter season.7
Hijack
Unauthorised content inserted into a genuine broadcast, as on Ukraine 24 in 2022 and Mir in 2023.2022
Provenance
Whether you are looking at the original piece of content.1
SDR
Software-defined radio: a receiver whose tuning and demodulation are done in software, which allows receivers to be shared over the web.9
Spectrogram
A picture of sound with time across, frequency up and loudness as colour, as in this page's header. Useful for spotting cuts and changes in background noise.6
Spoofing
Faking the apparent origin of a call or message, such as caller ID, as in the New Hampshire robocall.27
WebSDR / KiwiSDR
Networks of receivers open to the public through a browser, used to hear a broadcast from somewhere other than your own location.89
