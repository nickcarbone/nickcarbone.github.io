Geographic dossier · Six cities · 19 institutions · Five case files

The Geography of Disappearing Wealth

A dossier on six cities where diamonds, watches and art are legitimately traded, stored and checked, and on what documented cases show about how stolen valuables are traced across borders.

By Nick Carbone · 1 November 2026 · About 22 minutes

The legitimate trade in valuable objects is far larger than anything thieves take from it. Antwerp alone handled about $19 billion of diamond imports and exports in 2025.28 In 2015 INTERPOL put the takings of the Pink Panthers, the loose network of jewel robbers it has tracked since 2007, at about €334 million since 1999.3 A stolen diamond, watch or painting has to find a way back into that legitimate trade, or stay hidden for good. The places where it would re-enter are the same places where it is most likely to be recognised.

This dossier is about those places. Six cities do very different jobs in the trade: Antwerp sorts and grades stones, Geneva stores and auctions, London registers and insures, Monaco and Milan sell, and Belgrade turns up in the record mainly as a place where investigations ended. It sets out what each city legitimately does, which institutions sit there, and what five documented cases show about how investigators followed stolen objects from one to another.

It does not explain how to steal anything or how to get past a check. Where the record is an allegation, a defendant’s own account or a disputed figure, it says so, using four labels:

Established court finding, conviction or official record Reported credible reporting or an official estimate, untested in court Alleged claim by a party, prosecutor or defendant Disputed sources conflict

Nothing here suggests that any business or institution named, other than people a court convicted, did anything wrong. Most of the institutions appear because they are the ones that catch stolen goods.

01 · The object and the paper

Every valuable travels twice

A diamond, a watch or a painting has two lives that usually run together. One is physical: the object sits in a safe, goes through customs, crosses a counter. The other is on paper: a grading report, a serial number, an invoice, an auction record, an insurance schedule. Theft separates them. The object goes one way and the paper stays where it was, and much of the investigative work in these cases consists of matching them up again.

How objects carry their identity

Diamonds are identified by measurement. A grading report from a lab such as the Gemological Institute of America (GIA) or HRD Antwerp records a stone’s size, shape and characteristics; GIA calls it the stone’s “blueprint” and stresses that it is not a valuation.38 Labs can laser-inscribe the report number on the girdle, the thin outer edge, where it is visible only under 10× magnification.36 GIA itself notes the limits: inscription matching does not work on a girdle that has been bruted or bleached, and it has found stones carrying counterfeit GIA numbers, so a number on its own proves little.36,37 In 2013 an Antwerp police diamond specialist told Foreign Policy that stolen diamonds can be sold, a view the trade’s own anti-laundering literature shares.81,64

Watches carry serial and case numbers. Registers of lost and stolen watches work by matching those numbers when a watch is offered to a dealer, pawnbroker or auction house.48,49

Art is identified by its history. Provenance is the record of who has owned a work since it was made; a catalogue raisonné tries to document every work an artist produced.74,75 A painting that turns up without a believable provenance draws questions.

The tracks are a simplification. Checks catch only what is registered and only where a business runs one; the Art Loss Register itself says no database can be complete.47

Ownership can move while the object stays still

The reverse also happens. In a freeport or customs warehouse, goods sit with import duties and taxes suspended, and Swiss auditors noted in 2014 that goods in Swiss freeports could be shown and offered for sale without leaving, with no time limit on storage.41 A painting can change hands several times without moving, and the record of who owns it then lives in contracts and the warehouse’s inventory. Since 2016 Swiss law has required freeport operators to keep an inventory of goods and their owners.40 In the EU, the Fifth Anti-Money Laundering Directive of 2018 brought art dealers, auction houses and freeports storing art into money-laundering rules for deals of €10,000 or more.65

Who ends up owning a stolen object also depends on where it was sold. The rules for a buyer who acted in good faith differ sharply across the countries in this dossier:

England

A buyer gets no better title than the seller had: a thief cannot pass ownership (Sale of Goods Act 1979, s.21).70

Switzerland

A stolen object can be reclaimed from any holder for five years; for cultural property, up to 30 years. A good-faith buyer from a dealer or auction must be repaid the price (Civil Code art. 934).66

France

“Possession vaut titre”, but the owner of a stolen item can reclaim it for three years, repaying the price if it was bought from a dealer or at a public sale (Code civil arts 2276–2277).67

Italy

A good-faith buyer who receives a movable object under a suitable title becomes its owner, and good faith is presumed (Codice civile arts 1153 and 1147).68

These differences decided a well-known English case. In Winkworth v Christie Manson & Woods (1980), Japanese netsuke stolen in England were sold to a good-faith buyer in Italy and later sent to Christie’s in London for sale. The English court applied Italian law, as the law of the place where the sale happened, and the original owner’s claim failed.69 Switzerland tightened its own cultural-property rules again from 1 January 2026: storing cultural property in a freeport now counts as importing it, and dealers and auction houses must verify a seller’s identity with a document in every case, not only when in doubt, and keep certain records for 30 years.76

03 · The six cities

City profiles

Each profile sets the city’s legitimate role first, then the institutions that regulate or police it, then what the public record connects to it. Figures that change carry the year they apply to.

Antwerp

ANR · BELGIUM
SORTS & GRADES

Antwerp is the centre of the world’s trade in rough and polished diamonds. Its trade body dates the trade there to 1447 and counts four diamond bourses and more than 1,300 registered diamond companies.27 In 2025 the city’s diamond imports and exports came to about $19.07bn, roughly two-thirds of it polished stones.28 One of them, the Antwerpsche Diamantkring, founded in 1929, calls itself the world’s leading rough-diamond bourse and gives members a trading floor and its own arbitration.32

Every diamond entering or leaving Belgium from outside the EU passes through the Diamond Office, where experts physically check the declared value, weight and origin under the supervision of the economy ministry.29 Diamond traders have been covered by Belgian money-laundering law since 2004 and must register; a one-off deal of €10,000 or more triggers full customer checks.30 The trade body’s 2026 compliance guide gives €3,000 per transaction as the legal maximum for cash.31

Institutions
Antwerp World Diamond Centre (AWDC); HRD Antwerp, the grading lab founded in 1973 and now an AWDC subsidiary33; the Diamond Office; FPS Economy.
In the record
The 2003 vault theft at the Antwerp Diamond Centre (covered in Daily No. 7). The 2013 Brussels Airport theft of a consignment bound for Zurich (link E2, case file 5).

Geneva

GVA · SWITZERLAND
STORES & AUCTIONS

Geneva is a watchmaking city, an auction city and a storage city. Swiss watch exports were worth CHF 25.6bn in 2025.45 The major auction houses hold their big jewellery sales there, usually in May and November.44 And Ports Francs et Entrepôts de Genève, the freeport, majority-owned by the canton, stores goods with duties and taxes suspended.43,39

Freeports were built for goods in transit and are now used for long-term storage, which is why they attract scrutiny.39 The Swiss Federal Audit Office reported in 2014 that some goods had stayed for decades and that customs held no value data for freeport stock.41 Since 2016 operators must keep an inventory of goods and owners; the Geneva freeport added vetting of antiquities and checks of tenants against INTERPOL databases.40,39

Institutions
Geneva Freeports; Swiss Federal Office for Customs; the cantonal prosecutor; auction houses’ Geneva salerooms.
In the record
Antiquities seized in the freeport (link E11). Stones from the 2013 Brussels Airport theft found in Geneva (E2). In 2013 a Geneva court sentenced a Montenegrin man to nine years for a 2009 robbery on the Rue du Rhône and a 2012 watch robbery in Lucerne; he planned to appeal.24

London

LON · UNITED KINGDOM
REGISTERS & INSURES

Hatton Garden has been London’s jewellery quarter since the 19th century: jewellery, watch and clock businesses there grew from 11 in 1807 to 264 in 1907.46 London is also home to the main private registers of stolen property. The Art Loss Register, founded in 1990, says it holds more than 700,000 items.47 Its sister service, The Watch Register, lists more than 125,000 lost or stolen watches and checks serial numbers for dealers, pawnbrokers, auction houses and, free of charge, the police.48

Since 10 January 2020, “art market participants” have been covered by the UK’s money-laundering rules and supervised by HMRC. HMRC says the threshold changed from €10,000 to £10,000 from 30 June 2026.50

Institutions
Art Loss Register; The Watch Register; the Metropolitan Police Art and Antiques Unit; HMRC.
In the record
Graff, New Bond Street, robbed in 2003 (two men convicted in 2004, one sentenced to 15 years) and in 2009 (four men sentenced in 2010; none of the jewels recovered).8,51 Scotland Yard’s nickname for the Pink Panthers came from the 2003 case, after a stolen stone was found hidden in a jar of cream; sources differ on whether it was a loose diamond or a ring, and on the kind of cream.4,7,85

Monaco

MCM · MONACO
SELLS & BANKS

Monaco is a small, wealthy state with a large banking sector and luxury shopping concentrated in a few streets. Its anti-money-laundering law dates from 2009 and was amended in 2020 and 2022, the later changes aimed partly at traders in luxury goods.55 In 2023 a new authority, AMSF, replaced the old financial-intelligence unit, SICCFIN.56

The Financial Action Task Force placed Monaco under increased monitoring, its “grey list”, in June 2024. In September 2026 press reports said it was expected to come off the list at the FATF’s October plenary.52,54 We could not confirm the outcome when this was published; check the FATF’s Monaco page for its current status.53

Institutions
AMSF; the Sûreté Publique; Monaco’s INTERPOL National Central Bureau.
In the record
Two suspected Pink Panther members arrested in 2008 (E9). The 2007 watch theft and the Milan sale its thief described (E5). A stolen Cézanne kept in Monaco for years (E4, case file 3).

Milan

MIL · ITALY
SELLS & MAKES

Via Montenapoleone was ranked the most expensive luxury shopping street in the world by Cushman & Wakefield in 2024, at about €20,000 per square metre a year, ahead of New York’s Upper Fifth Avenue and London’s New Bond Street.57 Italy’s jewellery manufacturing is concentrated in districts at Arezzo, Vicenza and Valenza.59

The Carabinieri’s cultural heritage unit, founded in May 1969, is often described as the world’s first specialist art-crime police.82 Its database holds more than 1.3 million files, and in 2024 it recovered 80,437 cultural objects worth about €129.6m.58

Institutions
Carabinieri Command for the Protection of Cultural Heritage (TPC); Guardia di Finanza; Milan prosecutors.
In the record
The Monaco watches its thief said were sold in Milan (E5). In July 2022 police arrested three Montenegrin men accused of jewellery robberies in central Milan between 2019 and 2022; we found no verdict.23 Alleged

Belgrade

BEG · SERBIA
WHERE TRAILS ENDED

Belgrade is not a centre of the luxury trade. It belongs in this dossier because of where the Pink Panthers came from and where some investigations finished. Reporting on the network traces its origins to Serbia and Montenegro during the wars and UN sanctions of the 1990s.5,6 The Organized Crime and Corruption Reporting Project (OCCRP) has pointed out that “Pink Panthers” began as a Scotland Yard nickname that stuck, and that Serbian police were not sure it described a single organised group.4 INTERPOL’s former secretary general called it “networks of teams” without a single leader.5

Serbian courts and police have been part of several cross-border cases: the trial of a Tokyo robbery in Belgrade (E7), and the recovery of the Bührle Cézanne in 2012 in raids in Belgrade and Čačak, coordinated with police from several European countries (E3, case file 4).25,20

Institutions
Serbian Ministry of the Interior; INTERPOL National Central Bureau Belgrade; Serbian courts.
In the record
INTERPOL’s project figures: about 380 robberies since 1999 in 35 countries and an estimated 800 members, many from the former Yugoslavia (2015).3 Reported
04 · The legitimate trade

Who handles a valuable object

Five kinds of business handle a valuable object on its way from maker to owner and back. Each keeps records, and each is a place where a stolen piece might be recognised.

Jewellers

Sell to the public and keep invoices and grading reports. In the US, dealers buying and selling more than $50,000 a year in covered goods must run an anti-money-laundering programme, though most retailers are exempt.86

Gem labs

Grade and inscribe stones and keep records of what they graded, which is how a recut stone can still be matched.36,9

Auction houses

Publish catalogues before each sale, which lets registers such as the Art Loss Register check the lots.47

Dealers & bourses

Trade between businesses on invoices carrying a warranty statement under the World Diamond Council’s System of Warranties.35

Resale

A secondary watch market estimated by the data firm EveryWatch at $16.73bn in 2025; serial checks happen at the point of sale.83,48

05 · Case files

Five traces

Each case is told through its trace: where the object or the evidence went, which institution recognised it, and what a court decided. Values are given as each source reported them; they often disagree.

01DUBAI · 2007
DNA, INTERPOL, extradition

Established

The robbery that started a project

In April 2007 a Graff store in Dubai’s Wafi Mall was robbed. Reported values range from about $3.5m to about $15m.11,5 Value disputed Gulf News reports that the case prompted INTERPOL’s Pink Panthers project.11

  1. Dubai Police sent DNA profiles from the investigation to INTERPOL, which matched evidence from the scene to a 2006 robbery in Liechtenstein.11
  2. In February 2014 a Serbian suspect was arrested in Spain. He was extradited to the UAE in October 2015.11
  3. In November 2016 a Dubai court sentenced him to ten years and deportation.12 No source we found reports the jewels being recovered.

What it shows: the trace followed the people, through shared forensic data, rather than the goods. It took nine years.

02LONDON → HONG KONG · 2007–12
Gem-lab records

Alleged in lawsuit

A yellow diamond, recut

Graff was robbed in London in 2007. In January 2012 it brought suits in New York State Supreme Court over two stones it said came from that robbery.9,10

  1. According to the suit, a yellow diamond of more than 16 carats was submitted to GIA by a Hong Kong pawn shop.9
  2. GIA had graded the stone before the theft and matched it despite recutting: JCK reported 16.4 carats stolen and 16.28 carats submitted. A second, 4.2-carat stone was also identified.10
  3. According to JCK, the smaller stone was submitted by a different person. GIA held both stones pending a resolution.10,9 The pawn shop told AP it had paid for the yellow stone and had no way of knowing it was stolen. We found no published outcome.

What it shows: a lab’s own records can identify a stone that no longer matches its paperwork. Who then owns it can depend on where, and from whom, it was bought (see section 01).

03MASSACHUSETTS → MONACO → LONDON · 1978–2010
Register check

Established

The Cézanne that waited 21 years

In 1978 seven works were stolen from a collector’s home in Stockbridge, Massachusetts, among them Cézanne’s still life Bouilloire et fruits.17

  1. The holder, a retired lawyer, has said he kept the paintings in Monaco and later in a Swiss bank, through a shell company.19
  2. When the holder tried to move the paintings to London for sale, an investigation by the Art Loss Register found they were stolen, and it alerted the owner. Under a deal it brokered, the Cézanne was handed back in 1999, and the owner sold it at Sotheby’s in London for $29.3m.92,88
  3. The holder, Robert Mardirosian, was convicted in 2008 of possessing stolen goods and sentenced to seven years. The FBI recovered the last of the works in 2010.17,18

What it shows: storage in a discreet jurisdiction kept the painting out of sight, but not out of the register. It was caught when it tried to come back to market.

04ZURICH → BELGRADE · 2008–12
Cross-border police operation

Established

The Boy in the Red Vest

In February 2008 four paintings were taken from the Bührle collection in Zurich. The Cézanne among them was valued at the time at about $109.6m.20

  1. In April 2012 Serbian police found it during raids in Belgrade and Čačak coordinated with police from several European countries.20,21
  2. Reports differ on whether three or four people were arrested; about €1.5m in cash was seized.20,21 Disputed
  3. Zurich prosecutors confirmed the painting’s identity.20 We did not find a verdict in a Serbian trial, and no court finding we saw links the theft to the Pink Panthers.

What it shows: a painting this famous can’t be sold openly, and it surfaced four years later in another country.

05BRUSSELS → GENEVA · 2013–23
Three countries, one conviction

Established

The Zurich consignment

On 18 February 2013 a consignment of diamonds and other precious goods, flown from Brussels Airport towards Zurich, was taken from the plane on the tarmac. Belgian court figures put it at about €37.9m; earlier reports said $50m.14,13

  1. In May 2013, 31 people were detained: 24 in Belgium, six in Switzerland and one in France. Prosecutors said diamonds were found in Switzerland and cash in Belgium.13
  2. About €5m of the stones was found at a Frenchman’s home in Geneva.14
  3. In 2018 a Brussels court acquitted 18 of 19 defendants for lack of evidence. In 2019 the Frenchman, Marc Bertoldi, was sentenced to five years: the court found he had played a decisive role in the robbery and convicted him of money laundering and criminal conspiracy, while clearing him of the preparatory acts. In 2023 an appeal court acquitted the last four defendants.15,14,16

What it shows: finding stolen goods is not the same as proving who stole them. Of 31 people detained, one was convicted, and most of the haul was not recovered.

07 · Limits

What the map cannot show

Unrecovered goods leave no route

The links on the map exist because something was found. Where nothing was found, in the 2009 Graff robbery or most of the Brussels Airport haul, no route can be drawn.51,16

Numbers move

Values in these cases are insurers’ figures, retail prices or court figures, and change between reports. The Wafi Mall robbery has been put at anything from about $3.5m to $15m.5,11

Registers see what is registered

A watch whose serial number was never recorded, or a painting never reported, will pass a database check.47,49

Few reports come from the trade

The FATF found almost no suspicious-transaction reports from diamond dealers, and in 2017 Transparency International called for more reporting across luxury sectors.64,77

08 · Glossary

Trade and investigation terms

Rough diamond
A diamond that is unworked or simply sawn, cleaved or bruted (EU Kimberley regulation).78
Grading report
A lab’s record of a stone’s measurements and characteristics. Not a valuation.38
Girdle inscription
A laser-inscribed number or message on a diamond’s outer edge, visible at 10×.36
Kimberley Process certificate
A government document accompanying a shipment of rough diamonds across a border.78,34
System of Warranties
An invoice statement passed along each sale of rough, polished or set diamonds between businesses.35
Bourse
A members’ exchange where diamonds are traded, with its own rules and arbitration.32
Freeport
A secure customs zone where goods are stored with duties and taxes suspended.39,41
Customs warehouse
Approved premises where imported goods are stored with duties and import VAT suspended.89
Provenance
The ownership history of a work from its creation to the present.74
Catalogue raisonné
A thorough, systematic record of all the works by an artist.75
Consignor
The person or entity on whose behalf an auction house sells an item.73
Hammer price
The final bid, before the buyer’s premium is added.73
Buyer’s premium
A percentage the buyer pays the auction house on top of the hammer price.73
Title
The legal basis of ownership, distinct from possession.90
Good-faith purchaser
A buyer who pays for property with no reason to suspect a problem with it.91
Fence
Someone who receives stolen goods, often to resell them. English law calls the offence handling stolen goods.80
Art market participant
A UK business trading or storing art in deals above the threshold, covered by money-laundering rules.50
FIU
Financial intelligence unit: the national body that receives and analyses suspicious transaction reports.72
Red notice
An INTERPOL request to locate and arrest a person wanted for prosecution or to serve a sentence. It is not an arrest warrant.71
Diffusion
A request circulated directly by one country’s INTERPOL bureau to some or all others.71
Mutual legal assistance
Cooperation between states to gather evidence for criminal investigations or prosecutions.84
European Arrest Warrant
A simplified procedure for surrendering a person between EU states for prosecution or to serve a sentence.79
