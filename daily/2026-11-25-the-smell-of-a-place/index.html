A field guide in twenty phials

The Smell of a Place

Twenty places with a scent of their own, the molecules that make each one, why a smell can carry you back decades and how perfumers build one from parts.

Some places can be recognised with your eyes shut. A jasmine field at dawn, a peat kiln, a geothermal town where the ground steams: each has a smell that belongs to it, and each smell is a handful of molecules that chemists can name and measure.

This guide visits twenty of them. For each it explains where the smell comes from, when to catch it and which compounds do the work, often in proportions that surprise. In rose oil a compound at about one part in seven hundred supplies most of the scent1. In durian two compounds out of dozens reproduce the smell of the pulp95. Some of the places grow their smell, some burn it, and one, a small German town, manufactures it.

After the places come two short pieces of background: why smells seem to reach memory so directly, and how perfumers assemble a scent from parts.

The twentyPlaces and their smells

FloralFlowers picked for their oil

No. 01JasmineFloral

GrasseFrance

When to smell itPicked from sunrise to about 1pm, July to October

Jasmine in Grasse is picked at the start of the day. The flowers of Jasminum grandiflorum open overnight, and pickers take them one at a time from sunrise until about 1pm, before the heat; a picker gathers two to three kilos in a morning3, 4. About 8,000 flowers make a kilo4. The Mul family, which has farmed near Grasse since the 1800s and has supplied Chanel since 1987, reckons on around 1,000 blossoms in a single one-ounce bottle of No. 5 parfum3, 5.

The trade grew out of leather. UNESCO describes perfume developing in Grasse from at least the 16th century inside a craft economy “long dominated by leather tanning”, and in 2018 it added the town’s perfume skills, from growing the plants to composing the scent, to its list of intangible heritage6. The fields have shrunk badly: CBS put them at about 12,000 acres in the early 1900s and about 142 acres now, and one trade estimate has Grasse jasmine down from over 200 hectares to about 20, with roughly 60% of perfumery grandiflorum now grown in Egypt3, 7.

The flowers are steeped in solvent to make a waxy concrete, which is refined into an absolute3. In one analysis of an Egyptian absolute, the largest single component, about a quarter, was benzyl acetate, sweet and fruity. Indole, which smells animal and faintly faecal on its own, gives jasmine its heavy depth, and cis-jasmone and methyl jasmonate add a green, tea-like lift2. Picking alters the balance. IFF chemists who sampled the air around living jasmine found benzyl acetate at about 60% of the scent and indole at 11%; once the flower was picked those fell to roughly 40% and 2%, while linalool climbed from 3% to 30%8.

No. 02Damask roseFloral

KazanlakBulgaria

When to smell itAbout three weeks from late May, picked from sunrise to before noon

The Rose Valley around Kazanlak picks its crop of Rosa damascena over about three weeks in May and June, starting at sunrise and finishing before noon9, 10. The petals give up almost nothing. Industrial distilleries recover 0.015–0.048% of the flowers’ weight as oil11; Bulgarian National Radio puts it at about a tonne of petals for a kilo of oil in a good year, and three to four tonnes in a bad one10. Estimates vary widely, and National Geographic’s figure is nearer 3,200 to one9.

Kazanlak has held a rose festival since 1903, now on the first weekend of June12, 9. About 95% of the country’s rose oil is exported10, and Bulgaria is often described as the world’s largest producer13.

Rose oil is the clearest case of a scent carried by its smallest parts. Citronellol and geraniol make up half the oil by weight and only about 5% of its smell. β-Damascenone, at about 0.14%, contributes around 70% of the odour, because the nose detects it at 0.009 parts per billion1. Most of the 2-phenylethanol, the note most people call “rose”, stays behind in the rosewater11. See the chart below.

No. 03Orange blossomFloral

SevilleSpain

When to smell itBlossom in March and April; fruit in winter

Seville’s streets are planted with bitter orange trees, more than 47,700 by one 2023 count and over 50,000 in a 2024 report15, 16. Their blossom, azahar, from the Arabic zahr, “flower”, peaks in March and April17.

The winter fruit is where the story usually goes wrong. The bitter oranges in British marmalade come from commercial orchards around the city; one Seville exporter sends nearly 65% of its crop to the UK18. The street oranges are collected by the city, which expected about 2,500 tonnes in its 2023–24 campaign, and end up composted, turned into biogas, fed to goats or landfilled; pollution is one reason industry does not use them15, 16. In 2021 the water company EMASESA piloted turning 35 tonnes of them into electricity19. Fruit from the trees of the Real Alcázar is the exception, and goes to the British royal household for marmalade16.

Linalool dominates the flower oil, with linalyl acetate, limonene and nerolidol20. The solvent-extracted absolute adds indole and methyl anthranilate, the grape-like note (up to about 10% and 4% in one analysis), which is why orange-blossom absolute smells heavier and sweeter than distilled neroli14.

  • Nerolidolwoody, floral5–9%
  • Methyl anthranilategrape, orange flower1–4%
No. 04Ylang-ylangFloral

AnjouanComoros

When to smell itDistilled in one long run of 20 to 24 hours

The Comoros dominate the world’s ylang-ylang. By the University of Liège’s estimate, the islands make 50–65 tonnes of oil a year, most of a world output it puts at no more than 70; Madagascar and the French island of Mayotte are the other main producers, and the estimates do not add up neatly21. Anjouan is the heart of it: 45–50 tonnes in 2008 figures, against about 3 for Mohéli and under 2 for Grande Comore22.

Ylang-ylang is sold by the hour. A single distillation of the flowers of Cananga odorata runs for about 20 to 24 hours, and the oil is drawn off in cuts as it goes: Extra Supérieure and Extra first, then grades I, II and III21. The early cuts are the lighter, more floral “head”; the last are heavy with sesquiterpenes. The cut points are empirical, and different sources give different timings21, 22.

Linalool gives the fresh floral note and benzyl acetate the sweetness. The odd one is p-cresyl methyl ether, which lends ylang-ylang its faintly medicinal edge. Sesquiterpenes such as caryophyllene and farnesene can make up as much as 40% of the oil and form its warm base21.

On the blotter21

  • Linaloolfresh, floral
  • Benzyl acetatesweet, fruity
  • p-Cresyl methyl ethermedicinal, narcotic
  • Farnesenegreen, woody

Citrus & herbPeel and leaf

No. 05Lavandin and lavenderCitrus & herb

ValensoleFrance

When to smell itIn bloom from mid-June to mid-July

The purple stripes in photographs of the Valensole plateau are mostly lavandin, a sterile hybrid of true lavender and spike lavender that can only be grown from cuttings24, 25. It covers 90–95% of the plateau’s fields because it is generous with oil: about 150–200 kg a hectare against about 20 kg for fine lavender25. The regional tourist board dates its arrival to the 1920s26. In 2023 France grew 22,260 hectares of lavandin and 8,394 of lavender, 7,046 hectares of the two on the Valensole plateau27.

True lavender is the protected one. The appellation “Huile essentielle de lavande de Haute-Provence”, created by decree in 1981, requires Lavandula angustifolia grown from seed, normally at 800 metres or higher28. Fields bloom from mid-June to mid-July26, and growers in Provence say the harvest is creeping earlier as summers warm25.

Both oils smell mainly of linalool and linalyl acetate, the soft pair also found in bergamot. What separates them is camphor: around 0.6% in fine lavender oils against roughly 10–12% in lavandin, which is why lavandin smells sharper23. The appellation caps camphor below 0.5%28.

On the blotter23

  • Linaloolsoft, floral
  • Linalyl acetatefresh, fruity
  • Camphorcool, sharp≈0.6% lavender · ≈10–12% lavandin
No. 06BergamotCitrus & herb

Reggio CalabriaItaly

When to smell itGrown in 45 coastal municipalities from Scilla to Monasterace

More than 90% of the world’s bergamot grows on a narrow stretch of Calabria’s southern coast29. Its essential oil has been a protected designation of origin since 200130, and about 200 kg of fruit gives a kilo of essence31. Bergamot is the flavour of Earl Grey tea31 and an ingredient of eau de Cologne29.

The oil is mostly limonene (25–53%), linalyl acetate (15–40%) and linalool (2–20%)29. That is a bright citrus top laid over the same soft floral pair that scents lavender.

It also carries a hazard. Bergapten, about 0.2% of the cold-pressed oil, makes skin burn and stain in sunlight, and early-20th-century cases of this “berloque dermatitis” followed the use of eau de Cologne29. Perfumers now use a vacuum-distilled “FCF” oil with the furocoumarins taken out, and industry standards limit bergapten in leave-on skin products to 15 parts per million29, 32.

On the blotter29

  • Limonenebright, orange peel25–53%
  • Linalyl acetatefresh, fruity, lavender15–40%
  • Linaloolsoft, floral2–20%
  • Bergaptenodourless; phototoxic≈0.2%

Earth, rain & leafSoil, storms and forests

No. 07Creosote after rainEarth, rain & leaf

Sonoran DesertArizona, US, and Mexico

When to smell itWhen a storm breaks over dry ground

In the Sonoran Desert the smell of rain is credited above all to the creosote bush, Larrea tridentata, a resinous shrub that has nothing to do with coal-tar creosote and that Mexicans call hediondilla, “little stinker”34. A University of Arizona team led by the ethnobotanist Gary Paul Nabhan found that creosote gives off more than 35 terpenes and other volatiles, and across 60-odd Sonoran plants they logged 115 compounds released around rainstorms. Their conclusion is that creosote is a dominant emitter but by no means the only one35, 36.

The ground contributes too. In 1964 the CSIRO chemists Isabel Joy Bear and Richard Thomas described an oil that collects in dry rocks and soil and is released when they get wet, and named it petrichor, from the Greek for stone and the fluid in the veins of the gods37. The earthy part is geosmin, made by Streptomyces soil bacteria and detectable by people at around 100 parts per trillion; reported thresholds vary considerably33. In 2020 researchers showed that geosmin draws springtails to the bacteria, which eat them and carry off their spores33, 38. How the smell gets airborne was shown at MIT in 2015: light and moderate rain trap air bubbles in porous soil that burst out as aerosols39.

Creosote also lives a very long time. King Clone, a ring of creosote in the neighbouring Mojave Desert, has been estimated from its rate of spread to be about 11,700 years old34.

  • 2-Methylisoborneolmusty, earthy

No. 08Mitti attar, the smell of first rainEarth, rain & leaf

KannaujIndia

When to smell itDistilled for days at a time, outside the monsoon

Kannauj, often called India’s perfume capital, bottles the smell of rain on dry earth. For mitti attar, kiln-baked clay is broken into a copper still, the deg, sealed with clay and heated over a wood or dung fire. A bamboo pipe carries the vapour to a copper receiver, the bhapka, which holds sandalwood oil or liquid paraffin and sits cooling in water41, 42. The finished attar is kept in camel-leather flasks that let excess water evaporate. Makers count the monsoon the least suitable season for distilling it41.

Of the 350 to 400 attar houses in the town, only about a tenth make mitti attar, according to the government’s Fragrance and Flavour Development Centre41. “Kannauj Perfume” was registered as a geographical indication in 201443. Its history is often stretched: the anthropologist Giti Datt has found no Kannauj perfumery with pre-British origins, and nobody knows when attars were first made there42.

When Bear and Thomas named petrichor in 1964, CSIRO notes, perfumers in India were already capturing the scent in sandalwood oil37. The carrier matters: good East Indian sandalwood oil is 41–55% α-santalol, the creamy, woody base the earth note rests on40.

  • Geosminearth, wet clay
  • α-Santalolcreamy, woody41–55% of sandalwood oil
  • β-Santalolwoody, milky16–24%
No. 09EucalyptusEarth, rain & leaf

Blue MountainsNew South Wales, Australia

When to smell itStrongest on hot, still days

The Greater Blue Mountains became a World Heritage area in 2000. It covers more than a million hectares and its eucalypts number around 90 to 100 species, depending on who is counting; the NSW government puts it at 96, about 13% of the world’s eucalypts45, 46.

The range is named for its blue haze, and the cause is still argued. The popular explanation, oil droplets from the eucalypts scattering blue light, goes back to the physicist Harry Messel in 1955, who said plainly that it was “merely a guess”47. CSIRO’s scientists describe the haze as the product of a chemical reaction in the air driven by isoprene, which the trees emit in large quantities; they call the region a global hotspot for such plant emissions48.

The smell itself comes from the trees’ leaf oil. Medicinal eucalyptus oil, distilled from a few species, must be at least 70% 1,8-cineole, the cool, camphor-like compound also called eucalyptol44.

On the blotter44

  • 1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol)cool, camphoraceous
  • Isoprenedrives the haze

Wood & resinBurned, warmed or distilled

No. 10IncenseWood & resin

KyotoJapan

When to smell itAll year; kōdō is practised indoors

Kyoto’s incense houses are among the oldest businesses in the city. Kyukyodo began in 1663 as a pharmacy that mixed medicinal ingredients, and took to making incense from the fragrant woods it already stocked50. Shoyeido was founded in 1705 by a man who had worked at the imperial court, and is still run by the same family51.

Incense came to Japan with Buddhism and was codified in the 16th century as kōdō, the way of incense, counted among the refined arts alongside the tea ceremony and flower arranging52. In kōdō one “listens” to incense: a sliver of wood about 2 mm square is warmed on a mica plate over hot ash in a small burner52. The greatest piece of all is in Nara, at the Shōsōin repository: the Ranjatai, an 11.6 kg log of agarwood 156 cm long. A Kyoto University study reported in 2025 found about 300 components in it53, 52.

Agarwood forms when an Aquilaria tree is wounded and floods the damage with resin; fungal infection probably speeds it up49. Its characteristic compounds are 2-(2-phenylethyl)chromones and sesquiterpenes49. Wild trees have been so heavily cut that the international trade has been controlled under CITES since the mid-1990s, and the whole genus since 2004–0554. Sandalwood, the other mainstay, gets its creamy note from santalols40.

On the blotter49

  • 2-(2-Phenylethyl)chromonesagarwood’s signature compounds
  • Sesquiterpeneswoody, balsamic
  • α-Santalolcreamy, woodyin sandalwood
No. 11FrankincenseWood & resin

DhofarOman

When to smell itTapped in the weeks before the khareef monsoon, roughly April to June

UNESCO listed Dhofar’s Land of Frankincense in 2000: the frankincense trees of Wadi Dawkah, the caravan oasis of Shisr/Wubar and the ports of Khor Rori and Al-Baleed, through which flowed one of the most valuable luxuries of the ancient world56, 57. Wadi Dawkah’s reserve holds about 5,000 trees58.

Harvesters cut shallow wounds in the bark of Boswellia sacra with a curved blade, the manghaf. A milky sap seeps out and hardens over weeks into tears, which are cleaned by hand and graded by colour and clarity; pale, faintly green tears fetch the most59, 58. Grades go by names such as Hojari and Najdi58.

Frankincense smells bright before it smells churchy. In one analysis of certified Omani resin, the oil was a third limonene and a third (E)-β-ocimene, with myrcene and α-pinene, nearly all monoterpenes; earlier studies found α-pinene dominant, and samples vary55. Octyl acetate, often listed as a frankincense marker, was the signature of the Ethiopian species B. papyrifera in one comparative study60. Incensole acetate, a heavier compound in the resin, reduced anxiety-like behaviour in mice in a 2008 study61.

No. 12VetiverWood & resin

Les CayesHaiti

When to smell itRoots dug and distilled in batches that run over a day

Haiti produces more vetiver oil than anywhere else: more than half the world’s supply, over 70 tonnes a year in AP’s 2019 reporting, or more than two-thirds of a roughly 100-tonne market by a trade estimate the same year63, 64. The industry is centred on Les Cayes in the south-west and involves somewhere between 15,000 and 60,000 farmers. Each distillation runs for more than 24 hours63. Haitian oil sold for over US$350 a kilo in 201964.

The scent is in the roots of Chrysopogon zizanioides, a tall grass. More than 300 compounds have been identified in the oil, and the main odour carriers are three ketones: α-vetivone, β-vetivone and khusimone65. Khusimol, an alcohol, is one of the main constituents, at 3.4–13.7% across oils from nine origins including Haiti62.

Vetiver is a base note: dry, earthy and woody, and slow to evaporate. Guerlain’s Vétiver, created in 1959 by Jean-Paul Guerlain as his first fragrance, is the best-known perfume built around it66.

SmokeFires made on purpose

No. 13Peat smokeSmoke

IslayScotland

When to smell itEvery working day at the maltings

Islay’s smoke is made deliberately. Malted barley is dried over a peat fire kept smothered so that it smokes; at Bowmore the kiln is fed broken peat for about 18 to 21 hours, and phenols in the smoke cling to the grain and survive into the spirit68. Since 1974 Diageo’s Port Ellen Maltings has supplied most of the island’s distilleries and burns up to 2,000 tonnes of peat a year69.

Peating is measured in parts per million of phenols in the malt: about 10 ppm is light, 25 medium, and 40–50 or more heavy67. Laphroaig’s own floor malt runs at about 50 ppm and Bowmore at 25–30; Octomore 8.3 was made from barley peated to 30968. Peat differs from place to place: heated in the laboratory, Islay peat gave mostly lignin-derived products, while Orkney peat gave more carbohydrate-derived and aromatic compounds70.

Phenol and the cresols give the “medicinal” note; guaiacol adds smoke and earthiness67, 71. The chemistry does not map neatly onto taste: when researchers measured phenols in ten Islay whiskies, the levels matched poorly with how smoky tasters judged them72.

On the blotter67

  • Guaiacolsmoky, earthy
  • p-Cresolmedicinal, tarry
  • Phenolmedicinal, sharp
No. 14Lapsang souchongSmoke

Tongmu, Wuyi MountainsChina

When to smell itLeaves withered and dried over pine fires

Lapsang souchong comes mainly from Tongmu village in the Wuyi Mountains of Fujian, and to count as authentic in China it must be grown within about 50 square kilometres of the Tongmu reserve74. The leaves are dried over fires of Chinese red pine. A legend dates the method to the fall of the Ming dynasty, when soldiers are said to have slept on the leaves and workers dried them quickly over pine; Sixth Tone notes the story is hard to verify74. Mount Wuyi has been a World Heritage site since 199975.

The smoke itself is now rationed. Pine wilt disease devastated Tongmu’s pine forest, and in 2020 the authorities banned bringing Chinese red pinewood into the village; only about 20% of Tongmu tea is still smoked the traditional way74.

Chemists found 49 volatile compounds in the tea, with longifolene and α-terpineol, both from the pine, the most abundant. Guaiacol, 4-methylguaiacol and 4-ethylguaiacol turned up only in the smoked tea, while the tea’s own aroma compounds fell during smoking73.

  • Longifolenewoody, resinousmost abundant
  • α-Terpineolpine, lilacmost abundant
  • 4-Methylguaiacolsmoky, spicysmoked tea only

Spice & sweetCured, dried or synthesised

No. 15ClovesSpice & sweet

PembaZanzibar, Tanzania

When to smell itDrying on mats after the harvest

Tradition credits an Omani trader, Harmali bin Saleh, with bringing cloves to Zanzibar from Réunion in 1812. In the 1830s Sultan Said bin Sultan moved his capital to Zanzibar and ordered three clove trees planted for every coconut palm on Zanzibar and Pemba; by the late 19th century about 90% of the world’s cloves grew there77. Pemba, the more fertile island, grows most of the crop78, 79.

The boom did not last. Output peaked at about 16,000 tonnes a year in the 1970s and fell to 1,500–3,500 tonnes a year in the 2000s. The state trading corporation, set up in 1968, was long the only legal buyer79. Harvests swing hard from year to year: in the year to June 2026, Zanzibar exported about 7,000 tonnes worth US$44.6m, around 63% of its goods exports, up from about 700 tonnes the year before80.

Clove bud oil is typically 75–88% eugenol, with eugenyl acetate and β-caryophyllene making up nearly all the rest76.

On the blotter76

  • Eugenolwarm, spicy, clove75–88%
  • Eugenyl acetatefruity, clove4–15%
  • β-Caryophyllenewoody, peppery5–14%
No. 16Curing vanillaSpice & sweet

SAVA regionMadagascar

When to smell itMonths of curing after the green pods are picked

Every vanilla bean is hand-pollinated, a technique worked out in 1841 by Edmond Albius, a 12-year-old enslaved boy on Réunion, who used a sliver of bamboo to press the orchid’s anther to its stigma82. Madagascar now grows roughly 80% of the world’s vanilla, and the north-eastern SAVA region about 80% of Madagascar’s; the figures are estimates and disputed83.

A green pod has almost no smell, because its vanillin is locked up as an odourless glucoside84. Curing releases it. The pods are blanched in hot water at 60–65°C for two to three minutes, sweated under blankets at about 40°C for one to three days until they turn brown, dried slowly on racks and then conditioned for months in boxes. An enzyme frees the vanillin; cured beans end up at about 2–3% vanillin, and traditional curing converts less than half of what is there81.

Natural vanilla extract contains nearly 200 different molecules, and less than 5% of vanilla flavouring sold is natural84.

On the blotter81

  • Vanillinsweet, creamy≈2–3% of cured beans
  • Glucovanillinodourless precursor
No. 17Vanillin from a factorySpice & sweet

HolzmindenGermany

When to smell itSince 1875

Not every place in this guide is defined by something growing. In 1874 the chemists Ferdinand Tiemann and Wilhelm Haarmann made vanillin from coniferin, a compound in conifer sap, and in 1875 Haarmann opened a vanillin factory in Holzminden, a small town on the Weser; coumarin, the hay-like smell of tonka beans, followed in 187885.

That factory became Haarmann & Reimer, which merged with Dragoco, another Holzminden firm, in 2003 to form Symrise, one of the world’s largest flavour and fragrance companies. Its headquarters is still in the town86.

Synthetic vanillin now accounts for most vanilla flavour; less than 5% is natural84. Holzminden’s place in the smell of the world is the vanilla in millions of things that never saw a vanilla orchid.

On the blotter85

  • Vanillinsweet, creamyfirst synthesised 1874
  • Coumarinhay, tonka, almondmade from 1878

SulphurThe smells people argue about

No. 18Hydrogen sulphideSulphur

RotoruaNew Zealand

When to smell itAll year; strongest downwind of the geothermal fields

Rotorua is nicknamed Sulphur City. The geothermal field under the town releases hydrogen sulphide, and levels run highest around Whakarewarewa, Ngapuna, Government Gardens and Motutara Point; the western suburbs, upwind of the prevailing westerlies, get the least88.

Its population of nearly 60,000 is the world’s largest exposed to geothermal hydrogen sulphide. A 2013 study of 1,637 adults measured median levels of about 20 parts per billion at home and 26 at work, with spot readings in hotspots briefly reaching 1–2 parts per million; it found no evidence of more asthma87. Most people can smell the gas at 0.5 to 30 parts per billion87.

The smell is a warning that fails when it matters. At around 100 ppm the nose stops registering hydrogen sulphide within minutes89, and at least 11 deaths in Rotorua over several decades have been blamed on geothermal gas90. The gas also eats metal: mild steel corrodes more than ten times faster in Rotorua’s geothermal areas than at rural or coastal sites88.

  • Hydrogen sulphiderotten eggsdetectable at parts per billion
No. 19The seaSulphur

The Breton coastFrance

When to smell itLow tide, warm days

The smell people call “sea air” is mostly dimethyl sulphide. Phytoplankton, seaweed and bacteria make more than a billion tonnes a year of a compound called DMSP; when microbes break it down, they release DMS, which gives the seaside its characteristic smell91. DMS also seeds cloud droplets. The once-popular idea that plankton regulate the climate this way, the CLAW hypothesis, has been seriously challenged; one major 2011 review said it was time to retire it91, 92.

Brittany is France’s seaweed coast. Specialised boats land 40,000–60,000 tonnes a year, mostly kelp, two-thirds of it at the small port of Lanildut in Finistère, and the region produces about 90% of France’s seaweed93.

The briny, “iodine” note of seafood comes from bromophenols, which marine algae produce; 2,4,6-tribromophenol was found in every one of 49 seaweed species tested in one Australian study, and fish that graze on algae are thought to pick up the flavour94.

No. 20DurianSulphur

Singaporeand Southeast Asia

When to smell itWhenever the husk is split open

Durian is barred from Singapore’s MRT trains, where SMRT introduced a S$500 fine for carrying one in 1988, and many hotels charge guests who bring one in96.

German food chemists took Thai Monthong durian apart in 2012 and found 44 odour-active compounds, 24 of them never before reported in the fruit97. In 2017 they reported that just two, at their natural concentrations, reproduce the smell of durian pulp: ethyl (2S)-2-methylbutanoate, which smells fruity, and 1-(ethylsulfanyl)ethane-1-thiol, which smells of roasted onion95.

The durian genome, published the same year, showed an expanded family of methionine gamma-lyase genes, which are linked to the production of volatile sulphur compounds and are more active in the fruit98.

  • 1-(Ethylsulfanyl)ethane-1-thiolroasted onion

Background IWhy a smell finds a memory

Smell takes a shorter road into the brain than the other senses. The olfactory bulb sends its signals straight to the piriform cortex and to the amygdala, which handles emotion, with no relay through the thalamus first, and on to the entorhinal cortex, the doorway to the hippocampus and memory99. Scientific American has called smell the only sense that reaches the memory system without first passing through the thalamus100.

The psychologist Rachel Herz found that memories called up by a smell carry more emotion than those cued by pictures, words, touch or sound100, 101. In a small brain-imaging study in 2004, with five participants, a personally meaningful perfume produced more activity in the amygdala and hippocampus than other cues for the same memory102. Whether smell memories are also more vivid is less clear: one 2012 study found them more detailed and arousing than memories cued by music, but no different from those cued by coloured lights, and no more vivid103.

They do seem to be older. When Maria Larsson and Johan Willander tested 93 older adults, memories triggered by smells came mostly from the first decade of life, while words and pictures brought back memories from between 11 and 20. The smell-cued memories also came with a stronger sense of being taken back in time104. Literature had got there first: Marcel Proust published Swann’s Way, with its madeleine dipped in tea, in 1913105. Strictly, that is taste and smell together.

How many smells can a person tell apart? People carry 388 potentially working olfactory receptor genes, by one count, and more than 400 broken ones106; Richard Axel and Linda Buck won the 2004 Nobel Prize for discovering how that receptor family is organised107. A 2014 paper in Science estimated that humans can discriminate at least a trillion odours108. Two papers the next year took the method apart; one began “This claim is wrong”109, 110. The honest answer is that nobody knows.

Background IIHow a perfumer builds a smell

A perfume is built in tiers according to how fast its materials evaporate. Top notes are the most volatile, the first impression, and fade fastest: citrus such as bergamot. Heart notes come through as the top fades, usually the florals. Base notes evaporate slowest and last for hours: woods, musks, resins. Their slow fade is the “dry-down”111. Most of the places in this guide supply one tier or another.

Weight is a poor guide to strength. In rose oil, β-damascenone is about 0.14% of the material and around 70% of the smell1; in durian, two compounds at their natural levels are enough to recreate the fruit95. Perfumers think in odour strength, and the trace compounds are where natural materials get their character.

Synthetics widened the palette. Vanillin was first made in 187485. Ernest Beaux’s Chanel No. 5 of 1921 laid a dose of aldehydes, unusual at the time, over Grasse jasmine and rose; the aldehydes include 2-methylundecanal112, 113. Hedione, an analogue of a jasmine molecule developed at Firmenich, was first used in Dior’s Eau Sauvage in 1966; accounts of when it was first made differ114, 115. Iso E Super makes up about a quarter of Dior’s Fahrenheit114.

A cut flower smells different from a living one, which is why the industry learned to sample the air around flowers still on the plant. IFF’s chemists showed how much jasmine changes once picked8. At Givaudan, Roman Kaiser began working on reconstructions of natural scents in 1975. His method encloses a living flower in a glass vessel for up to two hours, catches its scent in an adsorbent trap and analyses it; by 2006 he had analysed more than 1,900 scents116.

Then come the rules. The International Fragrance Association issues standards that ban some materials, cap the amounts of others and set purity specifications, based on safety assessments by an independent expert panel117. The bergapten limit on bergamot oil is one of them32.
