A writer’s casebook · Ten forgeries

The Forgery Casebook

Ten forgeries that fooled the Louvre, the Met, the Getty, the British Museum and the dealers who supply them: what each was meant to be, who vouched for it, and the eye, the paper, the laboratory or the informant that finally gave it away.

The usual story about art forgery runs like this: the experts were fooled by their own eyes until a scientist put the picture under a microscope and found a pigment that had not been invented yet. That story is true often enough to survive. It happened to Wolfgang Beltracchi, whose 1914 Campendonk turned out to contain titanium white39, and to the Knoedler gallery, whose “Motherwells” held red pigments that were not in use until the 1960s50.

Line up ten of the forgeries that fooled great museums and dealers, though, and the laboratory is the first thing to break the case only twice. Three times it was a curator or art historian who simply looked harder. Four times it was a person who talked: a jeweller writing to a newspaper, a former partner carrying a bag of papers to the police, a forger who confessed to escape a worse charge. Once the paper trail itself unravelled. And one of the most famous cases, the Getty kouros, has never been closed at all, because the science came back ambiguous.

What follows is a casebook for writers: each forgery as the museum’s wall label once described it, set against what was found, then how the case broke and where the object is now. Facts that sources disagree on are marked as such.

How this was judged: the “first break” in each case is the desk’s reading of the sources, meaning the evidence that turned suspicion into exposure. Most cases used more than one route, and the matrix below shows all of them.

Four ways a fake gets caught

The eye3

A specialist notices something wrong in the work itself: the same hand in two “different” artists, a harness that no Assyrian sculptor carved, a building that did not yet exist.

The paper1

The provenance, the sale record or the letters fall apart: a postcode that did not exist yet, a buyer who traces back to a forger.

The lab2

Materials analysis finds something that could not have been there on the claimed date.

The informant4

Somebody talks: an accomplice, a rival dealer, a former partner, or the forger.

Counts are first breaks across ten cases, with the Getty kouros included under “paper” because its provenance letters were the strongest evidence against it, although the case is unresolved.

The cases

Showing all ten cases, roughly in the order they were first accepted.

I

The Tiara of Saitaphernes

Musée du Louvre, Paris · bought 1896 · exposed 1903
Wall label
Tiara of the Scythian king Saitaphernes
Greek, 3rd century BC, said to be from Olbia on the Black Sea. Gold, about 7 inches tall. Bought 1 April 1896 for 200,000 francs.1
What gave it away
A goldsmith in Odessa, and a jeweller who had watched him work
Traces of modern tools, hidden modern soldering and the style of the inscription.1
Informant · firstEyePhysical exam

The tiara is a band of gold worked with scenes from the Iliad and from Scythian daily life, and an inscription saying the people of Olbia gave it to King Saitaphernes1. It was offered in Vienna in February 1896, where the imperial museum declined it, as did the British Museum. The Louvre bought it that spring12. The sellers’ name appears as Hochmann in one account and as the Gokhman brothers in another12.

Doubt came quickly. Within months the German archaeologist Adolf Furtwängler and a Russian professor, Vesselovsky, attacked it in print14. French critics put Furtwängler’s view down to spite12, and in November 1896 the director of fine arts, Henry Roujon, told parliament the tiara was an excellent acquisition4.

Seven years later the journalist Henri Rochefort was pressing for an inquiry when a jeweller named Lifschitz wrote to Le Matin to say he had watched his friend Israel Rouchomovsky, an Odessa goldsmith, make the thing12. Rouchomovsky travelled to Paris. He said Hochmann had ordered it “for an archaeologist friend”, named the picture books he had copied the scenes from, and then made a matching section in front of the investigators12. The orientalist Charles Clermont-Ganneau led the inquiry, and its conclusion that the tiara was wholly inauthentic reached the minister on 2 June 19033. Rouchomovsky, who said he had spent about seven months on it, presented himself as an unwitting accomplice2.

Now: still owned by the Louvre, which does not keep it on display. It was shown in a Louvre exhibition of fakes in 1954 and at the Israel Museum in 199712.

II

The Met’s Etruscan warriors

Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York · on view from 1933 · withdrawn 1961
Wall label
Three Etruscan warriors: the Old Warrior, the Colossal Head and the Big Warrior
Terracotta, believed to be about 2,500 years old. Bought through the museum’s agent John Marshall. Published by the curator Gisela Richter in 1937.56
What gave it away
A signed confession, a missing thumb and manganese in the glaze
Joseph Noble’s tests found manganese dioxide in the black glaze, which Time described as a modern colouring agent.6
Informant · firstLabEye

Marshall was excited. Of the Colossal Head he wrote: “I can find nothing approaching it in importance”5. The museum’s ceramics adviser, Charles Binns, judged the black glaze to be ancient6, and the three figures went on show in 19335.

The warning signs were early and specific. In 1936 a dealer, Pietro Tozzi, wrote to Richter naming “Fioravanti” and the “Riccardi Bros.”; in 1937, according to the Met curator Dietrich von Bothmer, the Italian Etruscologist Massimo Pallottino called the statues forgeries; through the 1940s and 1950s the dealer Harold Parsons and others kept raising doubts5. In 1959 Parsons tracked down Alfredo Fioravanti in Rome, and on 5 January 1961 Fioravanti signed a deposition at the US consulate saying he had made the figures with members of the Riccardi family5.

The detail that convinced people was physical. One warrior’s hand had lost its thumb. Von Bothmer made a plaster cast of the hand, and Fioravanti produced a terracotta thumb he had kept for decades. It fitted56. The forgers built the big figures in pieces because they had no kiln large enough to fire them whole6. Fioravanti told Time the forgers had been paid “a few hundred” dollars, and believed the dealer got at least $40,000 for one figure6.

Sources differThe Met’s announcement is dated 14 February 1961 by the Archaeological Institute of America5; other accounts give 15 February. One later obituary of Noble puts his glaze test in 1967, which conflicts with contemporary reporting from 19616.

Now: declared of doubtful authenticity in February 19615. The museum’s full findings were published that year by von Bothmer and Noble7.

III

Han van Meegeren’s Vermeers

Museum Boijmans, Rotterdam · bought 1937 · exposed 1945
Wall label
Johannes Vermeer, The Supper at Emmaus
Oil on canvas, signed “IVMeer”. Bought 1937 for 520,000 guilders, with the Rembrandt Society’s help. Endorsed by Abraham Bredius in the Burlington Magazine, November 1937.8911
What gave it away
A sale to Göring, then the forger’s own confession
Phenol-formaldehyde resin (a Bakelite-type plastic) in the paint; cobalt blue, a 19th-century pigment.1115
Confession · firstPaperLab

Bredius, the grand old man of Dutch art history, examined the Emmaus in September 1937 and declared it a Vermeer in the Burlington Magazine that November11. The Boijmans bought it the same year98. Six years later, in 1943, Hermann Göring acquired another “Vermeer”, Christ and the Adulteress, for 1.65 million guilders through the dealer Alois Miedl1113.

That sale undid the forger. Göring’s paintings were recovered in May 194511, investigators traced the Adulteress back to the painter Han van Meegeren, and he was arrested that month on a charge of collaboration1012. His defence was that he had painted it himself, along with the Emmaus. To prove it he painted a new “Vermeer”, Jesus among the Doctors, in 19451014; one account says he worked under police guard in a rented house, while Time reported at the time that he painted it in his cell1014.

Proof in law came from the Belgian chemist Paul Coremans, whose commission examined the paintings and materials from the studio. It found traces of phenol-formaldehyde resin, a bottle of which was in the studio, and cobalt blue, a pigment Vermeer could not have used1115. His report was published in English in 194910. The trial opened in Amsterdam on 29 October 1947 with the charge changed from collaboration to forgery and fraud; on 12 November he was given the minimum sentence of one year101112. He died of a heart attack on 30 December 1947 before serving it1211.

The case became a set piece in physics as well as art history. In 1967 Bernard Keisch and colleagues in Pittsburgh published a method in Science for dating lead white by its natural radioactivity: in lead white made recently, lead-210 is out of balance with the radium-226 that produces it, while in pigment centuries old the two have long since settled into equilibrium16. The paper itself used hypothetical paintings. In a later booklet for the US Atomic Energy Commission, written as a dialogue, Keisch said the method had been applied to van Meegeren’s paintings and that every one tested “couldn’t possibly have been more than 30 or 40 years old”17. This is the “lead isotope” test often told in connection with the Emmaus, although it came two decades after the trial.

Sources differThe Emmaus price is given as 520,000 guilders and as 520,000–550,0001011. The museum’s own record lists the work as a 1939 loan from its foundation8.

Now: the Emmaus is in the Boijmans collection, in storage, and the museum published new pigment analysis of it in 201189. Christ and the Adulteress is at Museum de Fundatie10.

IV

Eric Hebborn’s Old Master drawings

National Gallery of Art, Washington; British Museum; Morgan Library; Copenhagen · exposed from 1978
Wall label
Anthony van Dyck, Christ Crowned with Thorns
Drawing. British Museum, bought from Colnaghi in 1970.19
What gave it away
One curator saw one hand behind two “different” Renaissance artists
Konrad Oberhuber, National Gallery of Art, Washington, 1970s.18
Eye · firstConfession

Hebborn, a British artist, sold drawings through Colnaghi’s, Sotheby’s and Christie’s as the work of Old Masters. Copenhagen’s royal collection paid £16,000 for a “Piranesi”, by its curator’s account (Hebborn said £14,000)18.

The break came from looking. Konrad Oberhuber, a curator at the National Gallery of Art in Washington, recognised one hand behind a “Cossa”, a “Sperandio” and a “de Roberti” that had passed through the trade, and traced them back to Hebborn18. In 1978 the journalist Geraldine Norman’s reporting in The Times ended his career on the market18. He was never charged20.

Hebborn then made his name by boasting. In 1990 he wrote to say the British Museum’s Van Dyck was his, and in January 1991 the museum moved it to its fakes box19. His memoir, Drawn to Trouble, appeared in 1991 and claimed about 1,100 forgeries; The Art Newspaper’s reviewer questioned the figure and counted about 40 that had surfaced since 197818. He was found in a Rome street with a severe head injury in 1996 and died; whether he was murdered has never been established19.

Sources differThe tidy version says Oberhuber spotted two drawings from Colnaghi on the same paper. The Art Newspaper’s 1991 review says the “Cossa” came from a different dealer, Calmann, and that Oberhuber kept the “Sperandio” because it was on correct paper; what he recognised was the hand18.

Now: eight works registered as Hebborns are on the Art Loss Register19.

V

The Getty kouros

J. Paul Getty Museum, Los Angeles · on approval 1983 · bought 1985 · unresolved
Wall label
Kouros. Greek, about 530 BC, or modern forgery
Thasian dolomitic marble. Accession 85.AA.40. The Getty’s own catalogue record carries both dates.2421
What gave it away (perhaps)
A letter dated 1952 with a postcode introduced in 1972
Surface crust: first read as calcite, later found to be calcium oxalate. Inconclusive.22
Paper · strongestLab, ambiguousEye, divided

A marble youth arrived at the Getty on approval in September 1983 from the dealer Gianfranco Becchina, with papers placing it in a Swiss collection2122. The purchase was approved in January 1985 at a reported $9.5 million21 (another account says “nearly $9 million”23), and the curator Marion True announced it publicly in 19872122.

Its papers said it had been in a Geneva collection since the 1930s. True herself later wrote that a German expert had found the photocopied letters to be “cleverly manufactured composites”, and that “though the letter is dated 1952, the postal code on the letter head did not come into existence until 1972”22. In 1990 a fake kouros torso in the same kind of marble turned up; the Getty bought it as a study piece22.

Here the laboratory did not settle anything. The geologist Stanley Margolis studied the surface, and his work was read as proof of an ancient calcite crust; later study found calcium oxalate, which the Getty’s conservator still thought pointed to long-term alteration, and which True noted can come from lichens but is also used for cleaning stone22. A colloquium in Athens on 25–27 May 199222 drew about 130 scholars and reached no consensus, although, The Art Newspaper reported, “none of the Greek scholars present considered the Kouros to be a genuine piece”23.

Now: the museum’s record still reads “about 530 BCE or modern forgery”24. In 2018, according to a Los Angeles Times report cited by the archaeologist David Gill, it was taken off display; the director, Timothy Potts, was reported as saying it had not been formally downgraded25.

VI

John Drewe and John Myatt

Tate, V&A and ICA archives; London and New York salerooms · c. 1987–1996
Wall label
Works by Giacometti, Nicholson, Chagall, Dubuffet, de Staël and others
Provenance: Hanover Gallery, O’Hana Gallery; recorded in gallery papers held in the Tate archive.2627
What gave it away
A former partner took a stash of documents to the Tate and the police
The archive records themselves had been altered.2926
Informant · firstPaper

This is the case where the forgery was the paperwork. John Myatt, a painter, placed an advertisement in Private Eye offering “genuine fakes”; Drewe answered it, and Myatt went on to paint pictures in the manner of Chagall, Giacometti, Dubuffet, Nicholson, de Staël and others2627. What sold them was John Drewe’s paperwork.

The court heard that Drewe gave the Tate £20,000, was thanked in its annual report, and used his access to its archive to insert photographs of the fakes into the records of the Hanover and O’Hana galleries2627. He put a fake O’Hana Gallery catalogue into the V&A’s National Art Library2628, and gave two fakes to the Institute of Contemporary Arts, which gave him access to its records29. Scholars and estates accepted works, including Sir Alan Bowness for Nicholson and the Dubuffet Foundation26.

The dealer Peter Nahum went to the police in 1995 and Christie’s grew suspicious in 199627, but the decisive evidence came from Bat-Sheva Goudsmid, who had lived with Drewe for years and, after they split, took documents she had found to the Tate and to the police29. At Southwark Crown Court in February 1999 Drewe was convicted of conspiracy to defraud, forgery and other offences and jailed for six years; Myatt, who gave evidence, received one year and served four months262730. Scotland Yard called it “the biggest contemporary art fraud of the twentieth century”26.

Sources differTotals vary: about 200 fakes with about 60 traced26; over 60 traced and “at least a further 110” unidentified, citing Scotland Yard in 199928; 60 traced and about 140 untraced29. No later official count was found.

Now: on the 1999 figures, well over a hundred of the paintings were never identified28.

VII

Knoedler and the Rosales paintings

M. Knoedler & Co., New York · 1994–2009 · gallery closed 2011
Sale record
Mark Rothko, untitled
Oil on canvas. Sold by Knoedler in 2004 to Domenico and Eleanore De Sole for $8.3 million. Knoedler had paid $950,000.48
What gave it away
Two red pigments not used until the 1960s, in “Motherwells” said to date from the 1950s
Forensic analysis by James Martin, commissioned by the gallery.50
Lab · firstPaperEye

Between 1994 and 2009 a Long Island dealer, Glafira Rosales, supplied more than 60 works presented as Pollocks, Rothkos, Motherwells and others, with a story about a collector who, prosecutors said, never existed4445. According to the US Attorney’s Office, she sold them to two Manhattan galleries for about $33.2 million, and they resold them for more than $80 million45. Knoedler, which had been trading for 165 years, was one of them5148.

Some specialists doubted the works for years; Jack Flam said in 2007 that the Motherwells were not authentic49, and in 2009, after forensic analysis, the Dedalus Foundation withdrew its endorsement of a Motherwell52. The laboratory finding that later became central in court was Martin’s: two red pigments in the “Motherwells” that were not used until the 1960s. Martin testified in 2016 that a researcher working for the gallery asked him to delete those findings, and that “I refused to do that”50. Knoedler closed abruptly in November 201151.

Rosales pleaded guilty in September 2013 to nine counts including wire fraud and money laundering, agreed to forfeit $33.2 million45, and in 2017 was sentenced to time served and home detention46. In 2014 prosecutors indicted Pei-Shen Qian, a painter in Queens, and two brothers on charges that included wire fraud conspiracy; Qian is believed to be in China, and the charges against him remain unproven4746. She is the only person to have pleaded guilty53.

The De Soles’ civil case against Knoedler and its former president, Ann Freedman, went to trial in Manhattan in 2016 and settled before a verdict, on undisclosed terms4849. Freedman and the gallery denied knowingly selling fakes; her lawyer said she believed the paintings were “the most important discovery in art history”49. No court has found that either knew.

Now: the gallery is closed and the civil suits have settled4651.

VIII

The Bolton “Amarna Princess”

Bolton Museum · bought 2003 · exposed 2006
Wall label
Statue of a princess of the Amarna period
Egyptian, about 1350 BC. Alabaster. Bought 2003 for £440,000 with help from the National Heritage Memorial Fund and the Art Fund. Authenticated via Christie’s and the British Museum.3331
What gave it away
A different fake: “Assyrian” reliefs with the wrong horse harness and a spelling mistake in the script
Spotted by British Museum curators.34
Eye · first

Shaun Greenhalgh made the princess in a garden shed in about three weeks3135. His father, George, posed as the owner or heir of the objects the family sold35. The statue passed through Christie’s and a British Museum report before Bolton bought it3336.

The family overreached. They offered the British Museum a set of stone reliefs showing a bearded horseman leading two horses, for £500,00032. Curators noticed the harnesses were atypical and that the ancient Mesopotamian inscription contained a spelling mistake, and they alerted Scotland Yard’s Art and Antiques Unit, which investigated for 18 months3432. That inquiry led back to the Amarna figure, which was revealed as a fake in 200633.

At Bolton Crown Court the family admitted conspiring to defraud art institutions between 1989 and 2006 and laundering the proceeds, which were at least £850,0003135. Shaun was jailed in November 2007; his mother, Olive, received a suspended sentence, and George a two-year suspended sentence in 20083235.

Sources differShaun Greenhalgh’s sentence is reported as four years eight months3234 and as four years3133. Accounts date the British Museum approach to 200534 or to February 200632.

Now: Bolton displays the fake, openly, in its Egyptology gallery, reopened in 201836.

IX

Beltracchi’s Campendonk

Lempertz, Cologne · sold 2006 · arrests 2010 · trial 2011
Sale record
Heinrich Campendonk, Red Picture with Horses (Rotes Bild mit Pferden), 1914
Sold at Lempertz, Cologne, 2006, to a Maltese company, Trasteco, for about €2.9 million. Label: “Sammlung Flechtheim”.3738
What gave it away
Titanium white, a pigment not in use in 1914
Found in scientific analysis, later described by the scientist Nicholas Eastaugh. Titanium white came into use as an artists’ pigment only from the 1920s.3941
Lab · firstPaper

Wolfgang Beltracchi’s fakes came dressed in provenance. Some carried stickers from the collection of Alfred Flechtheim, a celebrated dealer of the 1920s40. The leading Max Ernst scholar, Werner Spies, had authenticated at least five of the Ernst fakes4042.

When the Campendonk was analysed, it was found to contain titanium white, a pigment that “was not in use when the painting was purportedly created”39. Investigators also noticed that Flechtheim had never produced stickers like the ones on the backs of the paintings40. The defendants had been in custody since August 201042.

In October 2011 a Cologne court sentenced Wolfgang Beltracchi to six years and Helene to four, on charges covering 14 works that had brought in more than €10 million3743. Police had listed 53 suspected fakes40. In 2012 a Cologne court ordered Lempertz to pay damages, saying the painting should have been scientifically examined, and the case was settled that December38. Beltracchi told Der Spiegel in 2012 that he had forged around 50 artists43; that is his claim, and has not been tested in court.

Sources differThe Campendonk’s price is reported as about €2.9 million3738, €2.8 million41 and €2.4 million42, perhaps reflecting hammer price against price with fees.

Now: both Beltracchis were released early41.

X

The Musée Terrus

Elne, Pyrénées-Orientales · alarm raised 2017 · announced 2018
Wall label
Étienne Terrus, landscapes of Roussillon
Collection of the town museum, bought over about 20 years. Terrus died in 1922.5456
What gave it away
A building in one landscape that went up decades after the painter died
NPR reported a structure built in 1958. Other signs: cotton supports instead of canvas, and a signature that came off under a white glove.5455
Eye · firstMaterials

The smallest museum here had the largest problem. The art historian Éric Forcada, hired to rehang the collection after the building’s restoration, raised the alarm in August 201756. A committee of experts then judged 82 of the museum’s works to be fakes, more than half the collection5456.

The giveaway did not need a laboratory. Some of the landscapes showed buildings that did not exist before 1922; one, according to NPR, showed a structure built in 195854. The mayor, Yves Barniol, announced the findings at the reopening on 27 April 201856.

Sources differThe collection is given as 140 works5556 and as about 13454. The cost to the town is given as about $200,000 spent on acquisitions54 and as €160,000 in estimated damage56.

Now: the town filed a complaint against persons unknown in 2018 and police seized the works for investigation; no forger had been publicly identified in the reports consulted5456.

Why the eye failed, and when it didn’t

The forger paints the expert’s idea of the artist

Rouchomovsky copied his scenes from published picture books12: the tiara was assembled from published images of what ancient work was known to look like. A fake built from the reference books will tend to agree with the reference books. A connoisseur compares a new work with an internal picture of the artist. A forger who has studied the same books can aim at that picture.

Paper builds belief faster than paint

Myatt’s paintings were made convincing by archive records Drewe had doctored26. Rosales’s works came with a collector prosecutors said never existed45; Beltracchi’s with Flechtheim stickers that Flechtheim never made40; the kouros with letters True later described as composites22. Provenance is meant to be the sceptic’s tool. In these cases it was the forger’s.

Institutions want the find

“I can find nothing approaching it in importance,” wrote the Met’s agent of the Colossal Head5. The French establishment treated Furtwängler’s objections to the tiara as jealousy1. The Met had a dealer’s letter naming the forger in 1936 and kept the warriors on show for another 25 years5. The doubters were usually there early. They were overruled.

Chemistry answers a narrower question

Analysis asks whether a material could have existed on the claimed date. When the answer is no, as with titanium white in a 1914 picture39 or 1960s reds in a 1950s canvas50, it is close to decisive. When the forger uses period materials, or when the object is carved stone rather than paint, the answer can come back ambiguous for decades, as it has with the kouros22. And a laboratory report only works if someone acts on it: Martin testified that he was asked to take his findings out50.

The eye still catches forgers

Three of these ten were first exposed by people looking at the objects: a curator who recognised one hand behind several attributions18, British Museum specialists who knew how Assyrian sculptors carved a harness34, and an art historian hired to rehang a small museum who saw that much of its collection was wrong5556.
