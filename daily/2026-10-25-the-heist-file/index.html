The Heist File

Five documented jewel thefts and what courts and investigators established about each, how stolen luxury goods are traced, why video evidence misleads, and a glossary of the trade, for writers who want the public record kept apart from invention.

By Nick Carbone · about 20 minutes

Item
Five case files
Period
2003 – 2026
Status
One case open

Heist stories sell the plan. The public record is better on everything that happens afterwards: the discarded rubbish bag, the car number plate that leads to a front door, the defendants in their seventies, the jewels that are never seen again. This file is built from that record. It covers five thefts between 2003 and 2025, how investigators and courts dealt with each, where stolen luxury goods tend to go, the checks that exist to catch them, and why a face on a security camera persuades people more than it should.

What this file leaves outIt describes what happened, how each case was investigated and what the courts found. It gives no detail of how security, alarms, verification or surveillance were defeated, and it does not speculate about who might be involved in cases that are still open. People are named only if they are public figures or were convicted and widely reported. Anything that can change is dated: the Louvre case, in particular, was still before investigating judges on 25 October 2026.

Evidence grades used in this file

ESTABLISHED
A court finding, a conviction or an official record or statement.
REPORTED
Reported by reputable outlets or investigators but not tested in court.
CLAIMED
An account given by one party, unverified or disputed by others.

01 · CHRONOLOGYFrom theft to verdict

Arrests often come fast. Verdicts take years, and the loot is usually gone long before the court sits. The 2017 theft of a 100 kg gold coin in Berlin is included because two of the men convicted over Dresden were already serving sentences for it.15

02 · CASE FILESFive thefts on the record

Each file gives the established outline first, then the points where accounts diverge, then a note on what the case offers a writer. Values are given as estimated by the source named; they are estimates, and in several cases the parties disagree about them.

FILE 01
ANTWERP, BELGIUM · WEEKEND OF 15–16 FEBRUARY 2003

The vault under the Diamond Centre

TakenLoose diamonds, gold, other jewellery
EstimateOver US$100m (Guinness World Records)
ConvictedFour men, led by Leonardo Notarbartolo
RecoveredMost of the diamonds never found

Over a weekend in February 2003, a vault beneath the Antwerp Diamond Centre was emptied of loose stones, gold and jewellery. Guinness World Records lists it as the largest diamond heist and says the industry calls it the biggest in history.1

  • ESTABLISHED The morning after, a landowner near the E19 motorway between Antwerp and Brussels found rubbish the gang had dumped and called the police. Items in it were linked to Notarbartolo, and DNA from a partly eaten sandwich tied the group to the crime.1,2
  • ESTABLISHED Notarbartolo was arrested on 21 February 2003 when he returned to the Diamond Centre. The Antwerp Court of Appeal sentenced him to ten years in 2005; three accomplices received five years each.2
  • ESTABLISHED He was paroled in 2009. A European Arrest Warrant followed in 2011 after he broke parole conditions, including a requirement to compensate victims; he was arrested in Paris in 2013 and served the rest of his sentence until 2017.2
  • REPORTED The vault had reportedly been refused an insurance policy because of its security flaws, according to the journalists Scott Andrew Selby and Greg Campbell in their book Flawless.2
  • CLAIMED In a 2009 interview with Wired, Notarbartolo said a diamond merchant had hired the gang, that only about €18m was taken and that the theft was an insurance fraud.2 The lead detective, Agim De Bruycker, told the Columbia Journalism Review: “Not one detail of Leo’s story can be confirmed by the facts in the investigation.” CJR criticised Wired for not putting the story to the police.3
For the writerThe best-known version of this heist is the convicted man’s own, and the investigators say none of it checks out. Two incompatible accounts, each with a motive, sit on the public record side by side.
FILE 02
HATTON GARDEN, LONDON · EASTER WEEKEND, APRIL 2015

The safe deposit boxes

TakenJewels, gold and cash from 73 boxes
EstimateUp to £14m
ConvictedSeven sentenced in 2016, an eighth in 2019
RecoveredAbout a third, by early 2016

Over the Easter bank holiday of 2015, thieves broke into the underground vault of Hatton Garden Safe Deposit Ltd in London’s jewellery quarter and opened 73 boxes. The haul was put at up to £14m.4,7

  • ESTABLISHED Just after midnight on Good Friday, an alarm company told the Metropolitan Police that an intruder alarm had gone off at the building. The call was graded as needing no police response. The Met confirmed this and opened an inquiry into why.5
  • REPORTED Detectives went through footage from about 150 cameras, picked out a distinctive white Mercedes and traced it through the congestion charge database to the home of John “Kenny” Collins. They then bugged his car and another suspect’s.8 Officers recorded the men at the Castle pub in Islington discussing how to divide and sell the goods.6
  • ESTABLISHED Nine people were arrested on 19 May 2015. Brian Reader, then 76, and Collins, 75, were among four who pleaded guilty to conspiracy to burgle; three more were convicted in January 2016.4,6 In March 2016 Reader was sentenced to six years and three months; Collins, Daniel Jones, Terry Perkins and William Lincoln got seven years; Carl Wood six. A ninth defendant was acquitted.4
  • ESTABLISHED In January 2018 four of the men were ordered to pay back a total of £27.5m or serve seven more years.4
  • ESTABLISHED The man the press called “Basil”, who wore a mask, hat and wig on camera, went unidentified for about three years.9 Michael Seed was arrested in March 2018, when searches turned up about £143,000 of gold, gems and jewellery, and was jailed for ten years in March 2019.9,10 He denied the charges and told the court his electronic equipment was for scaring pigeons off his balcony.9
For the writerThe gang avoided mobile phones and were caught through a car, a congestion charge record and their own conversations in a pub. The most elusive member was filmed in a mask, hat and wig and stayed unnamed for three years.
FILE 03
PARIS, FRANCE · NIGHT OF 2–3 OCTOBER 2016

The robbery during fashion week

TakenJewellery including a ring of about $4m
EstimateAbout €10m (AFP)
ConvictedEight of ten defendants, May 2025
RecoveredOne dropped diamond cross

During Paris Fashion Week in 2016, robbers tied up Kim Kardashian in the luxury residence where she was staying and took jewellery that AFP put at about €10m, including an engagement ring of about $4m set with an 18.88-carat diamond.12

  • REPORTED More than a dozen people were detained in January 2017. AFP, citing police sources, said they were identified chiefly through DNA found at the residence, one sample matching a man already known to police.13
  • ESTABLISHED On 23 May 2025 a Paris court convicted eight of the ten defendants, many of them in their sixties and seventies. The ringleader, Aomar Aït Khedache, 69, received eight years with five suspended. Because of suspended terms and time already spent in pre-trial detention, none of those convicted was sent back to prison.11
  • ESTABLISHED According to the Associated Press, his DNA on the bands used to bind Kardashian was a key breakthrough, and wiretaps caught him giving orders and arranging to sell the diamonds in Belgium.11
  • REPORTED A diamond-encrusted cross dropped in the escape is the only piece ever recovered. Most of the rest is believed to have been sold in Belgium.11,12
  • REPORTED AFP noted that her frequent social media posts about her jewellery and whereabouts may have helped the robbers.12
For the writerThe victim was one of the most photographed people alive, and the jewellery was all over her own feeds. Almost none of it has been seen since. Almost nine years passed between the robbery and the verdict, and the defendants walked out of court free.
FILE 04
DRESDEN, GERMANY · 25 NOVEMBER 2019

The Green Vault

Taken21 pieces, more than 4,300 diamonds
EstimateInsured for at least €113.8m
ConvictedFive men, May 2023
Recovered31 objects returned in 2022

Before dawn on 25 November 2019, thieves broke into the Green Vault, the Saxon royal treasury in Dresden, and took 21 pieces of 18th-century jewellery holding more than 4,300 diamonds.14,15 A fire on a nearby bridge shortly before had caused a power cut in the area.15 The pieces were insured for at least €113.8m; the director-general of the Dresden State Art Collections, Marion Ackermann, said their material value did not begin to reflect their historical importance.14,16

  • ESTABLISHED Police raided 18 properties in Berlin in November 2020 and arrested three suspects; a fourth was arrested the following month.15
  • ESTABLISHED In December 2022, during the trial and after exploratory talks between prosecutors and the defence about a possible agreement, police recovered 31 items in Berlin.15,16
  • ESTABLISHED On 16 May 2023 Dresden Regional Court convicted five members of the Remmo family and acquitted a sixth defendant. Sentences ran up to six years and three months. The court cited partial confessions and the return of some treasures as reasons for lighter sentences.14,15
  • REPORTED The recovered pieces went back on display on 14 August 2024, some still not fully restored. An epaulette set with the diamond known as the Saxon White and the large breast ribbon of Queen Amalie Auguste have not been recovered.17
Linked case: the Big Maple Leaf, Berlin, 2017. A 100 kg gold coin issued by the Royal Canadian Mint was stolen from the Bode Museum in March 2017. In February 2020 a Berlin court sentenced two Remmo cousins to four and a half years and a museum security guard who had grown up with them to three years and four months; a fourth defendant was acquitted. Gold particles were found in a getaway car and on clothing, and experts believed the coin was broken up, melted and sold. It has never been found.18,38
For the writerThe defendants’ most useful asset in court was the stolen property itself. Returning most of it shortened their sentences, and the pieces that stayed missing were among the most famous.
FILE 05
PARIS, FRANCE · 19 OCTOBER 2025 · OPEN CASE

The Galerie d’Apollon

TakenEight pieces of the French crown jewels
Estimate€88m (Louvre curator, per prosecutor)
ChargedFive people; four in custody
RecoveredEmpress Eugénie’s crown, dropped and damaged

About half an hour after the Louvre opened on Sunday 19 October 2025, thieves entered the Galerie d’Apollon from outside and left with eight pieces of 19th-century royal and imperial jewellery in under eight minutes.19,20 The Paris prosecutor, Laure Beccuau, said the museum’s curator put the loss at €88m.19,20 The crown of Empress Eugénie was dropped during the escape and recovered damaged.19

  • ESTABLISHED Two men were arrested on 25 October, one at Charles de Gaulle airport as he was about to fly to Algeria. The prosecutor said both partially admitted the charges, organised gang theft and criminal conspiracy.20 By the end of November 2025, five people had been charged.19
  • ESTABLISHED According to the prosecutor, one suspect’s DNA was found on a display case and on items the thieves left behind.20
  • ESTABLISHED The museum’s director, Laurence des Cars, told a Senate committee on 22 October that the only camera installed on that façade faced west and did not cover the balcony where the thieves got in.21 She resigned in February 2026.19
  • ESTABLISHED A Cour des comptes audit covering 2018 to 2024, published in November 2025, found that only €3m of an estimated €83m security modernisation had been spent and that investment had favoured “visible and attractive operations”. Its head, Pierre Moscovici, called the theft “a deafening alarm bell”.22
  • REPORTED As of 19 October 2026, AFP reported that four main suspects were in jail awaiting trial, searches had found nothing and the jewels were still listed as missing in INTERPOL’s database.23
For the writerThe prosecutor called the jewels unsellable within days.20 A year later nobody outside the case knows whether they still exist in their original form. Everything about where they went is, for now, expert speculation.

04 · THE TRAILWhere stolen luxury goes, and where it gets checked

Every valuable object has two lives. One is physical: the stone, the setting, the gold. The other is documentary: the grading report, the auction catalogue entry, the insurance schedule, the museum inventory. A theft takes the object, and the paperwork stays behind. Much of what happens to stolen jewellery afterwards can be read as an attempt to separate the two.

The documented fates in these five cases follow that pattern. Investigators believed the Big Maple Leaf coin was melted down.18 The Paris jewels of 2016 are believed to have been sold in Belgium.12 For the Louvre pieces, experts quoted by AFP a year on offered competing guesses: hidden or buried soon after the theft, passed to a middleman, or broken up and the stones recut and sold through diamond centres abroad. One industry figure told AFP that recut diamonds have little trouble “re-entering the legitimate supply chain”. INTERPOL’s Corrado Catesi said stolen objects can take “from three months to 40 years” to resurface.23 All of that is opinion about an open case.

The checkpoints

Stolen-property registers

The Art Loss Register, founded in London in 1990, describes itself as the world’s largest private database of lost, stolen and looted art, antiques and collectibles, with more than 700,000 items. Auction houses, dealers, lenders and police search it; law enforcement uses it free.32 INTERPOL’s Stolen Works of Art database holds almost 57,000 objects and can be searched by anyone through its free ID-Art app.31 The Louvre jewels were added on 20 October 2025.19

Anti-money-laundering rules

Since 10 January 2020, UK “art market participants”, including dealers, auction houses and galleries, have had to carry out customer due diligence on transactions over €10,000, and since 2021 they have had to be registered with HMRC.33

Diamond certification

The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme exists to keep conflict diamonds out of trade, and its certificates apply to shipments of rough diamonds.34 Grading laboratories such as GIA can laser-inscribe a report number on a stone’s girdle, visible at 10× magnification, which links the stone to its report.35

Provenance at auction

Christie’s defines provenance as information about a lot’s current or prior ownership that may affect value.36 A gap in that history is what due-diligence searches, and buyers, look for. In France, buying the Louvre jewels would itself be a crime: the prosecutor warned that anyone who did would be guilty of concealing stolen goods.20

05 · IDENTIFICATIONWhy video evidence misleads

Security footage feels conclusive. The research on face matching says it is far less reliable than it looks when the person watching does not know the face, and the cases above contain several reminders of how easily an image is misread.

CAM 05 · FINDINGSREC ● 2026-10-25
  1. Strangers are poor at matching faces from video. In a 1999 series of experiments, people matching unfamiliar faces from high-quality video against photographs made many errors, which got worse when the viewpoint or expression differed. Their judgements leaned on external features such as hair and face outline. The authors urged caution about using video to identify offenders. 25
  2. Familiarity changes everything. A 2001 follow-up found people who knew the individuals identified them accurately even from poor-quality CCTV, while strangers performed very poorly. Brief familiarisation helped little. 26
  3. Training and experience do not guarantee accuracy. In a 2014 study, Australian passport officers accepted photo cards showing a different person about one time in seven, and experienced officers did no better than the average person. 27
  4. Juries face the same problem. A 2009 study simulating a disputed CCTV identification at trial found matching a defendant to video images “highly susceptible to error”, even in good conditions. 28
  5. Software can be wrong too. In January 2020 Detroit police arrested a man on the strength of a facial-recognition match between an old driving-licence photo and grainy footage of a 2018 watch theft. The charges were dropped. In 2024 the city paid $300,000 and agreed that officers may not arrest people on facial-recognition results alone. 29, 30
  6. The camera may be pointing elsewhere. The Louvre’s only camera on the affected façade faced west, away from the balcony the thieves used. 21
  7. A disguise can outlast the investigation. Hatton Garden’s “Basil” was caught on camera in a mask, hat and wig and stayed unidentified for about three years. 9 Before police had released any footage, a newspaper published its own CCTV and gave the men nicknames. 4
  8. An innocent face can become a suspect overnight. Hours after the Louvre theft, an AP photograph of a man in a fedora and three-piece suit walking past police outside the museum went viral. Online, people decided he was a detective, an inside man or an AI fake. He was a 15-year-old visitor who had come with his mother and grandfather, found the museum closed and knew nothing about the heist. 24

For a writer, the useful point is the asymmetry. A clip that a family member would recognise instantly can leave a room of strangers, a border officer or a jury confidently wrong, and confidence is no guide to accuracy.26,27

06 · FOR THE WRITERContradictions that generate stories

Each of these tensions is documented in the cases above. They are the parts of the record that fiction tends to skip.

  1. The famous piece is the hard one to sell

    A recognisable object is hard to sell intact. The Louvre prosecutor called the jewels unsellable; the coin in Berlin was believed melted for its gold.20,18

  2. The paper trail outlives the object

    A grading report or inventory entry survives the theft intact. A stone that has been recut no longer matches it.23,35

  3. Careful gangs leave careless traces

    A sandwich in a roadside rubbish bag in Antwerp; a car and a congestion charge record in London; DNA on the bindings in Paris.1,8,11

  4. The loot is a bargaining chip

    In Dresden, returning stolen pieces helped shorten sentences.14 An art detective told AFP of the Louvre case: “You only need one to talk, and in many cases, one talks.”23

  5. The thief’s account is the most read and the least checked

    Notarbartolo’s magazine version of Antwerp is the one many people know, and the detective in charge says none of it is confirmed.3

  6. The robbers are pensioners

    Hatton Garden’s ringleader was 76 at his arrest; most defendants in the Kardashian trial were in their sixties and seventies.6,11

  7. The great museum was under-protected

    The state auditor found most of the Louvre’s planned security spending had not happened, with money going to more visible projects.22

  8. The alarm worked, and nobody came

    At Hatton Garden the alarm call reached the police and was graded as needing no response.5

  9. The face everyone suspected belonged to a bystander

    The most widely shared image of the Louvre day shows a 15-year-old visitor who knew nothing of the theft.24

  10. Justice arrives after the jewels are gone

    The Paris verdict came nearly nine years after the robbery, and the convicted men served no more time.11

07 · GLOSSARYThe language of the luxury trade

Hammer price
The final bid announced when the auctioneer’s gavel falls.36
Buyer’s premium
The percentage on top of the hammer price that the buyer pays the auction house.36
Reserve
The minimum price the seller will accept for a lot.36
Estimate
The auction house specialists’ expected price range, before premium and taxes.36
Consignor
The person or business on whose behalf an auction house sells an item.36
Guarantee
An agreement that the seller receives a minimum price whatever happens in the sale room.36
Condition report
A specialist’s written description of a lot’s condition.36
Provenance
The record of an object’s current and previous ownership, which can affect its value.36
Memo
Consignment in the jewellery trade: a supplier lends stones or pieces to a retailer, who pays only when they sell. Short-term memo usually runs one to two weeks; long-term memo from 30 days to six months. It runs on trust.37
Laser inscription
A microscopic engraving on a diamond’s girdle, often its grading report number, visible at 10× magnification.35
Girdle
The thin outer edge at a cut diamond’s widest point, where inscriptions are placed.35
Rough
Uncut diamonds, the kind the Kimberley Process certifies.34
Kimberley Process
The certification scheme meant to keep rough conflict diamonds, used to fund rebel groups, out of legitimate trade.34
Art market participant
In UK law, a business dealing in art transactions over €10,000, which must check its customers and register with HMRC.33
Stolen Works of Art database
INTERPOL’s register of reported stolen cultural objects, almost 57,000 entries, searchable through the ID-Art app.31
Art Loss Register
A private London-based database of more than 700,000 lost, stolen and looted items, used for due-diligence searches.32
Confiscation order
A court order to repay criminal proceeds, with extra prison time for failing to pay, as the Hatton Garden men found.4
Organised gang theft
The French charge (vol en bande organisée) brought in the Louvre case, carrying up to 15 years.20
