# Daily queue

The top idea runs next morning. Order top to bottom is run order. Put new ideas under **Ideas**, above **Parked**.

**How to add an idea:** write a title on its own line, with the brief on the lines straight under it. Leave one blank line between ideas, and no blank lines inside an idea. (Starting the title with `## ` is fine too, but not needed.) One line of brief is enough, but two or three sentences on the angle get a much closer result. These optional lines help when you have a view:

- `Must include:` things the piece has to cover
- `Avoid:` angles, sources or formats to stay away from
- `Look:` a steer on design or format (a map, a timetable, an index card, anything)
- `Review: yes` publishes as normal, but flags the piece for you to check afterwards and opens a review checklist on GitHub

**How to request a correction:** add an idea whose title line starts `CORRECTION:` followed by the link to the piece, and say what's wrong underneath. Corrections run before the day's new piece and don't use up the day.

Ideas the desk can't do well (too thin, not evergreen, would need private individuals) are moved to **Parked** at the bottom with a note, and the next idea runs instead.

# Ideas

The Everyday Infrastructure Atlas
The question: What unusual or overlooked infrastructure makes certain cities especially pleasant to live in, navigate, or explore?
This is the most systems-oriented idea of the five. It looks at the practical mechanisms behind a city's character rather than merely describing its attractions.
What to include
- 30 examples of infrastructure that shapes daily life: ferries, funiculars, coastal trams, integrated rail terminals, public markets linked to transit, and pedestrian connections.
- The city problem each system solves.
- How residents use it, not just tourists.
- A short history and a practical example of using it.
- A comparison of similar systems in different cities.
Interactive element: An atlas filterable by infrastructure type, geography, and everyday function. Selecting a feature reveals a map pin, a short explanation, and an official information link.
Sources to start with: OpenStreetMap, GTFS public-transit data documentation, city transport agencies, and municipal open-data portals. GTFS documentation provides a useful framework for representing stops, routes, and trips in a structured way. 
Finish line: 30 documented examples across at least 10 cities, organized into six infrastructure categories.

The Lost Beer Styles Brewing Notebook
Research question: Which historical regional beers can a modern homebrewer realistically recreate using available ingredients?
Rather than collecting beer recipes indiscriminately, select styles with a documented regional identity and a clear reason to brew them.
Proposed contents
- Six styles, such as Kentucky Common, German Lichtenhainer, French bière de garde, Polish grodziskie, Scandinavian farmhouse ale, and a historically grounded London porter.
- Origins, historical period, and the evidence behind each reconstruction.
- Traditional ingredients versus modern substitutes.
- A practical recipe scaled to a five-gallon batch.
- Estimated original gravity, bitterness, fermentation temperature, and expected flavor.
- A sourcing list for specialty malt, hops, and yeast.
Visual element: A regional map of beer traditions with expandable recipe cards and a timeline.
Sources to start with: Brewers Association Beer Style Guidelines, Historical Beer: An Overview, and brewing-history resources from established brewing organizations. The Brewers Association's current guidelines include a dedicated historical beer category, while also recognizing that modern commercial examples don't always replicate historical beers exactly. 
Finish line: Six concise style profiles and one complete recipe ready for a future brew day. Clearly distinguish documented historical ingredients from modern reconstruction choices.

Proposed contents

- How steeping time, temperature, and ingredient size influence extraction.
- Three prototype directions: a bitter-citrus spritz base, a savory herbal tonic, and a ginger-spice concentrate.

Visual element: A botanical pairing matrix. Select a base ingredient and a desired flavor profile to reveal promising combinations and suggested starting ratios.

Finish line: A 12-ingredient reference table and three small-batch recipes with a standardized tasting worksheet. Keep any room-temperature storage claims out of scope unless the preservation process has been validated.

The Geography of Disappearing Wealth
Research question: How do luxury goods, money, and ownership move between legitimate commercial centers, and what can those networks tell us about the geography of modern crime?
This is the most direct extension of your Pink Panthers research. The focus is not on reconstructing how to commit a theft, but on the legitimate industries and cross-border relationships surrounding valuable objects.
Proposed contents
- A geographic dossier covering Antwerp, Belgrade, Milan, Monaco, Geneva, and London.
- The roles of jewelers, gem laboratories, auction houses, dealers, and luxury resale businesses.
- The difference between an object's physical movement and its documented ownership.
- Documented cases illustrating how investigators trace stolen goods.
- A glossary of trade terminology and an evidence table separating established facts from reporting and speculation.
Visual element: An interactive network map linking cities, institutions, industries, and documented cases. Each connection should explain what is established and cite its source.
Sources to start with: OCCRP, INTERPOL's stolen works of art database, and the New Yorker's reporting on the Pink Panthers. The latter is particularly useful for understanding how the investigation crossed national borders and why the group's organization remained uncertain. 
Finish line: Six city profiles, 15–20 documented entities or institutions, a sourced relationship map, and five case studies. Label allegations carefully and avoid implying that ordinary businesses are involved in crime without evidence.
Review: yes

Houses That Tell You Who Lived There
A building-by-building guide to landmark European houses and apartment buildings designed for wealthy clients, and what each one says about how its owners wanted to live. Where The Secret Geography of European Luxury maps districts, this one goes inside single buildings: the commission, the architect, the plan, the materials, and how the design signalled status in its day.
Include: 15 buildings from about 1890 to the present, favouring ones that are now museums, heritage-listed or thoroughly documented (the level of Villa Tugendhat, Palais Stoclet or Casa Malaparte). Avoid: current private residents, property prices, live listings.

Finish line: 15 building profiles, a timeline of styles, and a comparison matrix of how each house balances privacy against display.
