# Daily queue

The top idea runs next morning. Order top to bottom is run order. Put new ideas under **Ideas**, above **Parked**.

**How to add an idea:** write a title on its own line, with the brief on the lines straight under it. Leave one blank line between ideas, and no blank lines inside an idea. (Starting the title with `## ` is fine too, but not needed.) One line of brief is enough, but two or three sentences on the angle get a much closer result. These optional lines help when you have a view:

- `Must include:` things the piece has to cover
- `Avoid:` angles, sources or formats to stay away from
- `Look:` a steer on design or format (a map, a timetable, an index card, anything)

**How to request a correction:** add an idea whose title line starts `CORRECTION:` followed by the link to the piece, and say what's wrong underneath. Corrections run before the day's new piece and don't use up the day.

Ideas the desk can't do well (too thin, not evergreen, would need private individuals) are moved to **Parked** at the bottom with a note, and the next idea runs instead.

# Ideas

The Hotel Bar Atlas
A guide to hotel bars as urban third places: where you can sit comfortably, observe a city, meet people, and enjoy a sense of occasion without staying at the hotel.
Include: 20 bars in cities around the world, architectural identity, neighborhood context, price expectations, solo-visitor friendliness, best time to visit, and public access policies.
Finish line: a curated directory organized by atmosphere, not just by star rating. Favor places that offer a window into their city over generic luxury interiors.

The Secret Geography of European Luxury
Investigate where understated wealth actually lives: villa districts, seaside enclaves, resort towns, and overlooked neighborhoods with exceptional architecture and views.
Include: 10 locations, property styles, local wealth indicators, architectural history, nearby amenities, transport access, and representative properties or streets.
Finish line: a visual atlas that explains why each place feels distinctive. Use public property listings and documented examples rather than speculating about private residents or their finances.

The Invisible Systems That Keep Countries Connected
Explore the physical and commercial infrastructure behind the digital world: submarine cables, internet exchanges, mobile operators, satellite systems, and international data routes.
Include: 10 strategically interesting countries, major infrastructure, operators, dependencies, chokepoints, redundancy, and geopolitical implications.
Finish line: a sourced explainer with a map or network diagram and three case studies of how disruptions can affect ordinary users.

The Airport Systems Atlas
A guide to why airports work the way they do, how they connect to cities, and how travelers can make smarter routing decisions.
Include: hub structures, airline alliances, rail-air connections, terminal transfers, overnight connections, ground transport, alternative airports, and examples of counterintuitive routes.
Finish line: five worked journeys showing how to compare total travel time, transfers, ground access, and cost—not merely the headline airfare.

The Anatomy of a Modern Luxury Crime
A writer's reference to the environments, methods, and social machinery behind sophisticated contemporary thefts.
Include: real documented jewel thefts, luxury resale networks, auction houses, private security, identity verification, surveillance evidence, and the distinction between public facts and fictionalized mechanics.
Finish line: five case studies, a glossary of the luxury trade, and a collection of story-generating contradictions. Pay particular attention to mistaken identity and why video evidence can be misleading.

The Airport-to-City Reality Index
The question: Which airports make it easiest to start living like a local immediately after landing, and which quietly consume an hour of your trip?
This connects your interest in airport systems with your preference for efficient travel and avoiding unnecessary driving.
What to include
- 30 airports serving interesting European cities.
- Rail, metro, tram, and bus availability.
- Typical journey time to a central transit hub.
- Number of transfers and whether services run late.
- Whether the airport is actually near the city it claims to serve.
- A separate note for low-cost airports located far outside their advertised destination.
Interactive element: A sortable airport directory with filters for direct rail, journey time, overnight service, and transfer count. A simple scorecard could rate convenience, provided the scoring rules are visible.
Sources to start with: International Air Rail Organisation, Eurostat's airport transport dataset, and official airport and railway websites. The IARO directory is a particularly useful starting point for identifying airport rail links. 
Finish line: 30 airports, comparable access data for each, and a ranked list of the 10 most convenient arrivals.

The Everyday Infrastructure Atlas
The question: What unusual or overlooked infrastructure makes certain cities especially pleasant to live in, navigate, or explore?
This is the most systems-oriented idea of the five. It looks at the practical mechanisms behind a city's character rather than merely describing its attractions.
What to include
- 30 examples of infrastructure that shapes daily life: ferries, funiculars, coastal trams, integrated rail terminals, public markets linked to transit, and pedestrian connections.
- The city problem each system solves.
- How residents use it, not just tourists.
- A short history and a practical example of using it.
- A comparison of similar systems in different cities.
Interactive element: An atlas filterable by infrastructure type, geography, and everyday function. Selecting a feature reveals a map pin, a short explanation, and an official information link.
Sources to start with: OpenStreetMap, GTFS public-transit data documentation, city transport agencies, and municipal open-data portals. GTFS documentation provides a useful framework for representing stops, routes, and trips in a structured way. 
Finish line: 30 documented examples across at least 10 cities, organized into six infrastructure categories.

The Lost Beer Styles Brewing Notebook
Research question: Which historical regional beers can a modern homebrewer realistically recreate using available ingredients?
Rather than collecting beer recipes indiscriminately, select styles with a documented regional identity and a clear reason to brew them.
Proposed contents
- Six styles, such as Kentucky Common, German Lichtenhainer, French bière de garde, Polish grodziskie, Scandinavian farmhouse ale, and a historically grounded London porter.
- Origins, historical period, and the evidence behind each reconstruction.
- Traditional ingredients versus modern substitutes.
- A practical recipe scaled to a five-gallon batch.
- Estimated original gravity, bitterness, fermentation temperature, and expected flavor.
- A sourcing list for specialty malt, hops, and yeast.
Visual element: A regional map of beer traditions with expandable recipe cards and a timeline.
Sources to start with: Brewers Association Beer Style Guidelines, Historical Beer: An Overview, and brewing-history resources from established brewing organizations. The Brewers Association's current guidelines include a dedicated historical beer category, while also recognizing that modern commercial examples don't always replicate historical beers exactly. 
Finish line: Six concise style profiles and one complete recipe ready for a future brew day. Clearly distinguish documented historical ingredients from modern reconstruction choices.

The Botanical Drinks Laboratory
Research question: How can you create complex, savory, low-sugar drinks using herbs, spices, citrus, and other botanicals?
This is a practical guide to flavor experimentation, drawing on your interest in infusions and drinks that aren't dominated by sweetness.
Proposed contents
- A flavor reference for coriander seed, rosemary, mint, citrus peel, ginger, black pepper, and other botanicals.
- Water-based infusions versus oil- or alcohol-based extraction.
- How steeping time, temperature, and ingredient size influence extraction.
- Three prototype directions: a bitter-citrus spritz base, a savory herbal tonic, and a ginger-spice concentrate.
- A tasting sheet for aroma, bitterness, acidity, body, and finish.
- Ingredient cost per serving and batch scaling.
Visual element: A botanical pairing matrix. Select a base ingredient and a desired flavor profile to reveal promising combinations and suggested starting ratios.
Sources to start with: Harold McGee's Curious Cook for food chemistry, NIST Chemistry WebBook for reference data on individual compounds, and USDA FoodData Central for ingredient composition.
Finish line: A 12-ingredient reference table and three small-batch recipes with a standardized tasting worksheet. Keep any room-temperature storage claims out of scope unless the preservation process has been validated.

Radio Intelligence & Broadcast Verification Guide
The Concept: A technical field manual for verifying live news, eyewitness feeds, and radio broadcasts during major geopolitical events. Move beyond standard social media OSINT to focus on audio-visual signal verification and newsroom-grade source assessment.
Key Content: Cross-referencing shortwave/digital radio feeds, verifying live audio broadcasts (e.g., evaluating live coverage from international news outlets), audio spectrum analysis, verifying timestamps against daylight/timezones, and cross-checking eyewitness statements with local emergency services.
Finish Line: A clean, single-page verification flowchart plus a case study examining a breaking live news broadcast and how to spot synthetic audio or misattributed recordings.

The Geography of Disappearing Wealth
Research question: How do luxury goods, money, and ownership move between legitimate commercial centers, and what can those networks tell us about the geography of modern crime?
This is the most direct extension of your Pink Panthers research. The focus is not on reconstructing how to commit a theft, but on the legitimate industries and cross-border relationships surrounding valuable objects.
Proposed contents
- A geographic dossier covering Antwerp, Belgrade, Milan, Monaco, Geneva, and London.
- The roles of jewelers, gem laboratories, auction houses, dealers, and luxury resale businesses.
- The difference between an object's physical movement and its documented ownership.
- Documented cases illustrating how investigators trace stolen goods.
- A glossary of trade terminology and an evidence table separating established facts from reporting and speculation.
Visual element: An interactive network map linking cities, institutions, industries, and documented cases. Each connection should explain what is established and cite its source.
Sources to start with: OCCRP, INTERPOL's stolen works of art database, and the New Yorker's reporting on the Pink Panthers. The latter is particularly useful for understanding how the investigation crossed national borders and why the group's organization remained uncertain. 
Finish line: Six city profiles, 15–20 documented entities or institutions, a sourced relationship map, and five case studies. Label allegations carefully and avoid implying that ordinary businesses are involved in crime without evidence.

The World's Most Intriguing Private Addresses
Research question: Which residential enclaves, buildings, and neighborhoods communicate wealth and social position in ways that an ordinary luxury-property ranking misses?
This would combine your interest in overlooked European luxury, architectural detail, and the settings of modern geopolitical thrillers.
Proposed contents
- 15 residential enclaves across Europe, from coastal villas to urban palazzi and discreet modernist residences.
- Architectural character, local history, landscape, and relationship to the surrounding city.
- The difference between conspicuous luxury and understated prestige.
- Publicly documented examples of notable buildings and their architects.
- Nearby hotels, restaurants, transport, and public spaces that help explain the area's social geography.
- A short section on how a fictional character's choice of residence can reveal priorities, without reducing wealth to a single stereotype.
Visual element: A map-led atlas with filters for architectural style, coastal versus urban setting, historical versus contemporary design, and degree of urban integration.
Sources to start with: DOCOMOMO International, The World Monuments Fund, ArchDaily, and municipal architectural heritage inventories. Use public listings and published architectural coverage for property examples.
Finish line: 15 locations, 30 architectural references or images with proper attribution, and a comparative matrix explaining the distinctive character of each enclave.
