Wake Up Somewhere Else

Twenty overnight trains, ferries and coaches on five continents, timed from evening departure to morning arrival, with how much of the next day each one really gives you.

An overnight journey is a trade. You give up a hotel bed, and usually some sleep, and in return you keep the evening before and get the whole of the next day somewhere else. Some night services make that trade well. A train that arrives at 05:50 buys a long day with a short night; one that arrives at 11:00 buys a proper sleep and half a day. A few are less about the next day than about not losing one to the journey.

This directory sets out twenty routes on five continents: thirteen trains, five ferries and two coaches. For each it gives the times, what you sleep in, what is waiting when you get off, whether you can come back the same night, and what is most likely to go wrong. The times were checked on 8 November 2026 against the operator wherever its timetable could be read, and against independent timetables and guides where it couldn’t. Where sources disagree, the entry says so.

Clock times move. Many European timetables, the Caledonian Sleeper’s among them, run only to 12 December 2026,[1] and the Sunrise Izumo left Tokyo 24 minutes earlier from March 2026, which older guides still miss.[2] Treat every time here as a starting point and check your date before you book.

How to read the entries

Hours on the ground
The timetabled gap between arriving and the same evening’s departure home, on the same service or its return working. It ignores transfers, check-in and delays, so it flatters every route a little. Hull–Rotterdam is the exception: its port is 40 km out, so it is counted from coach to coach.
Swaps a hotel night
Under 12 hours aboard. The main gain is the bed: you sleep in transit instead of paying for a room.
Gives back a travel day
12 hours aboard or more. Made in daylight on the same kind of service, the trip would eat most of a waking day, so running it overnight hands that day back. This split is the desk’s own rule of thumb.
Short night
Arrives at or before 06:30.

The sleeping cars were built in East Germany in the early 1980s. Train 86 also makes a good Luxor train: it arrives there at 06:10.[125]

No route matches all of those filters. Try loosening one.

Five comparisons

Britain’s two sleepers

The Night Riviera and the Caledonian Sleeper are Britain’s only two sleeper services,[15] and they make opposite trades. The Riviera leaves Paddington at 23:45 and has you on the sea wall at Penzance by 07:50: a short night, but almost 14 hours before the 21:45 back.[9,10] The Fort William train leaves earlier, at 21:15, and takes until 10:00, so you get a full night and a little under ten hours in town before the 19:50 south.[1] Neither runs on Saturday nights.[1,9]

Out and back by sea

Every ferry in the directory turns round the same evening, so two nights aboard can buy one day ashore. How much of a day you get varies a lot. It is shortest in Helsinki: the Silja ship docks at 10:30 and its sister sails at 17:00.[42] Hull–Rotterdam gives about seven hours in the city between P&O’s coaches from Europoort, and P&O sells it as a mini cruise.[50] Sunflower’s Beppu run is the most generous of the three: it docks before seven, sails back at 18:45 and will keep your bags on board in between.[120,121]

Japan three ways

Night departures west from Tokyo and Osaka come as a bed, a seat or a cabin. The Sunrise Izumo’s private rooms reach Izumo at 10:00.[55] Willer’s WK156 coach reaches Kyoto at 06:25, after eight hours in a reclining seat with rest stops instead of a toilet, but leaves you more than 16 hours before a night bus back.[64,66] The Sunflower ferry from Osaka has a buffet and a large bath, and docks in Beppu at 06:55.[120]

The early ones

Four routes put you ashore at or before half past six: Lao Cai at 05:50, Devonport at 06:00, Kyoto at 06:25 and Heraklion at 06:30. They give some of the longest days in the directory and some of its shortest nights. From Lao Cai there is still an hour’s bus up to Sapa;[81] the Heraklion ferry calls at Milos at about one in the morning;[85] and the Spirit of Tasmania starts letting people off about half an hour after it docks.[131]

When the journey is the point

Three of the longest routes matter less for the day at the far end than for the day you don’t spend getting there. The Auto Train carries you and your car 855 miles in just under 17 hours.[107] Silja’s Stockholm–Helsinki crossing takes about 16¾ hours, by way of Åland and across a time zone.[42] The Cairo–Aswan sleeper takes 13 h 40 and reaches Luxor at 06:10, which makes it as useful a Luxor train as an Aswan one.[125]

Two routes on hold

Stockholm to Narvik. SJ runs the Swedish state’s night train to the far north, and has won the contract for December 2026 to December 2028.[132] Since April 2026 passengers for Kiruna, Abisko and Narvik have had to change trains at Boden. Through couchette and sleeping cars are due back from 13 December 2026, and tickets for 13 December to 9 January went on sale on 19 October.[133,134,135] No source we could read gave times for the restored train, so it stays out of the directory for now.

Tangier to Marrakech. Seat 61 reported in 2026 that Morocco’s overnight couchette train had been suspended indefinitely while the high-speed line to Marrakech is built.[136] ONCF’s own site could not be checked. The works have closed Marrakech station before, from 21 to 23 April 2026.[137]

How this was put together

Routes were chosen for a useful morning arrival, a spread of modes and continents, and an operator that publishes enough to check. Times, accommodation names and arrival details come from operators’ timetables, booking pages and press releases where those could be read, and otherwise from independent timetable sites and established guides such as The Man in Seat 61, named in each entry. Wikipedia is used only for history and background. Every page was read on 8 November 2026.

Fares appear only where an operator published them and they could be dated: the Night Riviera cabin supplements and SNCF’s private-compartment prices, both as listed on 8 November 2026. Hours on the ground are worked out from the timetables. Booking windows and departure counts are as stated by operators or guides on 8 November 2026. The labels for what a route saves are the desk’s judgement, applied by the 12-hour rule above.
