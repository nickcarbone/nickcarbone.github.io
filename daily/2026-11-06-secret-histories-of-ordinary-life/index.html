The Secret Histories of Ordinary Life

Ten systems you use before breakfast

Ten everyday systems, from house numbers and surnames to plugs and price tags, traced from what came before to who fixed them and what they quietly changed.

Published 6 November 2026 in Daily by Nick Carbone · Researched and designed with Claude · about 24 minutes

On 11 October 1770, officials in Vienna began painting numbers on houses. The Habsburg state wanted to know how many men lived where, so that it could recruit soldiers, and the number over the door marked a household that had been counted.1,2 Where houses were numbered, some people threw filth at the new numbers or scratched them off with iron tools, and nobles complained that their palaces were being numbered like the huts of the poor.1

That is the pattern this index keeps finding. A system that now feels like part of the furniture usually began as somebody's answer to a specific problem: a recruiting sergeant who could not find his men, a shop with too many clerks to trust each one to haggle, a wiring scheme that had to save cable after a war. The answer stuck, people built habits and machines around it, and the original reason faded from view.

Each entry below sets out what existed before, why the change came, who settled it and when, what it changed, and what is still with us. Each ends with a check on the most popular story told about it, because several of the best-known origin stories are wrong.

To count people

House numbers, surnames, passports and ID numbers. Each made a person easier for a state to find, tax, conscript or admit.

To move things

Postcodes, the side of the road and traffic lights. Each replaced local knowledge with a rule a stranger, or a machine, could follow.

To sell and serve

Price tags, plugs and hotel keys. Each let a business serve far more customers without trusting every one of them, or every member of staff.

To count people

House numbers

Painted over doors to find soldiers, then tax-payers, then everyone.

Before

Premises were known by signs, names and directions. London houses were known by their signs; one of Philadelphia's first city directories, in 1785, placed households by cross streets, such as "3rd above Chestnut".3,4

After

Every door carries a number in a predictable sequence, so a stranger, a postman or an ambulance crew can find it without asking.

The historian Anton Tantner, who has spent much of his career on the subject, places the origins of house numbering in the border zone between the military, the treasury and what was then called police science. Quartering and recruiting soldiers, collecting tax, controlling begging and insuring against fire all came before helping people find their way.1

The great push came in the Habsburg lands. In 1770 and 1771 commissions toured the Austrian and Bohemian provinces counting "souls" and numbering houses to prepare a new system of military recruitment, which is why the numbers are still called Konskriptionsnummern, conscription numbers.1,2 Some places had started earlier: Tyrol and Vorarlberg had house numbers from 1767.5 The system also sorted people. Houses owned by Jews were given Roman numerals and Christian houses Arabic ones, a distinction that lasted into the 19th century.1,5

Paris tried and failed several times. In 1779 Marin Kreenfelt, publisher of the Almanach de Paris, paid to have numbers painted along main streets, but residents, wary of burglars and tax collectors, painted over them and the scheme was abandoned.6 The rule that survived is a decree of 4 February 1805: even numbers on the right and odd on the left, with streets running away from the Seine numbered from the river end.6,7 The white-on-blue enamel plaques came in 1847.6

In London, Prescot Street in Goodman's Fields is the earliest street recorded with numbered houses, in 1708, according to the Postal Museum.3 Numbering stayed haphazard for another century and a half, often running up one side and back down the other, until the Metropolitan Board of Works, created in 1855, took control of street naming and numbering.3 Philadelphia added the idea most American cities now use: in 1856 it gave every block its own hundred, so a house between 6th and 7th Streets is numbered in the 600s.4,8

The geographer Reuben Rose-Redwood calls addressing a "political technology", one of the most basic ways a government taxes, polices and keeps track of where people live.9 Japan shows there are other ways to do it. Under a 1962 law most Japanese addresses number districts and blocks, and the streets themselves usually play no part.10

Dates

  • 1708Prescot Street, London, numbered3
  • 1770Habsburg conscription numbering; Vienna begins 11 October1,2
  • 1805Paris decree: odd and even sides, numbered from the Seine6,7
  • 1855Metropolitan Board of Works gets power over London numbering3
  • 1856Philadelphia's hundred-per-block system4
  • 1962Japan's block-based address law10

Still with us

In Vienna only a few numbers from the first round survive. The Federal Chancellery is Ballhausplatz 2, and conscription number 19 since an 1821 renumbering.1,2 Pall Mall and Downing Street in London still use the old up-one-side, down-the-other numbering.11

Myth check

Doubtful"British houses were numbered because of the Postage Act of 1765." The Act's text does not mention house numbers, and Prescot Street was numbered more than half a century earlier.3

To count people

Surnames

Fixed family names were often handed out by governments, sometimes from a printed list.

Before

In much of the world a person was known by a given name plus a father's name, a title, a trade or a home town. In the Philippines, many converts shared a handful of devotional names such as de los Santos or de la Cruz.12,13

After

A fixed surname passes down the generations and appears on every register, so the state can tell one Juan or one Mehmet from another.

The political scientist James C. Scott and colleagues argued in 2002 that permanent family surnames grew up alongside the modern state, as a way of making a population legible to it.14,15 The clearest cases are the ones where a government simply ordered names into existence.

In November 1849 (sources give the 21st or the 29th) the Spanish governor-general of the Philippines, Narciso Clavería, ordered families to take surnames from a printed Catálogo alfabético de apellidos of about 61,000 names. The stated problem was that duplicated names made censuses and tax collection unworkable.12,13 The catalogue was distributed to towns through provincial officials and parish priests.13 It contains native words as well as Spanish ones, and the historian Ambeth Ocampo notes that it is the reason a Spanish surname in the Philippines says nothing reliable about Spanish ancestry.12,13

Napoleon's decree of 20 July 1808 gave Jews in his empire who lacked fixed names three months to adopt them before a civil registrar. It banned names taken from the Old Testament and from towns, and threatened expulsion for anyone who did not comply.16

Turkey's Surname Law was adopted on 21 June 1934. Before it, people were commonly known by titles such as hacı, hoca or paşa, or by where they came from.17 On 24 November that year parliament gave Mustafa Kemal the surname Atatürk, "Father of the Turks", and two days later it abolished the honorifics Efendi, Bey and Pasha.18 Some people chose names from their trade, so a poultry seller became Kanat, "wing"; others were left with registrars' slips, such as Dündar for Dindar, "religious".17

Iceland went the other way. Most Icelanders still use patronymics ending in -son or -dóttir, and from 1925 the law banned the creation of new family surnames.19

Dates

  • 1808Napoleon's decree on Jewish surnames16
  • 1849Clavería's surname catalogue, Philippines12,13
  • 1925Iceland bans new surnames19
  • 1934Turkey's Surname Law, 21 June; Atatürk named 24 November17,18

Still with us

Because towns were assigned blocks of the alphabet, surnames in some Philippine towns still cluster around a single initial.13 In Iceland, a 1996 law created approved middle names and let the children of immigrants inherit surnames, while the ban on new Icelandic surnames remained.19

Myth check

Largely false"Officials changed immigrants' names at Ellis Island." Passenger lists were drawn up at the port of departure, and inspectors were not allowed to alter names unless the immigrant asked or the record was wrong. Name changes generally came later, by the immigrants' own choice or at naturalisation.20

To count people

Passports

Nearly abolished in the 19th century, brought back for a war, and never removed.

Before

Travel documents were letters of request. HM Passport Office describes the safe conduct as "little more than a note signed by the king or queen" asking that the bearer be allowed to pass.21 By the late 19th century much of Europe had stopped asking for passports at all.22

After

A standard booklet issued by the state of nationality, carrying a photograph and a machine-readable strip, is required at almost every border.

Safe conducts were mentioned in an Act of Parliament under Henry V in 1414, which is why that date appears in so many passport histories.21 One of the earliest surviving English passports is dated 18 June 1641 and signed by Charles I.21,23

The 19th century went the other way. Railways brought tourist crowds that swamped passport and visa checks, and in 1861 France abolished both.22 The sociologist John Torpey describes a 19th-century fall and a 20th-century rise in state control of movement.24 A 1922 International Labour Organisation report put the pre-war position simply: "Migration was generally speaking, unhindered."25

The First World War ended that. France, Germany and Italy made passports compulsory in 1914, and by the end of the war they were widespread.25 Photographs had at first been pasted into old passports with no rules about posing; in 1915 Britain issued its first passport designed to carry one, a single pink sheet folded into a card cover.26

The modern booklet was designed at a League of Nations conference in Paris that opened on 21 October 1920. Delegates agreed a booklet of 32 pages measuring 15.5 by 10.5 cm, with a card cover bearing the country's name and arms, and text in French plus at least one other language.27,28 Delegates treated controls as temporary. An international emigration conference in Rome in 1924 resolved that "the necessity of obtaining passports should be abolished as soon as possible", and a UN conference in 1963 finally concluded that worldwide abolition was not feasible.25

The same era produced the passport for people with no state. From 1922 the League's refugee commissioner, Fridtjof Nansen, issued identity certificates to stateless refugees, first from Russia and later from Armenia and elsewhere. Around 450,000 refugees used them, and 52 countries recognised them.28,29,30

The machine-readable passport came from the UN's civil aviation body, ICAO. Its first specification, Doc 9303, was published in 1980; biometric chips followed after the 11 September attacks, and the UK issued its first e-passport on 6 March 2006.21,31 The United States set 26 October 2006 as the deadline for visa-waiver countries to issue e-passports.32

Dates

  • 1414Safe conducts mentioned in an Act of Henry V21
  • 1641Early surviving English passport, signed by Charles I21
  • 1861France abolishes the passport and visa22
  • 1914Compulsory passports return with the war25
  • 1920League of Nations Paris conference sets the booklet27,28
  • 1922Nansen certificates for stateless refugees28,30
  • 1980ICAO's first machine-readable standard31
  • 2006UK's first biometric e-passport, 6 March21

Still with us

The two lines of 44 characters at the foot of the photo page are the machine-readable zone. The chevrons are filler: spaces in a name become "<", and two of them separate surname from given names.21,31,33 Thirty-two pages remains a common booklet size.21,27

Myth check

Overstated"Henry V invented the passport in 1414." The 1414 Act made breaking a safe conduct or truce a treason; it did not create the document, which already existed.21,34

To count people

ID numbers

America's national identifier began as a bookkeeping reference for a pension scheme.

Before

The United States had no national numbering of people. The Social Security Act of 1935 did not mention numbers at all; it only required records of workers' earnings.35

After

A nine-digit number is asked for by employers, banks, landlords and doctors, and is treated as proof of who you are.

The numbers were issued through the Post Office. Employers received forms from 16 November 1936 and workers from 24 November; about 45,000 post offices took part, 1,074 of them acting as typing centres.36 Roughly 30 million applications were processed by the end of June 1937.35 The Social Security Administration describes the number as identifying an earnings record, not the person holding the card.37

Government pushed it into wider use almost at once. Executive Order 9397, signed by Franklin Roosevelt in 1943, told federal agencies to use the number when they set up new systems for identifying individuals. Take-up was slow: the Civil Service Commission adopted it in 1961, the Internal Revenue Service in 1962 and the Defense Department in 1969.35,37 The Tax Reform Act of 1976 made it the taxpayer number for all purposes and let states use it for driving licences.35

By 1971 an internal Social Security task force acknowledged that "the number has ceased to be merely a 'social security number'". The Privacy Act of 1974 made it unlawful for government agencies to deny a benefit to someone who refused to give it, with exceptions; private businesses were not covered.37

Other countries built a national number on purpose. Sweden introduced its personnummer in 1947, made of the date of birth plus a birth number, and added a check digit in 1967.38

Dates

  • 1936First Social Security numbers issued via post offices36
  • 1943Executive Order 939735
  • 1946Cards print "Not for identification"39
  • 1947Sweden's personnummer38
  • 1972Legend dropped from cards39
  • 1976Tax Reform Act makes it the tax ID35
  • 2011Numbers randomised, 25 June40

Still with us

Until 25 June 2011 the first three digits showed the state where a number was issued. Randomisation removed that, partly to make numbers harder to reconstruct from public information.40 In 1938 a wallet maker printed a real employee's number on sample cards sold in Woolworth; according to the agency, more than 40,000 people went on to report it as their own.41

Myth check

Largely true"It was never meant to be an ID." From 1946 to 1972 the cards said "Not for identification".39 Yet a 1943 order was already telling federal agencies to use it as an identifier, so both things were official at once.35,37

To move things

Postcodes

Invented for sorters who did not know the map, then adopted by everyone who sells by area.

Before

Letters were sorted by clerks who had to carry the geography in their heads. In the United States, an average letter passed through the hands of eight to ten postal workers.42

After

A short code tells a machine where an item is going, and the same code tells insurers, retailers and statisticians a great deal about who lives there.

London came first. After the penny post swelled the volume of mail, Rowland Hill's plan divided the capital into ten compass districts in 1857 and 1858.43 Two did not survive. The NE district was folded into E in the 1860s on a report by a Post Office surveyor, the novelist Anthony Trollope, and S was split between SE and SW in 1868.43,44 Numbered subdistricts such as E1 and SW6 came during the First World War.43

War also drove the codes elsewhere. Germany's Reichspost introduced two-digit postal zones in 1941, when volumes were high, experienced staff had been called up and new place names kept appearing as territory was occupied.45 The United States brought in zone numbers in 1943 in 124 large cities, because experienced clerks had left for the forces.46 A Philadelphia postal inspector, Robert Moon, proposed a national three-digit code in 1944. Combined with the zones, it became the five-digit ZIP Code, launched on 1 July 1963 and promoted by a cartoon mascot, Mr ZIP.42,47

Britain's national scheme began as a trial in Norwich, where codes took effect on 8 October 1959 alongside new sorting machines.48 The present format was set in Croydon in 1966, and the whole country was coded by 1974.43,48

Reunified Germany had to start again. East and West had each built four-digit systems, leaving about 800 duplicates; 5300 was both Bonn and Weimar. New five-digit codes took effect on 1 July 1993.49,50 Ireland was one of the last developed countries to go without. Its Eircode launched in July 2015, giving every address a unique code partly because more than a third of Irish properties shared their address with another.51,52

The codes soon outgrew the post. A 2013 study for the US Postal Service inspector general listed census geography, insurance pricing, bank risk profiles and the checks done when you pay at a petrol pump among their uses.42

Dates

  • 1857London's ten compass districts43
  • 1941German two-digit postal zones45
  • 1943US zone numbers in 124 cities46
  • 1959Norwich postcode trial, 8 October48
  • 1963ZIP Code launched, 1 July42,47
  • 1974UK coding completed43
  • 1993Germany's five-digit codes, 1 July49,50
  • 2015Ireland's Eircode51

Still with us

London still has no NE or S district; those letters now belong to Newcastle and Sheffield.44 In 1897 Hackney businessmen petitioned to have NE restored, unhappy at being lumped in with the East End.44

Myth check

Half true"Robert Moon invented the ZIP Code." He proposed the first three digits in 1944. The last two came from the wartime city zones, and the scheme was put together under Postmaster General J. Edward Day.42

To move things

Which side of the road

There is no technical reason to prefer either side, which is why changing is so hard.

Before

Custom varied by place, by vehicle and by who was walking, riding or driving a team. The US Federal Highway Administration's history of the question finds no formal American rule before 1792.53

After

Every country has a single rule for all its roads. Treaties require that much, and say nothing about which side it should be.54,55

How a vehicle was driven seems to have mattered more than anything else. A teamster riding or walking beside a wagon team tended to sit on the left horse and keep right, so that he could judge the clearance of oncoming wagons. The big Conestoga wagons of 18th-century Pennsylvania were driven this way, and Pennsylvania's 1792 charter for the Philadelphia–Lancaster turnpike required traffic to keep right. New York made it the rule on all public highways in 1804.53 The Federal Highway Administration quotes the road historian Maxwell Lay's conclusion that there is no technical reason to prefer either side.53

Britain wrote the opposite into law. Section 78 of the Highway Act 1835 told drivers of wagons and carts meeting other traffic to keep "on the left or near side of the road", on pain of a fine.56

Changing sides is a national project. Swedes voted against switching to the right in a 1955 referendum, by about 83 per cent, but the Riksdag approved the change in 1963 anyway: neighbouring countries drove on the right, and most Swedish cars were already left-hand drive.57,58 On Sunday 3 September 1967, Dagen H, traffic stopped at 04:50, changed sides and moved off again at 05:00.57,58 Samoa went the other way at 6am on 7 September 2009, switching to the left so that Samoans could import cheaper used right-hand-drive cars from Australia and New Zealand, where many relatives lived.59,60

Today around 75 countries and territories drive on the left. Estimates of their share of the world's population range from about 30 to 35 per cent, depending on the source and year.61,62

Dates

  • 1792Pennsylvania turnpike charter: keep right53
  • 1835Highway Act: keep left56
  • 1949Geneva road traffic convention: one rule per country54
  • 1967Dagen H in Sweden, 3 September57
  • 1968Vienna convention repeats the one-rule principle55
  • 2009Samoa switches to the left59

Still with us

The 1835 clause, much amended, can still be read on the UK government's legislation website.56 The 1949 Geneva convention's wording, that traffic going the same way "shall keep to the same side of the road", is why no country lets drivers choose.54

Myth check

Unproven"Medieval riders kept left to keep their sword arm free." It is plausible and endlessly repeated, but there is little evidence for it, and most road users were carters and walkers, not knights.63 The claims that a pope ordered pilgrims to keep left, and that Napoleon single-handedly spread keep-right across Europe, are also disputed.63

To move things

Traffic lights

A railway idea brought to the street, where the first one exploded.

Before

Pedestrians in Victorian London "depended for their protection on the arm and gesticulations of a policeman", as The Times put it in 1868.64

After

An automatic signal tells every road user what to do, in colours borrowed from the railways, without a police officer at the junction.

The first road signal was the work of John Peake Knight, a railway manager, and was built by the railway signal makers Saxby & Farmer.65,66 It went up in December 1868 at the junction of Bridge Street and Great George Street, outside Parliament: a pillar with semaphore arms for daytime and red and green gas lamps for night, worked by a police constable.64,65 Within weeks there were complaints that the roadway smelt of gas. In January 1869 the lamp exploded and badly burned the constable's face, and the signal was removed.64,65

Electricity made the idea practical. On 5 August 1914 Cleveland, Ohio, tested James Hoge's system at Euclid Avenue and East 105th Street: red and green lamps on each corner marked "stop" and "move", switched by a policeman in a booth, with its wiring arranged so that conflicting signals could not show.67,68 In Detroit in 1920 a police officer, William Potts, built a four-way signal with a third, amber light for caution.69 Britain's first automatic, timer-controlled lights were tried in Wolverhampton in 1927.70

The colours came from the railways, where white once meant all clear. That had an obvious flaw. In 1891 the Railroad Gazette described a derailment in Michigan after a red signal glass broke and the lamp behind it showed white, and argued that white should be dropped.71 Railways moved to green for clear and yellow for caution, and road signals inherited the scheme.

Dates

  • 1868Gas signal outside Parliament, December64
  • 1869It explodes; constable burned64,65
  • 1914Cleveland electric signal, 5 August67
  • 1920Potts's three-colour signal, Detroit69
  • 1923Garrett Morgan's signal patent72
  • 1927Wolverhampton's automatic lights70

Still with us

The red–amber–green sequence goes back to Potts's 1920 signal, which is on display at the Henry Ford museum in Dearborn, Michigan.69

Myth check

False"Garrett Morgan invented the traffic light." Morgan patented a manually operated signal in 1923 with an all-way stop phase, which the US patent office calls a forerunner of the yellow light, and sold the rights to General Electric for $40,000. Electric signals were already working in Cleveland and Detroit.72,73

To sell and serve

Price tags

One price for everyone made shopping fairer, and made big shops possible.

Before

The price was whatever buyer and seller agreed. Different customers paid different amounts for the same goods, and shop clerks served long apprenticeships learning the stock and how to bargain.74

After

The price is printed on the item, the same for everyone, and the person at the till has no say in it.

The moral case came first. Early Quakers objected to charging different people different prices, and George Fox's journal for 1653–54 describes how their straight dealing won trade: if a child was sent to a Quaker shop, it was "as well used as if they had come themselves".74,75 Fox does not use the words "fixed price", and later writers who credit Quakers with inventing the practice tend to hedge.75,76

The commercial case came with scale. A store employing dozens of clerks could not let each one set prices. A. T. Stewart's "Marble Palace", opened in New York in 1846, kept to a single sale price for every customer throughout his career.77 R. H. Macy, founded in 1858, sold at fixed prices and for cash only.78 In Paris the Bon Marché under Aristide Boucicaut, from the 1850s, marked prices on goods, let people come in without obligation to buy and accepted returns; Britannica calls it widely considered the first department store.79,80,81 John Wanamaker put price tags on everything at his Philadelphia store, Oak Hall, which opened on 8 April 1861, on the principle that all customers should pay the same.81,82

The consequences went beyond fairness. Clerks needed to know the goods but no longer had to be skilled negotiators, so stores could hire many more of them, and prices could be advertised in newspapers and catalogues.74,79

Dates

  • 1650sFox records Quaker shops' honest dealing75
  • 1846Stewart's one-price store, New York77
  • 1852Boucicaut becomes co-owner of the Bon Marché79
  • 1858Macy's founded, fixed prices and cash only78
  • 1861Wanamaker's Oak Hall, Philadelphia82
  • 1958US law creates the car sticker price83

Still with us

Haggling survives where prices are high and stock varies, as in car showrooms; the US sticker price only dates from a 1958 law.83 Electronic shelf labels have revived fears of surge pricing in supermarkets, but a 2025 study of one US chain found "virtually no surge pricing" after it installed them.84

Myth check

Overstated"Wanamaker invented the price tag", or "Quakers did". Stewart had kept a one-price rule since 1823, according to New York's landmarks commission, and the Paris stores were as early as Wanamaker or earlier, and the Quaker story rests on a reputation for honest dealing more than on any record of who printed the first tag.75,77,79

To sell and serve

Plugs and sockets

Each country solved the same problem in its own decade, and nobody has been able to undo it.

Before

In early American homes the only outlet was often the light fitting. To use an iron or a toaster, you unscrewed the bulb and screwed in an adapter on the end of the appliance's cord.85

After

Wall sockets in every room take a detachable plug, but the plug that fits depends on which country you are in.

Harvey Hubbell of Bridgeport, Connecticut, was granted a patent for a "separable attachment-plug" on 8 November 1904. It was meant for "persons having no electrical knowledge or skill in the use of tools" who wanted to connect lights, fans and heaters. The base screwed into a light socket and stayed there, and the appliance's two-pronged cap pushed into it.85,86,87

Germany's answer came from Albert Büttner, whose patent for a plug with an earth connection was filed in January 1926. It sold badly at first, partly because the earthed cord cost about twice as much. A Siemens engineer, Wilhelm Klement, later moved the earth contacts to grooves on the sides, and from 1930 the utility RWE adopted that design: the Schuko, short for Schutzkontakt, protective contact.88

Britain's distinctive plug was designed for post-war housing. A wartime committee reported in 1944 that the pre-war range of sockets was "wholly unsatisfactory", and recommended a single 13-amp socket on a ring circuit. With materials short, a ring needed far less cable than separate circuits, but its large circuit fuse could not protect a thin appliance flex, so each plug carries its own fuse. Shutters over the live and neutral holes were included with children in mind. The system, BS 1363, arrived in 1947.89,90

Efforts to agree one plug have repeatedly failed. The International Electrotechnical Commission published a universal design, IEC 60906-1, in 1986.91 Europe's standards body abandoned its own harmonised plug in the mid-1990s after years of talks; a British industry spokesman remarked that a single European currency appeared easier to establish than a single European plug.92 Only a few countries have adopted the IEC design or a close variant. Brazil phased out its old mixture of plugs by 1 July 2011, using a version that is not fully compatible, and South Africa made its version, SANS 164-2, the preferred standard in 2013.91,93

Dates

  • 1904Hubbell's separable plug patent, 8 November86
  • 1926Büttner's earthed plug patent filed88
  • 1930RWE adopts the Schuko design88
  • 1947BS 1363 fused plug and ring main89
  • 1986IEC 60906-1 universal plug published91
  • 2011Brazil's changeover deadline, 1 July93

Still with us

About 15 plug types are in use, according to the International Electrotechnical Commission.94 Japan runs at 50 hertz in the east and 60 in the west, because in the 19th century Tokyo bought German generators and Osaka American ones.94,95 A 2017 EU review again advised against harmonising plugs, citing costs of around €100 billion.91

Myth check

Overstated"Hubbell invented the plug." British makers were already selling plugs that fitted lamp holders in the 1890s.90 Hubbell's contribution was the detachable design that American manufacturers went on to share.85,87

To sell and serve

Hotel keys

A Norwegian lock-maker's punched card meant every guest got a key no one else had.

Before

Guests were handed a metal key on a heavy tag, often stamped with the hotel's address and a promise to pay postage, so that a key carried off could be dropped in any mailbox.96,97 Rows of keys in cubbies behind the desk showed which guests were out.96

After

The key is recoded for each guest, stops working at checkout and leaves a record of every door it opens.

A metal key had two weaknesses. It could not be changed for each new guest, and staff carried keys to many rooms, exposing hotels to theft and to liability.96,98 That risk became vivid in 1976, when a federal jury awarded the singer Connie Francis $2.5 million against the Howard Johnson's motor lodge where she had been attacked in her room two years earlier.99

Tor Sørnes, an engineer at the Ving lock factory in Moss, Norway, designed a lock that read a punched plastic card and could be reprogrammed for each new guest. His son has told the BBC that the Francis case prompted the work, and that the first prototype was built in a garden shed.100 Norway's national encyclopaedia, Store norske leksikon, records the first installations in 1978, at the Park Hotell in Sandefjord and the Peachtree Plaza in Atlanta; the company dates the Atlanta installation to 1979.101,102 VingCard followed with a magnetic-stripe card lock in 1992–93, around the time Sørnes retired.101

Card locks changed hotel security in practice. A trade article in 1999 noted that electronic locks could log entries to help settle disputes, and that staff cards could be limited to particular rooms and hours.98 Phone keys followed: Starwood launched keyless entry through its app at ten hotels in late 2014.103

Dates

  • 1976Jury verdict in the Connie Francis case99
  • 1978First punched-card locks installed, Norway and Atlanta101
  • 1993Magnetic-stripe card lock from the same firm101
  • 2014Mobile room keys launched103

Still with us

Old keys still come home. In 2017 a key to room 1102 of San Francisco's Maurice Hotel arrived by post from Texas, its tag still promising "Drop in Any Mail Box – We Guarantee Postage", with $3.27 postage due.97

Myth check

Family account"The keycard was invented because of the Connie Francis case." The link comes from Sørnes's son, interviewed decades later. The court verdict is on record; the inventor's motive is family testimony.99,100

How this was made

The ten systems were chosen because each changed how ordinary people are found, moved, charged or let in, and because each has a well-documented "before". Entries draw on government and institutional sources where they exist (passport offices, postal services, standards bodies, courts, national archives), on academic work, and on established reference works and newspapers. Wikipedia is used only as secondary support for a few details, and is marked as such in the sources.

Where sources disagree, the entry says so: on the year of the first hotel card-lock installation, the date of the Philippine surname decree, and the share of the world's population that drives on the left. Popular origin stories that could not be confirmed are tested in each entry's myth check. Dates of first use are the earliest well-documented ones; earlier, less documented claims exist for several of these systems, including an electric traffic signal reportedly built in Salt Lake City in 1912.66
