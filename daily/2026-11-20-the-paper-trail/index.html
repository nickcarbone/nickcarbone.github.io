Casebook  Twelve exhibits

The Paper Trail

Twelve official stories, myths and accepted histories that gave way when an ordinary record surfaced, and how each chain of evidence was put together.

Published 20 November 2026 · 59 sources

Every story in this casebook was settled once. The popes held Rome and the West by an emperor’s gift. A Jewish officer sold secrets to Germany. A Sussex gravel pit had yielded an early human ancestor. Football fans caused the deaths at Hillsborough. The Post Office’s computers were sound, so branch shortfalls were the subpostmasters’ to make good. Each version had institutions behind it, and some had courts.

What brought them down was usually unglamorous: a sheet of squared paper whose lines changed colour halfway down, a cashier’s cheque in the wrong bank account, a stack of death certificates with no burial records to match, a fault log kept by a contractor. On its own, each record proved little. Placed beside the official account, it showed that the account could not be true.

The twelve exhibits below reconstruct how that happened. Each sets the story as it was told against the record as it was found, traces the chain of discovery, maps the people, institutions, places and documents involved, and separates what the records prove from what investigators inferred and what is still argued over. Food-origin myths, covered in earlier Daily pieces, are left aside, and so are the Cold War’s many reopened files.

How a record breaks a story

The same few moves recur across five centuries.

  • It could not exist yet

    A word, a paper additive or a chemical signature belongs to a later age than the object claims. Valla’s satraps and the fluorescent paper of Stern’s diaries fail the same test.

    See 01, 03, 06

  • Money leaves tracks

    Cash disappears, but cheques have dates, account numbers and the names of the people who bought them.

    See 04

  • Old registers, read again

    Death certificates, insurance maps and ship registries outlast the people who filed them. At Tuam the deaths were registered and the burials were not, and that gap was the evidence.

    See 09, 10, 12

  • The file was edited

    A forged letter pieced from two sheets; police statements revised before they reached the inquiry. Comparing versions exposes the hand.

    See 02, 08

  • The institution wrote it down

    Organisations record their own decisions and faults. Tuskegee’s correspondence and Fujitsu’s Known Error Log were evidence against the bodies that kept them.

    See 05, 11

  • The witnesses waited

    Testimony set aside for decades, from Derry residents to the last survivors of the slave trade, was finally weighed against the physical record.

    See 07, 12

Exhibit01

The Donation of Constantine

Rome, 8th–15th centuries

The record The document’s own Latin

Text analysis

The story

For six centuries, popes and canon lawyers could point to a decree in which the Emperor Constantine, cured of leprosy and baptised by Pope Sylvester I, handed the papacy primacy over the great sees, the Lateran palace, the imperial insignia and authority over Rome, Italy and the western provinces 1. One modern editor calls it “almost universally accepted as genuine from the ninth to the fifteenth century” 2.

The record

Lorenzo Valla needed no new archive. He read the decree as a fourth-century text and showed it could not be one. Its Latin was not the Latin of Constantine’s time 1. It gave Roman officials the Persian title of satrap, which Valla said no Roman council had ever used 34. And it spoke of Constantinople at a moment when, on the document’s own telling, the city was still Byzantium 4. Scholars now place its writing in the second half of the eighth century, probably in Rome 12.

Chain of discovery

  1. c. 750–800An unknown cleric composes the Constitutum Constantini; Paul I’s pontificate (757–767) is one editor’s most probable date 12.
  2. c. 1000Doubts are raised in the circle of the Emperor Otto III 1.
  3. 1049–54Leo IX becomes the first pope to cite the Donation in an official act; it passes into canon-law collections 1.
  4. 1440Valla, secretary to Alfonso of Aragon, then in conflict with Pope Eugenius IV, writes his Declamatio exposing the forgery. It circulates in manuscript 23.
  5. 1517Ulrich von Hutten prints Valla’s treatise for the first time 23.
  6. 1534An English translation is published for Thomas Cromwell, during the break with Rome 3.

Exhibit02

The Dreyfus affair

Paris, 1894–1906

The record A sheet of squared paper

Paper and inkInternal files

The story

Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer on the French General Staff, was said to have written the bordereau, an unsigned list of military documents offered to the German military attaché, Maximilian von Schwartzkoppen. A court-martial convicted him unanimously in December 1894, and he was publicly degraded in January 1895 6.

The record

In 1896 Lt Col Georges Picquart, newly in charge of army intelligence, obtained a petit bleu, a letter-card from Schwartzkoppen to Major Ferdinand Walsin-Esterhazy, recovered from the German embassy’s wastepaper 7. Esterhazy’s handwriting matched the bordereau 5. The case against Dreyfus had meanwhile been shored up by a letter naming him, produced by Major Hubert-Joseph Henry in October 1896 6. In August 1898 Captain Louis Cuignet examined it by lamplight and saw that the ruled squares were not the same colour at the top and bottom: the heading and signature came from a genuine letter of 1894, pieced into one dated 1896 5. Henry confessed on 30 August and died by suicide in his cell the next day 57.

Chain of discovery

  1. 26 Sep 1894French intelligence intercepts the bordereau 6.
  2. Dec 1894Dreyfus is convicted by court-martial 6.
  3. 1896The petit bleu addressed to Esterhazy is intercepted in March; accounts differ on when Picquart first saw it 67.
  4. 1896Comparing samples, Picquart finds Esterhazy’s hand matches the bordereau 5.
  5. 31 Oct 1896Henry produces the forged letter naming Dreyfus, later called the faux Henry 6.
  6. Jan 1898Esterhazy is acquitted by court-martial; Émile Zola publishes “J’accuse” 8.
  7. 13 Aug 1898Cuignet spots the mismatched lines on the forgery 6.
  8. 30–31 Aug 1898Henry confesses to the war minister, Godefroy Cavaignac, and dies in custody 57.
  9. Sep 1899A second court-martial at Rennes convicts him again, with extenuating circumstances; he is pardoned days later 6.
  10. 12 Jul 1906The Cour de cassation annuls the Rennes verdict and Dreyfus is rehabilitated 68.

Exhibit03

Piltdown Man

Sussex and London, 1912–2016

The record Fluorine levels in bone

Scientific testing

The story

In December 1912 Charles Dawson and Arthur Smith Woodward of the British Museum (Natural History) presented a skull and jaw from gravel at Piltdown, Sussex, to the Geological Society as a new early human, Eoanthropus dawsoni 1012. Leading anatomists backed it, and its standing helped push aside Raymond Dart’s Taung skull from South Africa in 1925 12.

The record

Kenneth Oakley’s fluorine test in 1949 showed the bones were far younger than claimed, around 50,000 years 1012. In 1953 Joseph Weiner grew suspicious of the teeth. Under the microscope the molars and canine proved to have been filed; an improved fluorine test showed that the jaw and skull were of different ages; the staining was artificial; and the jaw was an orangutan’s 1012. Weiner, Oakley and Wilfrid Le Gros Clark called it “a most elaborate and carefully prepared hoax” 12. In 2016 a team using DNA and CT scanning found one orangutan and at least two humans, possibly medieval, behind the finds, assembled with the same method throughout, and named Dawson the most likely forger 11.

Chain of discovery

  1. Dec 1912Dawson and Smith Woodward announce the find at the Geological Society 1012.
  2. 1925Piltdown’s standing helps sideline Dart’s Taung skull 12.
  3. 1949Oakley’s fluorine test dates the remains to about 50,000 years 1012.
  4. 20 Nov 1953Weiner, Oakley and Le Gros Clark publish “The solution of the Piltdown problem” in the museum’s Bulletin 1012.
  5. 1959Radiocarbon dating puts the skullcap at roughly 520–720 years old 12.
  6. Aug 2016De Groote, Stringer and colleagues publish DNA and CT evidence pointing to a single forger, most likely Dawson 11.

Exhibit04

The Watergate money trail

Washington and Miami, 1972–74

The record A $25,000 cashier’s cheque

Financial records

The story

Five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee’s offices at the Watergate on 17 June 1972 16. In the first weeks there was nothing on the public record tying their cash to President Nixon’s re-election campaign.

The record

The money had left a paper trail. On 10 April 1972 Kenneth Dahlberg, a Midwest fundraiser for the campaign, obtained a $25,000 cashier’s cheque and handed it to the finance chairman, Maurice Stans. That cheque, and four cheques from a Mexico City bank totalling $89,000 payable to a lawyer, Manuel Ogarrio, were deposited on 20 April in a trust account of a company run by Bernard Barker, one of the burglars, at Republic National Bank of Miami 13. On 1 August the Washington Post reported the $25,000 cheque, drawing on an investigator for the Miami prosecutor 15; Dahlberg confirmed it was his 17. The government’s auditors later traced the trail in full 13.

Chain of discovery

  1. 10–11 Apr 1972Dahlberg’s $25,000 cashier’s cheque goes to Stans 13.
  2. 20 Apr 1972The Dahlberg cheque and $89,000 in Mexican cheques are deposited in Barker’s company account in Miami 13.
  3. 17 Jun 1972Five men are arrested at the DNC offices 16.
  4. 23 Jun 1972On tape, H. R. Haldeman tells Nixon the money is traceable; Nixon approves having the CIA ask the FBI to hold back 14.
  5. 1 Aug 1972Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein report the cheque in the Washington Post 15.
  6. 26 Aug 1972The General Accounting Office refers apparent violations by the campaign’s finance committee to the Attorney General 13.
  7. 5–8 Aug 1974The 23 June tape is released; Nixon announces his resignation 16.

Exhibit05

The Tuskegee syphilis study

Macon County, Alabama, 1932–72

The record The study’s own correspondence

Internal filesTestimony

The story

From 1932 the US Public Health Service, working with the Tuskegee Institute, followed 600 Black men in Macon County, Alabama: 399 with syphilis and 201 without. The men were told they were being treated for “bad blood”, and their informed consent was not collected 18.

The record

The government’s own files showed what the study was. Penicillin had become the treatment of choice by 1943, the CDC says 18, yet treatment was withheld. Peter Buxtun, a Public Health Service venereal-disease investigator in San Francisco, raised objections from 1966 19; in November 1968 he wrote again of “grave moral doubts” 21. In 1969 a CDC committee decided to continue the study 20. In July 1972 Buxtun gave documents to the Associated Press, where Jean Heller wrote the story that ran on 25 July 19 and on the front page of the New York Times the next day 20.

Chain of discovery

  1. 1932The study begins with 600 men 18.
  2. 1943Penicillin is the treatment of choice, according to the CDC 18.
  3. 1966–68Buxtun raises objections with CDC officials, writing again in November 1968 1921.
  4. 1969A CDC committee decides to continue the study 20.
  5. 25 Jul 1972AP publishes Jean Heller’s story built on Buxtun’s documents 19.
  6. Oct 1972A federal advisory panel advises stopping the study; its end is announced a month later 18.
  7. 1974A class action ends in a $10 million settlement 18.
  8. 16 May 1997President Clinton apologises on behalf of the government 18.

Exhibit06

The Hitler Diaries

Hamburg and Koblenz, 1983

The record Fluorescent paper under ultraviolet light

Paper and inkScientific testing

The story

In April 1983 the German magazine Stern announced about 60 handwritten volumes of Adolf Hitler’s diaries, obtained by its reporter Gerd Heidemann, for which it had paid more than nine million marks 2224. The historian Hugh Trevor-Roper vouched for them, and the Sunday Times bought serial rights 24.

The record

The paper gave them away. Tests by the Bundesarchiv, the federal materials-testing institute (BAM) and the federal police found a fluorescent additive in the paper that was not in use before 1954, post-war materials in the threads attached to the seals, and ink that had been applied recently 23. The Bundesarchiv declared them forgeries on 6 May 1983, less than a fortnight after publication 2223. The forger, Konrad Kujau, and Heidemann were convicted in July 1985 22.

Chain of discovery

  1. 25 Apr 1983Stern publishes its first issue on the diaries and holds a press conference 22.
  2. Early May 1983Laboratory tests find post-war paper additives and threads 23.
  3. 6 May 1983The Bundesarchiv announces the diaries are forged 2223.
  4. 21 Aug 1984The trial opens in Hamburg 22.
  5. Jul 1985Kujau is sentenced to four years and six months, Heidemann to four years and eight months 22.

Exhibit07

Bloody Sunday

Derry, 1972–2010

The record Thousands of witness statements

Testimony

The story

On 30 January 1972 British soldiers shot dead 13 people at a civil-rights march in Derry, and another died later 29. Lord Widgery’s tribunal reported in April 1972 that there was “no reason to suppose that the soldiers would have opened fire if they had not been fired upon first”, and recorded “a strong suspicion” that some others had been firing weapons or handling bombs, while finding none of the dead or wounded proved to have been shot while handling a firearm or bomb 26.

The record

The Saville Inquiry, set up in 1998, took evidence from 921 witnesses and considered about 1,500 further statements 27. Its report of 15 June 2010 found that the first shot near the march was, on balance, fired by the army; that none of the casualties posed a threat of death or serious injury; that no warning was given; and that soldiers knowingly put forward false accounts to justify their firing. The prime minister called the killings “unjustified and unjustifiable” 25.

Chain of discovery

  1. 30 Jan 1972Soldiers of the Parachute Regiment open fire in the Bogside 29.
  2. Apr 1972The Widgery report backs the soldiers’ account that they were fired on first 26.
  3. 1998The Saville Inquiry is established 27.
  4. 2000–04Hearings: 921 witnesses give evidence 27.
  5. 15 Jun 2010The Saville report is published; David Cameron apologises 25.
  6. 23 Oct 2025At Belfast Crown Court, the former paratrooper known as Soldier F is acquitted of two murders and five attempted murders 29.

Exhibit08

Hillsborough

Sheffield, 1989–2016

The record Amended police statements

Internal files

The story

Ninety-six Liverpool supporters died after a crush at Hillsborough on 15 April 1989. Four days later the Sun’s front page, headlined “The Truth”, alleged that fans had robbed the dead, urinated on police and attacked officers trying to help, citing unnamed police and others 33. Lord Justice Taylor’s interim report that August found the main reason was the failure of police control 32, but the original inquests returned accidental death, with evidence limited by a 3.15pm cut-off 30.

The record

The Hillsborough Independent Panel read over 450,000 pages of documents. It found that 164 police statements had been significantly amended, 116 of them to remove or alter comments unfavourable to the police; that police had run national computer checks on the dead in an attempt to impugn their reputations; that there was no evidence of exceptional drunkenness, ticketlessness or violence; and that the 3.15pm cut-off was flawed 30. The Sun’s former editor and its then editor apologised that day 33.

Chain of discovery

  1. 19 Apr 1989The Sun publishes “The Truth” 33.
  2. 4 Aug 1989Taylor’s interim report blames failures of police control 32.
  3. 12 Sep 2012The Independent Panel reports; the prime minister apologises in the Commons 30.
  4. 19 Dec 2012The High Court quashes the original inquest verdicts and orders new inquests 31.
  5. 26 Apr 2016The new inquests’ jury concludes the 96 were unlawfully killed and that fans’ behaviour did not contribute 35.
  6. 28 Jul 2021A coroner rules that Andrew Devine, who died the day before, was unlawfully killed: the 97th victim 34.

Exhibit09

Richard III under the car park

Leicester, 1485–2015

The record Old maps and a maternal bloodline

Registers and mapsScientific testing

The story

For centuries the story went that Richard III’s body was dug up after the dissolution of the friaries in 1538 and thrown into the River Soar 40. Shakespeare had given him a hunchback.

The record

Maps put the friary back on the ground. John Speed’s 1610 map had labelled the wrong friary; William Stukeley placed Grey Friars correctly in 1724, Thomas Roberts showed the precinct in 1741, and Goad insurance plans traced the land becoming car parks between the 1920s and 1930s 38. Philippa Langley, who began her search for the grave in 2004 and later led the Looking for Richard project, worked with John Ashdown-Hill, who had traced an unbroken female line from Richard’s sister to a living descendant, Michael Ibsen 40. Digging began in a council car park on 25 August 2012 40. In February 2013 the University of Leicester announced a mitochondrial DNA match with Ibsen and a second maternal-line relative, alongside radiocarbon dates, battle wounds and a curved spine 36.

Chain of discovery

  1. 1485Richard is buried in the choir of the Grey Friars church 36.
  2. 1610–1944Maps by Speed, Stukeley and Roberts, then Goad plans, fix and follow the site 38.
  3. 2004Langley begins the search that becomes the Looking for Richard project 40.
  4. 25 Aug 2012University of Leicester archaeologists dig in the car park; leg bones are found on day one 40.
  5. 4 Feb 2013The university announces the remains are Richard’s 36.
  6. May 2014The Lancet describes severe scoliosis, a balanced curve and no limp 39.
  7. Dec 2014Nature Communications publishes the DNA identification 3741.
  8. 26 Mar 2015Richard is reburied in Leicester Cathedral 42.

Exhibit10

The Tuam children’s burials

Co. Galway, 1925–present

The record Death certificates with no burial records

Registers and maps

The story

St Mary’s mother and baby home in Tuam was run by the Bon Secours Sisters on behalf of Galway County Council from 1925 to 1961 47. There was no public record of where the children who died there were buried. Locally the site was spoken of as a children’s burial ground; in 1975 two boys lifted a slab there and saw bones 43.

The record

The local historian Catherine Corless bought the children’s death certificates from the civil register, paying €3,184 in all 43. They recorded about 796 deaths, with no matching burial records. Old Ordnance Survey maps showed a sewage tank on the site 43. In March 2017 the state Commission of Investigation said test excavations had found “significant quantities of human remains” in at least 17 of 20 underground chambers, with ages at death from about 35 foetal weeks to two or three years 44. Its final report in 2021 put the deaths at the home itself at 802 and concluded that many children were likely buried on the site 45.

Chain of discovery

  1. 1925–61The home operates 47.
  2. c. 2011–14Corless obtains death certificates and finds no burial records to match 43.
  3. Jun 2014Her findings become international news; the Irish government announces an inquiry, later established as a commission of investigation 43.
  4. 3 Mar 2017The commission reports significant quantities of human remains in chambers on the site 44.
  5. 12 Jan 2021The final report records 802 deaths at Tuam and finds burial records for very few 45.
  6. Jul 2022The Institutional Burials Act passes, allowing excavation 46.
  7. 14 Jul 2025Full excavation begins 47.
  8. Jul 2026As of July 2026, AP reported 99 sets of remains recovered, seven believed to date from an earlier workhouse, and DNA samples taken from 62 relatives 48.

Exhibit11

The Post Office Horizon scandal

England, Wales and Northern Ireland, 1999–2024

The record The Known Error Log

Internal filesCourt records

The story

The Post Office consistently asserted that Horizon, the branch accounting system supplied by Fujitsu, was robust and reliable 50, and treated unexplained shortfalls as the subpostmasters’ to repay. It has identified 700 convictions in its own prosecutions between 1999 and 2015 in which Horizon evidence may have featured, and a further 283 cases brought by other prosecutors 52.

The record

In group litigation led by Alan Bates, the High Court read Fujitsu’s own fault records. Its Horizon Issues judgment of December 2019 found that bugs, errors and defects could cause shortfalls in branch accounts and had done so “on numerous occasions”, that the original Horizon was “not remotely robust”, and that Fujitsu could insert, edit or delete transaction data without a subpostmaster’s knowledge. The Post Office had argued the Known Error Log was not a record of bugs; the judge found it and related fault reports “a very good means of getting at the truth” 49. In 2021 the Court of Appeal quashed 39 convictions, calling the prosecutions “an affront to the conscience of the court” 50.

Chain of discovery

  1. 1999–2015Post Office prosecutions in which Horizon evidence may have featured 52.
  2. 12 Dec 2019The Bates claimants settle for £57.75m, with no admission of liability 53.
  3. 16 Dec 2019The Horizon Issues judgment finds bugs, remote access and an unreliable system 49.
  4. 23 Apr 2021Hamilton v Post Office: 39 convictions quashed 50.
  5. 24 May 2024The Post Office (Horizon System) Offences Act quashes qualifying convictions in England, Wales and Northern Ireland 51.
  6. 8 Jul 2025The public inquiry publishes the first volume of its final report 54.

The Clotilda

Mobile, Alabama, 1860–2019

The record A ship’s certificate of registry

Registers and mapsTestimony

The story

Importing enslaved people into the United States had been illegal since 1808. The last known slave ship to do it, the schooner Clotilda, was burned and sunk after its voyage in 1860, and was never found 5556. Without the ship, National Geographic reported, “deniers contended that the shameful episode never occurred” 57.

The record

The voyage itself had always had witnesses. The captain, William Foster, wrote his own account in 1890 58. Zora Neale Hurston interviewed one of the survivors, Cudjo Lewis (Oluale Kossola), in Africatown in 1927 and 1928; her book Barracoon was published only in 2018 59. The ship’s certificate of registry recorded its size: about 86 feet long with a 23-foot beam 56. In 2019 the Alabama Historical Commission and the maritime archaeology firm SEARCH announced that a burned wreck in the Mobile River matched the documented dimensions and construction of a schooner built before 1870; of the vessels assessed from the records, it was the only one that fitted 55.
