Sixteen Rooms the World Drinks In

A field guide to sixteen drinking institutions, from the English pub to the South African shebeen: where each came from, how its room works, what it did for the people who used it, and what is left of it now.

Sixteen institutions, 103 sources

In 1794 about two dozen friendly societies in Blackburn were based at inns1. Before the American Revolution, the Sons of Liberty met in a Boston tavern2. In Soweto under apartheid, the illegal front-room bar was one of the few places people could talk politics3. Almost every drinking culture has built a room for it, and most of those rooms ended up doing work nobody licensed them to do: meeting hall, labour exchange, newsroom, court.

The sociologist Ray Oldenburg called such places “third places”, distinct from home and work, where rank is set aside and conversation is the main event4. Jürgen Habermas traced part of Europe's democratic public sphere to its cafés and salons5. In colonial Massachusetts, David Conroy found taverns hosting courts and public business6.

Governments noticed early, and the room took its shape from what they allowed. A Bavarian decree of 1812 let brewers sell beer but not food, so Munich brings its own picnic to the beer garden7. An emperor's decree of 1784 lets a Viennese winegrower open his courtyard when he hangs out a pine bough8. Six o'clock closing gave Australia the swill and the tiled bar9. Rules about who could enter left men-only bars in Britain until 1976, cantinas closed to women in Mexico City until 1981 and the whole legal liquor trade closed to Black South Africans until 196210,11,12.

This guide takes sixteen of these institutions in turn. Each has a plan of a typical room (schematic, and not any particular building), the detail that tells you where you are, where the institution came from, what it did for its town, and how it is doing. The status label on each is the desk's judgement from the figures cited.

England and Wales

The English pub

public house

Where it came from

The pub has three ancestors. The inn gave food and beds to travellers who could pay for them, the tavern sold wine and meals to the better-off, and the alehouse sold ale and plain food to everyone else13,14. The Beerhouse Act of 1830 blew the system open: any ratepayer could buy a two-guinea licence and sell beer from his front room, with no magistrate to approve it, and thousands of new beerhouses opened15,16.

The room

The Victorian pub was a building about class. The public bar was the plainest and cheapest room. The saloon, smoke room, lounge and private bar charged a little more for the same beer and had bell-pushes for table service, and a jug-and-bottle hatch sold drink to take home. That arrangement of several rooms around one serving counter held until the 1960s, when most partitions came down10. Men-only rooms became unlawful in 1976 under the Sex Discrimination Act10.

What it did for the town

Before there were union halls, there were pubs. In Blackburn about two dozen friendly societies were based at inns in 1794, weavers met at the Pig and Whistle in 1800, and strike committees sat in pubs in 1847 and again in 1869–701. The British Library's map of Chartist meetings reported in the Northern Star between 1837 and 1848 is crowded with inns17. The state took notice in wartime: the No Treating Order of October 1915 made it an offence to buy someone else a drink, and from 1916 the government ran the pubs and breweries of Carlisle itself18.

Now

The Licensing Act 2003 ended fixed closing times in England and Wales from 24 November 200519. The numbers have gone the other way. British Beer and Pub Association data put the UK at 44,650 pubs at the end of 2025, against 60,800 in 200020.

Ireland

The Irish pub

teach tábhairne; the pub-grocery

Where it came from

In the 1830s and 1840s grocers in Ireland were barred from holding spirit licences, so customers drank behind screens at the back of the shop. A licensing bill in 1838 let grocers take out licences, and grocers and publicans merged their trade bodies in 186223. The result was the pub-grocery, where tea, rice and snuff were sold across one counter and porter across the other.

The room

Kehoe's on South Anne Street in Dublin, first licensed in 1803 by its own account and refitted late in the 19th century, still has the drawers that held the groceries, and a snug24: a small enclosed booth where customers could drink out of sight of the main room.

What it did for the town

In a country town the pub was also the shop, the place to collect news and the place to settle a deal. That double role is why closures land hardest in rural counties: publican licences fell from 8,617 in 2005 to 6,498 in 2024, roughly one in four, according to a report commissioned by the Drinks Industry Group of Ireland25,26.

Now

As the pubs at home thinned out, the idea was exported. The Irish Pub Company, founded in 1990, designed and built pubs abroad in partnership with Guinness, and by 2007 the Associated Press reported more than 1,000 of them in 40 countries27,28.

Bavaria, Germany

The Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

Bierhalle, Biergarten, Stammtisch

Where it came from

Duke Wilhelm V founded the Hofbräuhaus as a court brewery on 27 September 1589; Munich's ordinary drinkers were let in only in 1828, under King Ludwig I31,32. The beer garden came from a compromise. Brewers kept their beer cool in cellars dug into the banks of the Isar, spread gravel over them and planted shallow-rooted chestnut trees for shade. A royal decree of 1812 under Max I Joseph allowed them to sell beer on the spot from June to September, but not food, so that innkeepers would not lose their trade. Drinkers brought their own7,33.

The room

The beer hall is the beer garden brought indoors: long shared tables, strangers side by side, and somewhere a Stammtisch, the regulars' table, often marked with an ornate sign and historically a place for men to argue about politics34. The rebuilt Hofbräuhaus of 1896–97 put a vast ground-floor Schwemme under a festival hall31.

What it did for the town

The rooms were big enough for mass politics, with consequences that still shadow them. The Nazi party's 25-point programme was presented to about 2,000 people at the Hofbräuhaus on 24 February 192035. On 8 November 1923 Hitler fired a shot into the ceiling of the Bürgerbräukeller to begin the failed coup now called the Beer Hall Putsch; 20 people died in the march the next morning36.

Now

When noise rulings threatened Munich's gardens, about 20,000 people protested in May 1995. Bavaria responded with the Biergartenverordnung of 20 April 1999, which eases noise rules for gardens; its stated rationale includes the right to eat your own food there free of charge7,37,33.

Czech Republic

The Czech hospoda

hospoda, hostinec, štamgast

Where it came from

Prague's U Fleků has records going back to 1499, when a maltster named Vít Skřemenec bought the house; the brewery says it has brewed continuously for more than 500 years38. That claim is the venue's own, but the pattern it represents is old: a house that brews, or a house that pours a nearby brewery's beer, with a room full of benches attached.

The room

The hospoda runs on table service and trust. A waiter brings beer, marks it on a small slip of paper left on the table, and keeps bringing it as your glass empties39,40,41. Many pubs keep a table or two for štamgasti, the regulars, and a newcomer who sits there will be moved39. Nowadays, Expats.cz notes, many waiters ask before refilling39.

What it did for the town

The pub was where Czech literary life drank. Bohumil Hrabal was a famous regular at U Zlatého tygra, which first poured beer in 1843, and on 11 January 1994 he sat there with President Václav Havel and the visiting Bill Clinton42,43.

Now

Czechs drink less beer than they did. Consumption peaked at 163.5 litres a head in 200544; consumption of alcoholic beer was reported at 126 litres a head in 2024, using Czech Beer and Malt Association data45.

Belgium

The Belgian café

café, estaminet, bruin café

Where it came from

A Belgian café is a bar that treats glassware as part of the drink. Each beer has its own branded glass, serving it in the wrong one is a small social offence, and specialist cafés list hundreds of beers46. UNESCO added beer culture in Belgium to its Representative List of intangible heritage in 2016, noting almost 1,500 types of beer47.

The room

The classic room is a single long space with a counter, a wall of glasses and small tables. À la Mort Subite in Brussels has occupied a 1902 building's ground floor, converted to a café in 1910, since 1927, taking its name from a dice game played at its predecessor, La Cour Royale48,49.

What it did for the town

The Vandervelde law, in force from 1919 until 1984, banned the sale of spirits in drinking establishments. The usual story says it pushed Belgians towards strong beer. Beer historians dispute that: average strength barely moved between 1900 and 1930, and strong styles stayed rare until after 195050.

Now

Vossen descendants, the family behind the name, still run Mort Subite49.

The Netherlands

The Dutch brown café and tasting room

bruin café, proeflokaal

Where it came from

The brown café is named for its colour, and people disagree about the cause: dark wood panelling, according to some, or centuries of tobacco smoke, according to a barman quoted by the Guardian51,52. Café Hoppe on the Spui says it has been open since 1670; its present form dates from the 1930s, when two buildings were joined53.

The room

The proeflokaal, or tasting room, was where a distiller let customers try jenever before buying. At Wynand Fockink in Amsterdam, whose tasting room dates its premises to 1679, the house tradition is to bow before the first sip54. The glass is a tulip filled to the brim, so the first sip is taken bent from the waist, hands behind the back; a kopstootje, “little head-butt”, is that jenever with a beer alongside55.

What it did for the town

The brown café is small and crowded at the counter; at Hoppe, one of its two buildings is given over to the standing bar53.

Now

The Guardian has reported on campaigns to save Amsterdam's old bars from rising rents and changing tastes52.

Vienna and Lower Austria

The Viennese Heuriger

Heuriger, Buschenschank

Where it came from

On 17 August 1784 Emperor Joseph II issued a decree allowing winegrowers to sell their own wine and must from their own premises8. Accounts differ on food: some say cold food was allowed from the start, others that it was banned at first56. Heuriger means this year's wine, and the word became the name for the place that pours it.

The room

A Heuriger is a winegrower's courtyard or parlour with long tables, a self-service buffet and wine poured by the quarter-litre. A bough, usually pine, hung over the entrance shows it is ausg'steckt, open, which happens for a few weeks at a time, several times a year8,57.

What it did for the town

The Heuriger keeps the wine trade inside the city's own vineyards, on the edge of Vienna in Grinzing, Nussdorf, Neustift, Stammersdorf and Mauer57.

Now

Viennese Heuriger culture was added to Austria's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage, kept by the Austrian UNESCO commission, in October 201958.

Spain

The Spanish taberna and bar

taberna, bodega, bar

Where it came from

Spain's oldest bars are wine shops that never stopped. El Rinconcillo in Seville gives 1670 as its founding date, which is the venue's own claim; the de Rueda family has owned it since 186059. Casa Alberto in Madrid, founded in 1827 on the site of the house where Cervantes wrote the second part of Don Quixote, still makes its own vermouth60.

The room

The defining piece of furniture is the counter, where much of the eating and drinking is done standing. The tables come second.

What it did for the town

The neighbourhood bar is where a Spanish street meets. A study reported by Diario de León called bars one of the last bulwarks against rural depopulation61. The scale is large: Spain had 163,890 drink-serving establishments in 2024, according to INE business-register data compiled by the state food company Mercasa, roughly one for every 300 people62.

Now

Madrid's city council publishes a map of its centenary taverns and promotes them as heritage60.

France

The French café and bistrot

café, bistrot, le zinc

Where it came from

Le Procope in Paris was founded in 1686 by Procopio Cutò and became a meeting place for writers and, during the Revolution, for Danton, Marat and Robespierre. Its claim to be Paris's oldest café is disputed: the original café closed in 1872 and the restaurant reopened in 195763.

The room

The counter, called the zinc whether it is zinc, brass, Formica or steel, is the bistrot's emblem64. The room has a price structure built in. Since a 1987 order, cafés must post outside the prices of their most common drinks at the counter and in the room65.

What it did for the town

Jürgen Habermas built part of his account of the modern public sphere on the coffee houses, salons and literary circles of 18th-century Europe5. France's culture ministry, in its heritage inventory, treats the social life of bistrots and cafés as heritage in its own right64.

Now

France had about 200,000 cafés in the 1960s and 36,900 in 2010, according to trade figures cited in the National Assembly66; the culture ministry's own sheet says fewer than 40,000 now64. “Social and cultural practices in bistrots and cafés in France” joined the national intangible heritage inventory in 2024, and a campaign for UNESCO listing was still running in 202667,68.

Japan

The Japanese izakaya

居酒屋 izakaya, akachōchin, tachinomi

Where it came from

The name says what it was: i, to stay, and sakaya, sake shop69; Tokyo's tourism bureau glosses the three characters as “stay-drink-place”70. In Edo, shops that sold sake by measure began letting customers drink on the spot, standing at first, then sitting on barrels, with simple snacks added71,69. The economic historian Penelope Francks reads their spread as a sign that sake had become an everyday consumer good by the late 1700s69.

The room

An izakaya is a counter facing the kitchen and a few tables, sometimes a raised tatami area. A small dish arrives unasked when you sit down, the otōshi, billed as a seating charge of around 200 to 1,000 yen a head70,69. The red lantern outside, the akachōchin, has become shorthand for the small independent izakaya; the tachinomi is the standing-only version69.

What it did for the town

Tokyo's tourism bureau describes izakaya as places where people come to unwind with co-workers and friends70: the after-work room of Japanese office life.

Now

The small independent red-lantern bar and the standing-only tachinomi both remain part of the form69.

Japan

The Japanese beer hall and rooftop beer garden

ビヤホール, 屋上ビアガーデン

Where it came from

Japan's surviving beer hall was built as a showroom for draught beer. Beer Hall Lion Ginza 7-chome opened on 8 April 1934 as the direct outlet of Dainippon Beer, in a building by the architect Sugawara Eizō72,73. It is widely described as the oldest beer hall still operating in Japan, a claim that comes mainly from its operator and is repeated by the press74.

The room

The hall is one great room with a bar at the end under a wall of glass mosaic, made with the glass artist Ōkura Yoshizō72. The building was registered as a national Tangible Cultural Property with effect from 17 February 202275.

What it did for the town

The rooftop beer garden came from Osaka in 1953, on top of the Osaka Daiichi Seimei building76,77. It turned flat city roofs into a summer meeting ground for office workers; the gardens open after the rainy season and close with the end of summer77.

Now

Rooftop gardens remain a summer ritual on department stores and hotels; Ginza Lion is still pouring77,75.

South Korea

The Korean tent bar and hof

포장마차 pojangmacha, 호프 hof

Where it came from

Pojangmacha means covered wagon. In the 1950s mobile carts settled into fixed stalls under poles and cloth, later metal and tarpaulin, selling food with soju, makgeolli and beer to a city full of war refugees78. The hof, from the German word, is the draught-beer bar; Eulji OB Bear in Seoul's Euljiro opened in 198079.

The room

A tent, plastic stools, a cooking cart at one end. Inside, the hof's standard snack is dried pollack: Korea Times reports that Eulji OB Bear began serving it in 1981 after suppliers were barred from providing free snacks, and the habit spread along the alley79.

What it did for the town

Street vending was illegal, so the tents lived through removal drives. Before the 1988 Seoul Olympics they were cleared from main roads and reappeared on side streets; they came back in numbers after the late-1990s financial crisis80. From January 2019 Seoul ran a permit system for street stalls, with fees and rules on space and safety; the city counted 6,669 stalls, of which 1,883 qualified81.

Now

Eulji OB Bear was evicted by court order on 21 April 2022 and reopened near its old site in 202479,82.

United States

The American tavern and saloon

tavern, saloon, neighbourhood bar

Where it came from

Boston's first tavern, Cole's, opened in 1634, and colonial courts generally required each town to have a public house14. In Massachusetts, taverns hosted courts and public business and became, in the historian David Conroy's account, a forum where a republican citizenry formed6. The Sons of Liberty met in the Green Dragon in Boston2, and on 4 December 1783 George Washington said goodbye to his officers in the Long Room of Fraunces Tavern in New York83.

The room

The 19th-century saloon was a long bar, a rail and a free lunch. In Chicago's 17th Ward in 1900, 111 of 163 saloons offered one. The sociologist Royal Melendy called the saloon the workingman's club: it named itself after trades, hosted unions and doubled as an employment office84. Jacob Riis called it the poor man's club and said the rumshop turned the political crank in New York85.

What it did for the town

That political power made the saloon the target. The Anti-Saloon League, founded at Oberlin, Ohio, in 1893, campaigned against it by name86. The 18th Amendment brought national Prohibition from 17 January 1920 until the 21st Amendment was ratified on 5 December 193387,88.

Now

The sociologist Ray Oldenburg's The Great Good Place (1989) gave the neighbourhood bar a name: a “third place” after home and work, where rank is set aside and conversation is the point4. McSorley's Old Ale House, which gives 1854 as its founding date (the building is thought to date from about 1865), served women only from 10 August 1970, after a federal court case and a city law89,90.

Mexico

The Mexican cantina and pulquería

cantina, pulquería

Where it came from

Pulque, the fermented sap of the maguey, has been drunk for at least 2,300 years. In 1672 colonial Mexico City banned mobile pulque stands and licensed 36 fixed outlets, the first pulquerías; there were about 500 by 1854 and 1,691 by 190991.

The room

The cantina was historically a male room: early cantinas posted signs barring children, dogs and women11. Mexico City changed the rule in 1981, according to the city's press and a columnist who recalls the regent Carlos Hank González lifting it11,92, though some cantinas kept the old house rule longer. La Ópera, which moved to Cinco de Mayo in 1895, has a hole in its ceiling said to be from Pancho Villa's pistol, a story even its manager attributes only to “some newspapers and books”93,11.

What it did for the town

The pulquería was the working-class neighbourhood's meeting room, and it was pushed out deliberately: by shorter hours, higher taxes, a smear campaign and the promotion of beer as modern and hygienic. New licences were suspended in 1954, and by 2012 only 72 pulquerías were left in the city91. Salón Tenampa on Plaza Garibaldi, open since about 1925, became the home of mariachi94.

Now

Pulque has had a revival among younger drinkers in the capital, and the surviving pulquerías are studied as heritage91.

Australia

The Australian pub

the hotel, the front bar

Where it came from

Australian pubs are called hotels because, as the Encyclopedia of Melbourne puts it, licensing law from the 1830s required licensed premises to offer accommodation to the public9. In most states the First World War brought six o'clock closing: South Australia from 1916 until 1967, New South Wales from 1916 until 1955, Victoria from 1916 until 1966, Tasmania from 1916 until 193795,96.

The room

Closing at six produced the “six o'clock swill”: a rush of men after work, drinking as fast as they could before the doors shut, in bars tiled so they could be hosed down afterwards9. In Queensland, women were barred from public bars by law. On 31 March 1965 Merle Thornton and Rosalie Bogner chained themselves to the foot rail of the public bar at the Regatta Hotel in Brisbane; Queensland repealed the provision barring women from public bars in 197097,98.

What it did for the town

Because the law made every pub a hotel, the pub was also where travellers stayed9; and because the front bar was where the town's men gathered, being kept out of it mattered.

Now

Ten o'clock closing came to New South Wales in 1955 and Victoria in 196696,9. The hotels survived the swill and the protests.

South Africa

The South African shebeen

shebeen, from Irish síbín

Where it came from

The word comes from Irish síbín, illicit whiskey100. In South Africa the Liquor Act passed in June 1927 (some historians refer to it as the 1928 Act) barred Africans from holding liquor licences or entering licensed premises3,12,101. Drinking moved into private houses.

The room

A shebeen is a front room, a kitchen and a yard. The women who ran them, the shebeen queens, brewed and sold at home and could earn more than men in factory jobs; police raids brought fines and jail3.

What it did for the town

Because it was illegal anyway, the shebeen was one of the few places where township residents could meet beyond the state's eye, and it served as a gathering place for political activists3,12. Africans were allowed to buy liquor legally from 15 August 196212,101.

Now

After apartheid, licensing came under the national Liquor Act of 2003 and provincial laws102. In 2005 the Western Cape estimated 20,000 to 30,000 shebeens in the province against about 5,700 liquor licences and registrations, and proposed exemptions and conditional licences to bring them into the system103.

Four centuries of opening times

The decrees, licences, bans and openings that shaped these rooms.

  1. 1589

    Duke Wilhelm V founds the Hofbräuhaus as Munich's court brewery.31 Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

  2. 1634

    Cole's, Boston's first tavern, opens.14 American tavern and saloon

  3. 1667

    Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese in Fleet Street is rebuilt after the Great Fire, says its sign.21 English pub

  4. 1672

    Mexico City licenses 36 fixed pulque outlets, the first pulquerías.91 Mexican cantina and pulquería

  5. 1686

    Le Procope opens in Paris.63 French café and bistrot

  6. 1783

    Washington bids farewell to his officers at Fraunces Tavern, New York, on 4 December.83 American tavern and saloon

  7. 1784

    Joseph II's decree of 17 August lets Austrian winegrowers sell their own wine.8 Viennese Heuriger

  8. 1812

    A Bavarian decree lets brewers sell beer from their cellars, but not food.7 Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

  9. 1828

    The Hofbräuhaus opens to the public.31 Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

  10. 1830

    The Beerhouse Act lets any ratepayer sell beer for a two-guinea licence.15 English pub

  11. 1843

    U Zlatého tygra in Prague first pours beer.42 Czech hospoda

  12. 1893

    The Anti-Saloon League is founded at Oberlin, Ohio.86 American tavern and saloon

  13. 1909

    Mexico City has 1,691 pulquerías.91 Mexican cantina and pulquería

  14. 1915

    Britain's No Treating Order makes buying someone a drink an offence.18 English pub

  15. 1916

    Six o'clock closing begins in South Australia, New South Wales, Victoria and Tasmania.96 Australian pub

  16. 1919

    Belgium's Vandervelde law bans spirits in cafés, until 1984.50 Belgian café

  17. 1920

    US Prohibition begins on 17 January. The Nazi programme is presented at the Hofbräuhaus on 24 February.87,35 American tavern and saloon

  18. 1923

    The Beer Hall Putsch begins at Munich's Bürgerbräukeller on 8 November.36 Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

  19. 1927

    South Africa's Liquor Act bars Africans from licensed premises.12 South African shebeen

  20. 1933

    The 21st Amendment ends Prohibition on 5 December.88 American tavern and saloon

  21. 1934

    Beer Hall Lion opens in Ginza on 8 April.72 Japanese beer hall and rooftop beer garden

  22. 1953

    Osaka gets Japan's first rooftop beer garden.76 Japanese beer hall and rooftop beer garden

  23. 1954

    Mexico City suspends new pulquería licences.91 Mexican cantina and pulquería

  24. 1962

    Africans in South Africa may buy liquor legally from 15 August.12 South African shebeen

  25. 1965

    Merle Thornton and Rosalie Bogner chain themselves to the Regatta Hotel bar, 31 March.97 Australian pub

  26. 1967

    South Australia, the last state, ends six o'clock closing.95 Australian pub

  27. 1970

    McSorley's in New York serves women from 10 August.89 American tavern and saloon

  28. 1976

    Men-only bars become unlawful in Britain.10 English pub

  29. 1980

    Eulji OB Bear opens in Seoul.79 Korean tent bar and hof

  30. 1981

    Mexico City lifts its ban on women in cantinas.11 Mexican cantina and pulquería

  31. 1989

    Ray Oldenburg's The Great Good Place names the “third place”.4 American tavern and saloon

  32. 1990

    The Irish Pub Company is founded and begins exporting the Irish pub.27 Irish pub

  33. 1994

    Havel, Hrabal and Clinton drink at U Zlatého tygra on 11 January.42 Czech hospoda

  34. 1999

    Bavaria's beer garden regulation of 20 April protects the gardens.37 Bavarian beer hall and beer garden

  35. 2005

    England and Wales end fixed pub closing times on 24 November.19 English pub

  36. 2016

    UNESCO inscribes beer culture in Belgium.47 Belgian café

  37. 2019

    Seoul licenses street stalls; Vienna's Heuriger culture joins Austria's heritage inventory.81,58 Korean tent bar and hof

  38. 2022

    Ginza Lion's building becomes a registered Tangible Cultural Property.75 Japanese beer hall and rooftop beer garden

  39. 2024

    French bistrot and café culture joins France's national heritage inventory.67 French café and bistrot
