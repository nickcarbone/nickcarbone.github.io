An atlas of ten villa districts

Where Quiet Money Settled

Ten European villa districts and resort enclaves, from Cascais to Saltsjöbaden, and the land companies, railways and building rules that made them look the way they do.

Nick Carbone · 20 October 2026 · about 17 minutes

Money that wants to be seen buys a penthouse. The money in this atlas did something slower. It bought into a plan, usually drawn up by a land company or a railway, and accepted a list of conditions with the plot: how high to build, how far back from the road, what the roof could be made of, whether a wall was allowed at all. A century later those conditions are why the places look calm. Expensive districts all over Europe have big houses. What sets these ten apart is that they look as if somebody agreed what they should look like, because somebody did.

The ten run from a royal summer habit on the Lisbon coast in 1870 to a Tuscan pine wood sold in plots by a cooperative early in the century and built up in the 1920s. Each entry gives the founding deal, the rule that shaped the houses, the public buildings you can walk into, and how to get there without a car. The entries are ordered by the year each district's story begins.

This atlas sticks to streets, public buildings and documented rules. It names no current residents and points to no private house. Historical founders appear because their companies and contracts are on the public record.

The ten on one sheet

How to read one

The same few pieces turn up again and again, often within a few years of each other.

A railway first, or at least early. Saltsjöbaden's railway and its hotel opened in the same summer, 1893.1,2 Neguri was laid out by the directors of the company that ran the railway past it.3 Gstaad's tourist boom followed the narrow-gauge line from Montreux, completed in 1905.4,5 Kampen's crowds arrived over a rail causeway that opened in 1927.6

A rule written into the sale. The state's sale of the Grunewald land to its developers required country houses of no more than three storeys.7 Neguri banned boundary walls and set a minimum plot of 465 square metres.3 Vienna's Cottage district protected each neighbour's view, light and air by easement from 1873.8 Het Zoute's company regulated even the pitch of the roof.9

A golf club and a grand hotel from the founding decade. Knocke Golf Club was founded in 1909 and the Grand Hotel du Zoute went up in 1909–10.10,9 Neguri's golf society dates from 1911.11 Gstaad Palace opened in December 1913.12

A shared material. In Roma Imperiale it is travertine, terracotta and green-painted wooden window frames.13 In Kampen it is brick under thatch.14 In the Saanenland it is timber.15 The effect is consistency: from the street, one house reads much like the next.

The atlas

1870

Cascais and Estoril Lisbon coast, Portugal

Cascais became fashionable because a king took his holidays there. From 1870 King Luís I used the adapted governor's house inside the town's citadel as a summer residence, and the court spent each September there until 1908.16 The building passed to the presidency after the republic was declared in 1910 and opened as a museum in 2011.16

Courtiers and merchant families followed with summer chalets along the shore. Two are now public. Casa de Santa Maria was built in 1902 to designs by the architect Raul Lino.17 Next to it, the Torre de São Sebastião of 1900 mixes neo-Gothic, neo-Manueline and neo-Moorish in one building; its owner, the Count of Castro Guimarães, left it to the state when he died in 1927, and it opened as a museum in 1931.18

The railway turned the coast into a place you could live on. The Cascais–Pedrouços section opened in 1889, the line reached Cais do Sodré in central Lisbon six years later, and electric trains started on 15 August 1926.19 Estoril, next door, was a deliberate project. In 1914 Fausto de Figueiredo and Augusto Carreira de Sousa commissioned the French architect Henri Martinet to plan a new resort, with a casino and hotels around a park.20 Gambling was legalised there in 1927 and the Hotel Palácio opened in 1930.20,21 Casinos had operated along this coast since the late 19th century, but the Casino Estoril, the one the resort was planned around, opened in August 1931; it was remodelled and reopened in 1968.22,20

Neutral Portugal made Estoril a waystation in the Second World War. The Duke and Duchess of Windsor stayed on this stretch of coast in July 1940.23,24 In the decades after the war, deposed royals settled along this coast, among them Italy's Umberto II.24,20

A royal habit at first; then Figueiredo and Carreira de Sousa's Estoril plan of 1914.16,20
The rule
No single rulebook survives in the record. The organising idea was a resort around a casino concession.
Open to visit
Citadel Palace museum; Casa de Santa Maria; Museu Condes de Castro Guimarães.16,17,18
Getting there
Cascais line from Cais do Sodré, Lisbon.19
1872

The Cottage district Währing and Döbling, Vienna

The name is English on purpose. In 1872 the Wiener Cottage Verein was founded on the architect Heinrich von Ferstel's idea of a garden district of one- and two-family houses on the slopes of north-west Vienna.25,8 The intended buyers were the middle class, and the model was the English suburban cottage with its own garden.25

What made it last was a legal device. From 1873 the land carried easements, rights held by each owner over the neighbouring plots, that protected “free view, light and access to fresh air” and kept out noisy or smelly trades.8 Later versions added limits on the size and height of houses.8 The association still exists, and in 2023–24 it commissioned BOKU University to draw up design criteria for the district.26

The district's green centre came from the same residents. Türkenschanzpark was built from 1885 by local citizens and the Cottage community, then taken over by the city, and opened on 30 September 1888 with Emperor Franz Joseph present. It covers about 15 hectares.27

Laid out by
Wiener Cottage Verein, 1872.25
The rule
Easements protecting view, light and air; later limits on size and height.8
Open to visit
Türkenschanzpark.27 The streets themselves are the exhibit.
Getting there
S45 to Gersthof, or trams 40 and 41 to Gersthof, per BOKU's 2016 directions.28
1889

Grunewald and Wannsee south-west Berlin, Germany

Grunewald was a trade. After 1871 Otto von Bismarck wanted Berlin to have a boulevard to match the Champs-Élysées. Investors agreed to widen the Kurfürstendamm to 53 metres, and in return got first rights to 234 hectares of the Grunewald forest at its western end. A company led by Deutsche Bank carried out the deal, and Bismarck pushed the sale of state land through against objections from the city council and the forestry office.7

Building began in 1889. The ground was wet, so four lakes were dug to drain it: Dianasee, Koenigssee, Herthasee and Hubertussee. The sale contract between the state forestry office and the Kurfürstendamm company required country-house building of no more than three storeys, and otherwise left design free.7,29 Architects who built there include Otto March, Oskar Kaufmann and Otto Rudolf Salvisberg.29,7 Grunewald became a municipality of its own in 1899 and was merged into Wilmersdorf in 1920.29

Its station, opened in 1879 as Hundekehle, carries the district's darkest record. The Gleis 17 memorial there, installed in 1998, lists the trains that deported Berlin's Jews from 1941.7,29

Further south-west, by the Wannsee lake, the Colonie Alsen grew between 1863 and 1898 as a colony of summer villas, laid out by the garden director Gustav Meyer.30 Two of its villas are now public. The painter Max Liebermann's house, finished in 1910 to designs by Paul Otto Baumgarten, has been a museum since 2006.31 Along the shore, the villa where senior Nazi officials met on 20 January 1942 to coordinate the murder of Europe's Jews has been a memorial and education centre since 1992.32

Laid out by
Kurfürstendamm company, backed by Deutsche Bank, from 1889.7
The rule
Country-house style, at most three storeys, set in the state's sale contract.7
Open to visit
Liebermann-Villa; House of the Wannsee Conference; Gleis 17 at Grunewald station.31,32,29
Getting there
S-Bahn to Grunewald or Wannsee.29,33
1893

Saltsjöbaden Nacka, east of Stockholm, Sweden

Saltsjöbaden is a banker's resort built in one go. Knut Agathon Wallenberg planned a continental-style seaside spa on land east of Stockholm, with a hotel, a bathhouse, promenades and its own railway.2,34 Saltsjöbanan opened on 1 July 1893 and runs about 18.5 kilometres; it was electrified in 1913.1 The Grand Hôtel, designed by Erik Josephson, opened that same midsummer.2 Around 60 villas stood by 1896, and Per Hallman drew up a town plan in 1912.2,34

The public buildings are unusually grand for a place this size. Ferdinand Boberg's Uppenbarelsekyrkan, consecrated on 18 May 1913, is dark brick outside and Byzantine-minded within, with bronze doors and altar reliefs by Carl Milles and paintings by Olle Hjortzberg. The founder paid for it and is buried in it.35 On the hill, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences' observatory was inaugurated on 5 June 1931 and housed the Stockholm Observatory until 2001.36,2

The hotel lent its name to modern Swedish labour relations. The Saltsjöbaden Agreement between the trade union confederation LO and the employers' federation SAF, dated 20 December 1938, set the ground rules for collective bargaining and limited open conflict.37,38 The employers' organisation says it was negotiated at the Grand Hotel but signed in central Stockholm.38

Laid out by
Knut Agathon Wallenberg, 1890s; town plan by Per Hallman, 1912.2,34
The rule
One owner's plan: resort, railway and plots sold together.34
Open to visit
Uppenbarelsekyrkan; the Grand Hôtel; the observatory grounds.35,2
Getting there
Saltsjöbanan. As of 2026 trains start from Henriksdal while Slussen is rebuilt; a return to Slussen is planned for 2028.1
1903

Neguri Getxo, Biscay, Spain

Neguri was built by railwaymen for Bilbao's industrial families. Getxo's council approved the plan in May 1903, and the Sociedad de Terrenos de Neguri was formed in March 1904 with 750,000 pesetas of capital. Its founders, José Isaac Amann, Valentín Gorbeña and Enrique Aresti, ran the company that had bought the Bilbao–Las Arenas–Plentzia railway in 1901.3,39 The name, proposed by the linguist Resurrección María de Azkue, means roughly “winter town”: this was meant for living in all year, unlike the summer colonies along the coast.3,39

The rules were among the most precise in this atlas. Only single-family houses, with minor exceptions at the edges. A minimum plot of 465 square metres; between 1903 and 1913 plots averaged about 850. No boundary walls, only railings or hedges, houses set back from the street, and a duty to build within two years.3 The academic study that mapped all this calls Neguri, in its title, a “segregated city”.3

Of the buildings put up before 1936, about 40 per cent were neo-Basque, about 30 per cent followed English styles and about 15 per cent were neo-montañés or palatial; modernism barely registered.3 Emiliano Amann did most to set the district's look, with 22 houses and almost all of the shared buildings, including the church of 1909.3 Manuel María Smith, the most highly regarded architect of the day, designed about a dozen buildings in Neguri as well as the golf clubhouse of 1912–13.39,3

Just south, the Vizcaya Bridge has carried people and vehicles across the river mouth to Portugalete in a gondola hung from a high girder since 1893. It was the first bridge of its kind and has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2006.40

Laid out by
Sociedad de Terrenos de Neguri, 1903–04.3
The rule
Single-family houses, with minor exceptions at the edges; 465 m² minimum plot; railings or hedges, never walls; build within two years.3
Open to visit
The streets and the shared buildings; the Vizcaya Bridge at Las Arenas.40
Getting there
Metro Bilbao line 1 to Neguri, on the route of the 1893 railway; the stop in front of the estate was enlarged as Neguri station in 1906.41,3
1904

Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat Alpes-Maritimes, France

A commune of about 1,500 people on a pine-covered peninsula between Nice and Monaco.42 Its grandest estate era began with a king. In 1904 Leopold II of Belgium bought a villa on the cape, had landscape gardens laid out in 1905–06 and kept adding land until 1908.43 Later owners turned the estate into a botanical garden, and its park and gardens were partly listed as a historic monument in 2021.43

The cape's public treasure was bought out from under him. Béatrice Ephrussi de Rothschild acquired the isthmus plot in 1905, a site Leopold also wanted, and built the Villa Île-de-France there between 1907 and 1912.44,45 The architect is credited differently: the villa's own history names Jacques-Marcel Auburtin, while the Rothschild Archive names Aaron Messiah.44,46 In 1933 she left the house, its gardens and collections to the Académie des beaux-arts, and it is open as a museum with nine themed gardens over about seven hectares.44,45

The cape's other famous interior is Villa Santo Sospir, where Jean Cocteau, staying as a guest from 1950, painted directly onto the walls; he called it “tattooing” the house. It has been a listed monument since 2007.47

Laid out by
No developer: a peninsula settled estate by estate, most famously after 1904.43
The rule
The shape of the land. The cape's size, not a contract, kept it small.
Open to visit
Villa Ephrussi de Rothschild and its gardens.44
Getting there
Train to Beaulieu-sur-Mer, then bus 15. Nice airport is about 18 km away.48
1905

Gstaad and Saanen Bernese Oberland, Switzerland

Gstaad is a village inside the municipality of Saanen, alongside Saanen itself, Schönried and Saanenmöser.4 The old village of Saanen burned almost completely in 1575, and its church of St Mauritius, first recorded in 1228 and enlarged in 1444–47, keeps wall paintings from the later 15th century.4,15

Tourism started around 1900 and took off with the railway. The Montreux–Oberland Bernois line reached Gstaad in December 1904 and was completed to Zweisimmen on 6 July 1905.5,4 Gstaad Palace opened in December 1913.12

The look of the valley is enforced. According to the local tourist board, strict rules since the 1950s have required new buildings to follow the chalet style, with only timber construction and cladding in keeping with it allowed.15 A national rule now sits on top. In March 2012 Swiss voters accepted, by 50.6 per cent, an initiative capping second homes at 20 per cent of a municipality's housing; the law implementing it took effect on 1 January 2016.49,50 In 2019 Saanen's second-home share was recorded at 50.79 per cent, which puts it under the ban on new ones, with exceptions.51,50

Laid out by
No single developer; the railway, from 1904–05.4
The rule
Chalet style in timber for new buildings; a federal cap on new second homes since 2016.15,50
Open to visit
Saanen church; the village streets of Gstaad and Saanen.15
Getting there
MOB line from Montreux or Zweisimmen to Gstaad.5
1908

Het Zoute Knokke-Heist, Belgium

Het Zoute is a company town for holidays. The Lippens family, who managed the Zoute polder, sold 34 hectares to a consortium led by Alfred Verwee in 1887, and in 1908 they founded the Compagnie Het Zoute to develop the rest themselves.52

Its rules were strict, in the words of the local history society. The cottage style was the default. Anglo-Norman half-timbering was favoured around Engelspad and Fochlaan, while villas on Vlaamspad were to echo polder farmhouses with white walls, red tiles and shutters.52 Even an architect's own art deco house had to follow the company's rules, roof pitch included, and the typical result is a bare brick plinth, a rendered upper floor and a gable of decorative half-timber.9 A Flemish heritage decree protects 15 sites in Knokke-Heist, most of them in Het Zoute.9

Golf came with the British, first played here at the turn of the century; the club gives 1899 and the heritage decree 1900.10,9 Knocke Golf Club was founded in 1909 and became Royal in 1925.10 The Grand Hotel du Zoute followed in 1909–10.9

Laid out by
Compagnie Het Zoute, founded by the Lippens family in 1908.52
The rule
Prescribed styles by street, and a regulated roof pitch.52,9
Open to visit
The protected streetscape; the Dominicanenkerk, one of the listed sites.9
Getting there
Train from Bruges to Knokke, opened 1920; the Coast Tram, billed as the world's longest tram line, stops outside.53,54
1912

Kampen Sylt, Germany

Kampen is a village of fewer than 500 residents on Germany's northernmost island.14 Its look is the result of a rule: new houses must follow the traditional style, brick walls under a thatched roof. The rule is generally dated to a municipal ordinance of 1912; we could not find the text of the ordinance itself to confirm it.14

Around it is open country. The Rotes Kliff, a red cliff up to about 30 metres high, runs along the sea to the west and is being worn away by storms. Uwe-Düne, at 52.5 metres, is the island's highest point.14 The village had 1,213 visitors in 1923 and became a resort in that decade.14 Writers and painters came early; the island's tourism body says writers and publishers have been coming to Kampen for more than a century.55

What changed the island was a causeway. The Hindenburgdamm, a rail embankment of about 11 kilometres across the Wadden mudflats, opened on 1 June 1927. Cars still cross it on shuttle trains between Niebüll and Westerland.6,56

Laid out by
An old Frisian village, held to its own shape.14
The rule
Brick and thatch for new houses, by an ordinance dated to 1912 (single source).14
Open to visit
Rotes Kliff; Uwe-Düne; the Kampen lighthouse from the path.14
Getting there
Train over the Hindenburgdamm to Westerland, the island's railhead.6
1920s

Roma Imperiale Forte dei Marmi, Tuscany, Italy

Forte dei Marmi was a marble port before it was a resort. A road to the sea for Apuan marble was opened in 1518 on the orders of Pope Leo X.57,58 The small fort that gives the town its name was completed in 1788 for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo of Tuscany, to guard the coast and the marble trade; since 1997 it has housed a museum of satire and caricature.57,59,60 The town's pier was first built in the 1870s, destroyed in the Second World War and rebuilt in concrete in 1955.57,61 Forte dei Marmi split from Pietrasanta to become a municipality on 26 April 1914.57,61

The villa district behind the beach takes its name from the cooperative company Roma Imperiale, which bought pinewood land in the early 1900s and sold plots to holidaymakers; the district took shape in the 1920s.13 The styles vary, from Florentine Renaissance revival to 1930s Mediterranean rationalism and post-war modernism, but the materials hold it together: travertine, terracotta brick, local stone and green wooden window frames.13 Giovanni Michelucci, Gio Ponti and Giuseppe Pagano all worked here.13,58

Laid out by
The Roma Imperiale cooperative, early 1900s.13
The rule
A shared palette of materials rather than a style.13
Open to visit
The Fortino and its satire museum; Villa Bertelli, a municipal venue; the pier.59,60
Getting there
Forte dei Marmi–Seravezza–Querceta station, about 4 km inland; Pisa airport about 35 km.62
