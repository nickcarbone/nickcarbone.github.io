The Ghosts of Empires

Seventeen arrangements left by empires, from a border in the Hindu Kush to a currency peg in West Africa, traced from the original decision to what they still decide today.

Empires rarely left cleanly. When the flags came down, a great deal of the administrative machinery stayed where it was: a line on a survey map, a clause in a criminal code, a lease with a date in it, a box on an identity card. Some of it was kept because it suited whoever came next. Some survived because removing it would have reopened every question it had settled.

This guide follows seventeen such arrangements: sixteen made by or for British, French, Belgian and American power, or by settler governments within the British Empire, and one that independent African states chose to keep. Each entry starts with the original decision, runs through the moments when it might have ended, and stops at what it still does. The chart below shows how long each has lasted since the power that made it left or the rule was formally ended. Tap a row to go to its entry.

Borders

A border Kabul says it does not recognise

Afghanistan and Pakistan, 1893 to now

On 12 November 1893, in Kabul, Amir Abdur Rahman Khan and Sir Mortimer Durand, Foreign Secretary to the Government of India, signed a seven-clause agreement. It fixed the eastern and southern limit of the Amir's authority "from Wakhan to the Persian border" along a line drawn on an attached map. The Amir gave up claims to Swat, Bajaur and Chitral and to "the rest of the Waziri country", and his annual subsidy from the British rose from 12 to 18 lakh rupees[8, 9]. Joint commissions marked the line on the ground between 1894 and 1896. It runs for about 2,600 km[2], straight through the lands of the Pashtuns.

The record

  1. 1905–1930

    Afghan rulers Habibullah, Amanullah and Nadir Shah reaffirm the arrangements in later agreements with Britain, as the Afghan writer Arwin Rahi points out[1].

  2. 1947

    Pakistan inherits the line at partition. Afghan governments reject the line as imposed by the British; Pakistan says it inherited the agreements as successor state[2, 3].

  3. 1949

    After a Pakistani aircraft bombs a village across the frontier, an Afghan loya jirga declares that it recognises "neither the imaginary Durand nor any similar line" and voids the agreements made since 1893[4].

  4. March 2017

    Pakistan's army announces a fence along the frontier[5, 2].

  5. January 2022

    Taliban forces pull up stretches of the fence. Their defence ministry says Pakistan has "no right to erect barbed wire along the Durand Line and separate the tribes on both sides". Pakistani officials say the fence is more than 90 per cent complete[6].

  6. November 2023

    Pakistan starts demanding passports and visas at the Chaman crossing, ending decades of crossings on local identity papers. Protest leaders said nearly 40,000 people depended on daily crossings for a living[7].

Today

The line works as Pakistan's international border, and governments in Kabul have declared it void regardless of who holds power there[2]. Since clashes in October 2025 the main trade crossings have been closed for long stretches; in January 2026 the president of the Quetta chamber of commerce put traders' losses at "at least 50 billion rupees" a month[10].

What survived
The line itself, almost exactly as surveyed in the 1890s, as a working international frontier.
What changed
The state on the eastern side, and the line's physical form: a fence and visa regime where there used to be open passage.
Who gained
Pakistan says the fence is for counter-terrorism[6]; it keeps territory Kabul has long claimed.
Who lost
Pashtun communities split by the line, and the traders and porters who lived off daily crossings at places such as Chaman[7].

Often misstated

A durable claim holds that the agreement was for 100 years and expired in 1993. The 1893 text has no term or expiry clause[8, 9]; Pakistani officials have called the claim a "canard"[3], and Rahi writes that it was "not signed for 100 years, or any other time frame"[1].

Borders

Africa's choice to keep the lines it was given

The African Union, 1964 to now

Most of Africa's borders were drawn by European governments. What kept them in place after independence was a decision by Africans. Meeting in Cairo in July 1964, the heads of state of the new Organisation of African Unity passed resolution AHG/Res.16(I), in which "all Member States pledge themselves to respect the borders existing on their achievement of national independence"[16]. Daily No. 17 explained why the Berlin conference of 1884–85 did not itself draw these lines[17]; this is the other half of the story.

The record

  1. July 1964

    The resolution passes. Morocco and Somalia, both with claims on neighbours' territory, refused to back it; Judge Abdulqawi Yusuf of the International Court of Justice has written that they were the only two states to vote against[11].

  2. 22 December 1986

    In the Burkina Faso/Mali frontier case, a chamber of the ICJ calls uti possidetis, the rule that new states inherit old administrative lines, "a principle of general scope" whose purpose is to stop "fratricidal struggles provoked by the challenging of frontiers". It concedes that the rule "appears to conflict outright" with self-determination[12].

  3. 1993

    Eritrea votes for independence from Ethiopia, with 99.8 per cent in favour according to an observer's report[13]. Its border is the old Italian colony's.

  4. 11 July 2000

    The Constitutive Act of the African Union writes the principle into Article 4(b): "respect of borders existing on achievement of independence"[14].

  5. January 2011

    South Sudan votes to secede, 98.83 per cent in favour on a 97.58 per cent turnout, and becomes independent on 9 July 2011[15].

Today

The pledge is still African Union law and is cited in boundary cases at The Hague. Judge Yusuf argues that it is narrower than lawyers often assume: no OAU or AU text uses the phrase uti possidetis, and "inviolability does not mean invariability"[11].

What survived
Almost every colonial boundary, frozen as an international border.
What changed
Who guarantees the lines: from European administrations to African states acting together.
Who gained
New states' stability, in the ICJ's own framing[12].
Who lost
Peoples whose claims to self-determination crossed those lines, a tension the court itself acknowledged[12].

Often misstated

The Cairo pledge is often treated as identical to uti possidetis. An ICJ judge says the two are "neither identical nor equivalent"[11].

Leases and zones

A rent cheque Cuba will not cash

Guantánamo Bay, Cuba, 1903 to now

In February 1903 the United States and Cuba agreed that the US would lease 45 square miles of land and water at Guantánamo Bay, at first as a coaling station[19]. Cuba kept "ultimate sovereignty"; the US got "complete jurisdiction and control"[21]. A supplementary lease in July fixed the rent at "two thousand dollars, in gold coin of the United States", payable "as long as the former shall occupy and use said areas", and barred any commercial or industrial enterprise there[22].

The record

  1. 29 May 1934

    A new treaty of relations abrogates the 1903 treaty that carried the Platt Amendment, but keeps the lease. It can end only when "the two contracting parties agree", and the base keeps its area "so long as the United States of America shall not abandon" it[18].

  2. 1934

    The rent is reset to the equivalent of $4,085 a year[19].

  3. 1959 onwards

    After the revolution, Cuba stops cashing the cheques. Fidel Castro said in 2007 that only one had ever been cashed, in 1959, through "confusion", and that he kept the rest in his desk drawer[20].

  4. 12 June 2008

    In Boumediene v. Bush the US Supreme Court finds that Cuba holds de jure sovereignty, the US de facto sovereignty, and that habeas corpus reaches detainees at the base[21].

Today

The rent remains $4,085 a year[19]. In 2014 the US Treasury cheque was still being sent each year through the Swiss embassy in Havana, and Cuba was still leaving it uncashed[23]. Fidel Castro called the occupation illegal[20].

What survived
The whole 1903 lease, through a new treaty in 1934, a revolution in 1959 and a break in relations.
What changed
The base's use, from coaling station to naval base and, since 2002, detention centre.
Who gained
The United States: a deep-water base for a nominal rent, terminable only with its consent[18].
Who lost
Cuba: sovereignty on paper, with no means of ending the lease.

Often misstated

The lease is often said to run "in perpetuity". That word is not in it. The rent was payable for as long as the US occupied the land, and the 1934 treaty made the lease terminable only by mutual agreement or US abandonment[22, 18]. Nor was the original rent "2,000 gold coins": it was $2,000 payable in gold coin[22].

Leases and zones

The lease that set a date for a whole city

Hong Kong, 1898 to 2047

Britain took Hong Kong in three pieces. The island was ceded in 1842 and Kowloon in 1860[28]. The much larger New Territories came by a convention signed in Peking on 9 June 1898, as a lease for "ninety-nine years". The same convention left a Chinese enclave, Kowloon Walled City, inside British territory, and promised "no expropriation or expulsion of the inhabitants"[31].

The record

  1. 1898 onwards

    Colonial land grants in the New Territories are written to run "99 years less three days", so that they expire just before the lease itself[24]. The lease date is built into every title.

  2. 1972

    The colonial government introduces the Small House Policy: a male indigenous villager, descended through the male line from a resident of a recognised village in 1898, may once in his life apply to build a house of up to three storeys and 700 square feet[25].

  3. 19 December 1984

    The Sino-British Joint Declaration is signed. Its Annex III lets leases expiring before 1997 be extended to 30 June 2047[26, 27].

  4. 1 July 1997

    The whole territory, ceded parts included, returns to China[28].

  5. 30 June 2017

    China's foreign ministry calls the Joint Declaration a "historical document" that "no longer has any practical significance"[29].

  6. 5 November 2021

    The Court of Final Appeal upholds the Small House Policy in full as a traditional right protected by Article 40 of the Basic Law[30].

Today

The Basic Law says Hong Kong's "previous capitalist system and way of life shall remain unchanged for 50 years", and keeps the common law[32]. More than 40,000 small house applications had been approved by 2016[25]. The 1898 lease, in other words, still sets two clocks: the 2047 horizon, and a land right defined by who lived in a village in the year the lease was signed.

What survived
The 1898 cut-off for indigenous villagers' rights, the common law, and lease terms pegged to dates set by the old lease.
What changed
Sovereignty, and the meaning China gives to the 1984 treaty.
Who gained
Indigenous male villagers, whose small house right the courts have upheld[30].
Who lost
Challengers argued the policy discriminates by sex and by birth; the courts disagreed[30].

Often misstated

Strictly, only the New Territories lease expired in 1997; the island and Kowloon had been ceded outright[31, 28]. And the Basic Law promises no change "for 50 years"; it does not say what happens after 2047[32].

Leases and zones

A canal zone granted 'in perpetuity' by a Frenchman

Panama, 1903 to now

Panama became independent of Colombia in November 1903 with American backing. Two weeks later its envoy in Washington, Philippe Bunau-Varilla, signed a treaty with Secretary of State John Hay. Bunau-Varilla was a French citizen and the former chief engineer of the failed French canal company[37]. The treaty granted the United States a zone "of the width of ten miles" "in perpetuity", with all the rights it would have "if it were the sovereign of the territory", for $10 million in gold and $250,000 a year[38].

The record

  1. 9 January 1964

    After American students raise only the US flag at Balboa High School inside the Zone, Panamanian students march in, a Panamanian flag is torn and rioting follows. About two dozen people die, 22 Panamanians and four US soldiers by the usual count[33, 34]. The day is now Panama's Martyrs' Day[34].

  2. 7 September 1977

    Omar Torrijos and Jimmy Carter sign two treaties, one handing over the canal and one on its neutrality. The US Senate approves both by 68 votes to 32[35].

  3. 1 October 1979

    The Canal Zone ceases to exist[35].

  4. 31 December 1999

    Panama takes full control of the canal[36].

Today

The neutrality treaty has no end date. It declares the canal "permanently neutral", and from 2000 only Panama may operate it and keep military forces there[39]. The US Senate attached a condition letting either country act independently, including with military force, if the canal is closed or its operation interfered with[40]. Panama's instrument of ratification added its own declaration, which a Panamanian legal scholar argues cancels the condition's effect[41]; the US view is that both governments accepted the Senate's conditions in 1978[40].

What survived
A permanent treaty role for the United States in the canal's neutrality and defence.
What changed
Sovereignty over the zone, and operation of the canal itself.
Who gained
Panama now runs the canal and keeps its tolls.
Who lost
Panama, for 76 years: a foreign-run strip across its middle, under a treaty signed for it by a man without its government's formal consent[35].

Often misstated

The grant "in perpetuity" lasted 96 years[38, 36]. Responsibility for the 1964 deaths is still disputed: US accounts said some Panamanians were killed by their own countrymen firing at US troops, while Panamanians held US forces responsible[34].

Courts and codes

Final appeals still heard in London

The Commonwealth Caribbean and beyond, 1833 to now

The Judicial Committee of the Privy Council was set up by an Act of 1833 to hear appeals from across the empire[53]. It sits in London and "can hear every kind of case: international, constitutional, civil or criminal"[54]. Independence did not end that. Jamaica became independent in 1962[55] and still sends its final appeals to London.

The record

  1. 1970

    Guyana abolishes appeals to the Privy Council on becoming a republic, and has no outside final court for 35 years[42].

  2. 2 November 1993

    In Pratt and Morgan, from Jamaica, the Privy Council rules that where execution would come more than five years after sentence, there are strong grounds for treating the delay as inhuman or degrading punishment[43]. The two men had spent 14 years under sentence and had three death warrants read to them[44].

  3. 16 April 2005

    The Caribbean Court of Justice is inaugurated in Port of Spain; Barbados and Guyana join its appellate jurisdiction that year[45, 46].

  4. 2009

    Lord Phillips, about to become the first president of the new UK Supreme Court, complains of the "disproportionate" time his judges spend on Privy Council appeals[47].

  5. 2010–2023

    Belize (June 2010), Dominica (March 2015) and Saint Lucia (May 2023) move their final appeals to the CCJ[48, 49, 50].

  6. 6 November 2018

    Referendums in Antigua and Barbuda and in Grenada fail to reach the two-thirds majority needed to switch[51, 52].

Today

The Privy Council's own list of jurisdictions still includes ten independent states: Trinidad and Tobago, Mauritius, Kiribati, Jamaica, the Bahamas, Grenada, St Vincent and the Grenadines, St Kitts and Nevis, Antigua and Barbuda and Tuvalu. It also hears appeals from Brunei by agreement with the Sultan, from the Cook Islands and Niue, and from the UK's overseas territories, crown dependencies and sovereign base areas in Cyprus[53]. Five states use the CCJ instead[45].

What survived
A London court as the last word on the constitutions of independent countries.
What changed
Five states have left for a Caribbean court; others have tried and failed.
The case for leaving
Supporters of the CCJ say a regional court is cheaper to reach and completes independence[51].
The vote for staying
Voters have rejected switching in every referendum on it so far: St Vincent and the Grenadines in 2009, Grenada in 2016 and 2018, Antigua and Barbuda in 2018[51].

Often misstated

Kiribati's link is narrower than the list suggests: appeals go to London only on the constitutional rights of the Banaban people and their Rabi Council[56].

Courts and codes

One colonial clause, three dozen countries

India and former British colonies, 1860 to now

The Indian Penal Code was drafted by a law commission under Thomas Babington Macaulay, submitted in 1837, passed in 1860 and brought into force on 1 January 1862[61]. Section 377 punished "carnal intercourse against the order of nature" with up to life imprisonment[62]. Human Rights Watch calls the code the first comprehensive codified criminal law anywhere in the British Empire, and traces how colonial administrators copied it from Asia to East Africa[63].

The record

  1. 2 July 2009

    The Delhi High Court rules in Naz Foundation that the section cannot criminalise consensual sex between adults[57].

  2. 11 December 2013

    The Supreme Court reverses that, saying change is for Parliament[57].

  3. 6 September 2018

    In Navtej Singh Johar, five Supreme Court judges unanimously read the section down: consensual adult sex is out, non-consensual acts remain[57].

  4. 29 November 2022

    Singapore's Parliament repeals section 377A, which criminalised sex between men, by 93 votes to 3, and in the same sitting amends the constitution to protect Parliament's definition of marriage[58].

  5. 1 July 2024

    India's new Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaces the Penal Code[59]. It has no equivalent of section 377, and in August 2026 the Delhi High Court said it could not create one; that was for the legislature[60].

Today

Human Rights Watch counted laws descended from the Indian code in more than three dozen countries in 2008[62]. As of March 2026, the Human Dignity Trust counts 29 of the Commonwealth's 56 members that still criminalise consensual same-sex intimacy[64].

What survived
The clause, or close copies of it, in criminal codes across Africa, Asia, the Pacific and the Caribbean.
What changed
India and Singapore, among others, have removed it, through courts or Parliament.
Who kept such laws
Most of the Commonwealth: 29 of its 56 members criminalise same-sex intimacy, as of March 2026, many under descendants of section 377[64, 62].
Who bore them
Gay men and others prosecuted or threatened under it; HRW calls the laws a "survival of colonial injustice"[62].

Often misstated

The code is often said to have come into force in 1860. It was passed that year and took effect in 1862[61]. And the 2018 judgment read the section down; it did not strike it out[57].

Land

Land revenue fixed 'forever'

Bengal (now India and Bangladesh), 1793 to now

On 22 March 1793 the Governor-General, Lord Cornwallis, proclaimed that the land revenue assessed on Bengal's zamindars, the landholders who collected rent from cultivators, would be "fixed forever" and "unalterable forever, to whomsoever the property may be transferred". The proclamation became Regulation I of 1793. If a zamindar failed to pay on time, sale of his land "will positively and invariably take place"[67]. The state's demand was frozen; what zamindars charged their tenants was not.

The record

  1. 1790s

    Under the "sunset law", hundreds of zamindari estates a month are auctioned for arrears[65].

  2. 1799 and 1819

    New regulations let zamindars seize crops and cattle and confine defaulting tenants, then sublet permanently. In some districts layers of intermediaries pile up between the cultivator and the state[65].

  3. 1859 and 1885

    Rent and tenancy acts claw back some of the landlords' power after peasant unrest[65].

  4. 1950–1955

    East Bengal abolishes the system in 1950; West Bengal's Estates Acquisition Act of 1953 vests the intermediaries' rights in the state from 15 April 1955[65, 66].

Today

In 2005 the economists Abhijit Banerjee and Lakshmi Iyer compared Indian districts where the British gave property rights to landlords with those where they dealt directly with cultivators. The landlord districts had "significantly lower agricultural investments and productivity in the post-independence period" and lower investment in health and education, which the authors attribute to different policy choices rather than to geography[68]. The gap opened after the mid-1960s, with the Green Revolution, long after the zamindars had gone[69].

What survived
Patterns of investment and politics, in the landlord districts Banerjee and Iyer studied[69].
What changed
The zamindari tier itself was abolished in the 1950s.
Who gained
The East India Company got a predictable revenue and an allied landed class[65].
Who lost
Cultivators, who faced rising rents and cesses while the state's take stayed fixed[65].

Often misstated

The date is sometimes given as 1773; it was 1793[67]. Banerjee and Iyer's "landlord" districts also include areas outside Bengal, so their findings are about landlord-based systems in general[69].

Land

The 1913 date in South Africa's land law

South Africa, 1913 to now

The Natives Land Act, passed by the parliament of the Union of South Africa, then a self-governing British dominion, became law on 19 June 1913. It confined land ownership by Black South Africans to about 7 per cent of the country, raised to about 13 per cent by a further act in 1936[70]. Sol Plaatje wrote: "Awaking on Friday morning, June 20, 1913, the South African Native found himself, not actually a slave, but a pariah in the land of his birth"[77].

The record

  1. 1936

    The Native Trust and Land Act raises the ceiling to about 13 per cent[70].

  2. 30 June 1991

    The Abolition of Racially Based Land Measures Act takes effect, repealing what had by then been renamed the Black Land Act, 1913[71].

  3. 1994

    The Restitution of Land Rights Act lets people claim land lost to racial laws, but only for dispossessions after 19 June 1913: the old Act's own date[72]. About 80,000 claims are lodged by the 1998 deadline[73].

  4. 2014–2016

    Claims reopen and 166,560 more are lodged before the Constitutional Court strikes down the reopening for lack of public consultation[73].

  5. December 2024

    President Ramaphosa signs the Expropriation Act, announced in January 2025, which allows nil compensation in some public-interest cases[74, 75, 76].

Today

The government's 2017 land audit found that of farmland owned by individuals, 72 per cent was held by white owners and 4 per cent by Black African owners; individuals held only about 39 per cent of privately owned land, the rest being in trusts, companies and other hands whose race was not recorded[78]. The Expropriation Act had still not been brought into force in October 2025, when the president conceded that parts of one section were flawed[76]. Challenges to it were heard in the Western Cape High Court in August 2026[79].

What survived
The 1913 date, now the cut-off for restitution, and an ownership pattern the audit still records.
What changed
The Act and its successors were repealed in 1991.
Who gained
White landowners under the Act, which reserved most of the country for them[70].
Who lost
Black South Africans, barred from buying or leasing land outside the reserves[70, 77].

Often misstated

"White South Africans own 72 per cent of the land" misreads the audit. The figure is 72 per cent of farmland owned by individuals, which is itself under 40 per cent of privately owned land[78].

Land

Why most of Fiji cannot be sold

Fiji, 1874 to now

When Fiji was ceded to Britain in 1874, Governor Sir Arthur Gordon halted land sales. A commission in 1876 confirmed about 400,000 acres of earlier European claims as freehold; a Native Lands Commission from 1880 recorded the rest as belonging to Fijian kin groups[85]. In 1940 the Native Land Trust Board took over the leasing of that land, with Ratu Sir Lala Sukuna as its first general manager[85]. Meanwhile the colony's sugar industry ran on indentured labourers brought from India, whose descendants farmed on leases.

The record

  1. 10 October 1970

    Fiji becomes independent with the system intact[80].

  2. 1976

    The Agricultural Landlord and Tenant Act sets 30-year farm leases and gives older leases a single 20-year extension[81].

  3. 1997–1999

    Leases under the Act begin to expire. Only 26 per cent of leases expiring in those years are renewed to the sitting tenants; Indo-Fijians are 77 per cent of cane growers[81].

  4. May 2000

    George Speight takes Prime Minister Mahendra Chaudhry hostage. The US Congressional Research Service noted fears among many indigenous Fijians that lease terms would favour tenants[82].

  5. 2002

    119,523 hectares of state land are transferred to the Native Land Trust Board[83].

  6. 2013

    The constitution says iTaukei land "shall not be permanently alienated, whether by sale, grant, transfer or exchange", except to the state[84].

Today

The iTaukei Land Trust Board puts the share of Fiji's land in customary ownership at about 91 per cent, and freehold at about 6 per cent[86, 87]. It manages about 53,000 leases[86]. Older academic counts put native land at about 83 per cent before the 2002 transfers[83].

What survived
Customary ownership of almost all land, and a ban on selling it, now in the constitution.
What changed
The board's name, the lease law, and the share of land, which grew after state land was transferred.
Who gained
Fijian landowning groups, who kept title through colonial rule and after[85].
Who lost
Indo-Fijian tenant farmers, many of whom lost their farms when leases were not renewed[81, 83].

Often misstated

The share of native land is quoted anywhere from 83 to 91 per cent. The differences are real: the share rose after the 2002 transfer of state land, and the higher figure is the board's own[83, 86].

Networks

Three railway gauges in one country

Australia, 1852 to now

In 1850 Francis Webb Sheilds, the Irish-born engineer of the Sydney Railway Company, persuaded New South Wales to adopt the Irish gauge of 5 ft 3 in; London agreed, and in 1852 the colony made it law and told Victoria and South Australia, which ordered broad-gauge equipment[93, 94]. Sheilds resigned. His successor, the Scottish engineer James Wallace, preferred the British standard of 4 ft 8½ in, and in 1854 New South Wales switched[93, 95]. Queensland, Western Australia and Tasmania later built to a cheaper 3 ft 6 in.

The record

  1. 14 June 1883

    The New South Wales and Victorian lines meet at Albury, where passengers have to change trains[88, 89]. Mark Twain, passing through in 1895, wrote: "Think of the paralysis of intellect that gave that idea birth"[89].

  2. 1962

    A standard-gauge line from Albury to Melbourne is completed; on 12 April a ceremony marks its official opening, and through passenger trains between Sydney and Melbourne begin[90].

  3. 23 February 1970

    The first Indian Pacific leaves Sydney for Perth on an unbroken standard-gauge line[91].

  4. February 2004

    The Ghan reaches Darwin on a new standard-gauge line from Adelaide, a link first proposed in 1858[92].

Today

Sydney, Melbourne, Adelaide, Perth and Darwin are now joined by standard gauge[90, 91, 92], but all three gauges survive. Victoria's regional freight network is still a mix of broad and standard gauge, and Infrastructure Australia has described miners trucking concentrate from broad-gauge lines to the standard-gauge network[96]. Inland Rail, a 1,600 km freight route planned from Melbourne to Brisbane, was still being built in 2026, with work focused on the section to Parkes in New South Wales; north of Parkes, beyond two completed sections, the government has committed only to preserving the corridor[97].

What survived
All three colonial gauges, with conversions bolted on.
What changed
The interstate main lines, standardised between 1962 and 1995.
Who gained
Each colony's own railway and ports. Twain was told in 1895 that the breaks came from "jealousy" between the colonies[89].
Who lost
Shippers, then and now, who pay to move goods between incompatible tracks[96].

Often misstated

Victoria is often said to have chosen broad gauge to spite New South Wales. It followed New South Wales's own 1852 law; it was New South Wales that changed its mind[93].

Language

English for fifteen years, then indefinitely

India, 1835 to now

On 2 February 1835 Thomas Babington Macaulay wrote his Minute on Indian education, arguing that the East India Company's education money should go on English. He claimed that "a single shelf of a good European library was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia", and wanted "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste"[104]. Within weeks the Governor-General, William Bentinck, resolved that the government's object should be "the promotion of European literature and science"[105].

The record

  1. 14 September 1949

    India's Constituent Assembly adopts what becomes Article 343: Hindi in Devanagari is the official language of the Union, but English continues for all official purposes "for a period of fifteen years"[98]. Article 348 keeps English as the language of the Supreme Court and High Courts and of the authoritative text of every law[99].

  2. 7 August 1959

    Jawaharlal Nehru tells the Lok Sabha that English will be an alternative language "as long as people require it", and that the decision will be left "to the non-Hindi-knowing people"[100].

  3. 1963

    The Official Languages Act says English "may" continue after 1965. Opponents in Madras wanted "shall"[101, 102].

  4. January–February 1965

    As the fifteen years run out, protests against Hindi spread across Madras State. The government's estimate was about 70 dead[102, 103].

  5. 1967

    An amendment keeps English until every state that has not adopted Hindi, and then both houses of Parliament, resolve to drop it: in effect, a veto for any one state[101].

Today

Under Article 348, proceedings in the Supreme Court are still in English, and so is the authoritative text of every Act of Parliament[99]. A governor can, with the president's consent, allow a High Court to work in Hindi or the state's language, and under the Official Languages Act even to give judgments in it, so long as an English translation goes with them[99, 101].

What survived
English as a language of the Union government, the higher courts and the statute book.
What changed
A fifteen-year transition became an open-ended guarantee with a veto held by non-Hindi states.
Who gained
Non-Hindi states, whose leaders won the guarantee[102], and the English-educated.
Who lost
Anyone who does not read English, since Supreme Court judgments and most High Court judgments are still written in it[99, 101].

Often misstated

A speech in which Macaulay supposedly told Parliament on 2 February 1835 that he had seen no beggar in India circulates widely. There is no record of it, and the date is that of the Minute, which argues the opposite[106].

Concessions

A Persian oil concession that became BP

Iran, 1901 to now

On 28 May 1901, Mozaffar al-Din Shah granted William Knox D'Arcy, a British investor, a 60-year concession for oil and gas across about 480,000 square miles of Persia, everything except five northern provinces. Persia got £20,000 in cash, £20,000 in shares and 16 per cent of "annual net profits", a phrase so loosely defined that it caused disputes for decades[109].

The record

  1. 26 May 1908

    Oil is struck at Masjed Soleyman. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company is formed in 1909[107].

  2. 1914

    The British government takes a 51 per cent stake to secure oil for the Royal Navy[107, 108].

  3. 1932–1933

    Iran cancels the concession; the replacement signed in 1933 shrinks the area but runs for another 60 years, to 1993[109].

  4. March 1951

    Iran's parliament nationalises the oil industry, on a measure championed by Mohammad Mosaddegh, who becomes prime minister weeks later[109].

  5. 19 August 1953

    Mosaddegh is overthrown in a coup[110]. In 2013 a released CIA history acknowledged that the agency helped plan and carry it out, with British intelligence[111].

  6. 1954

    A consortium deal gives the British company 40 per cent of Iranian production, and the company takes the name British Petroleum[109, 107].

Today

BP, the direct corporate descendant of the D'Arcy concession[107], is one of the world's largest oil companies. Iran's oil has been state-owned since 1951, and the 1953 coup remains a fixed point in Iranian politics.

What survived
The company, which has outlived both concessions.
What changed
Ownership of Iran's oil, which passed to the state.
Who gained
The British state, which got a secure naval fuel supply and a profitable shareholding[107, 108].
Who lost
Iran, by its long-running complaint over the profit formula[109].

Often misstated

How much the coup owed to the CIA and MI6 and how much to Iranian clerics, officers and crowds is still argued over by historians[112, 113]. The State Department's retrospective volume of documents appeared only in 2017, because the 1989 edition left out the CIA's role[110, 114].

Concessions

A 99-year concession ended twelve years early

Egypt, 1854 to now

On 30 November 1854, Egypt's ruler Sa'id Pasha gave the French diplomat Ferdinand de Lesseps a concession to cut a canal through the isthmus of Suez; the definitive act of January 1856 set the company's life at 99 years from the canal's opening[121, 117]. Egypt was to receive 15 per cent of the profits[116]. The canal opened on 17 November 1869[122].

The record

  1. 25 November 1875

    Britain buys the bankrupt Khedive Ismail's shares for about £4 million. It had expected 177,642 shares and got 176,602, so the price was cut[115]. The stake made Britain the largest shareholder, with about 44 per cent[116].

  2. 29 October 1888

    The Convention of Constantinople declares that the canal "shall always be free and open, in time of war as in time of peace"[117].

  3. 26 July 1956

    Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalises the company, offering compensation at the previous day's share price[117]. Israel, Britain and France invade and are forced into a ceasefire by US pressure[118].

  4. 24 April 1957

    Egypt declares that it will respect the "terms and spirit" of the 1888 Convention[119].

  5. 1967–1975

    The canal closes after the Six-Day War. A convoy of fifteen ships is trapped, fourteen of them in the Great Bitter Lake, for about eight years[120].

Today

Under the original concession the company would have handed over the canal in 1968[117]. The Suez Canal Authority still invokes the 1888 Convention; in 2025 its chairman cited it in rejecting a US demand for free passage for American ships[123].

What survived
The 1888 regime of free passage for all ships, which Egypt chose to keep.
What changed
Ownership and toll revenue, since 1956.
Who gained
European shareholders and, from 1875, the British state[115, 116].
Who lost
Egypt, which sold its shares and later its 15 per cent profit share[116].

Often misstated

Britain did not buy a controlling stake in 1875: about 44 per cent[116]. And Nasser nationalised the company; the canal ran through Egyptian territory all along[117].

Categories

An ethnic label on an identity card

Rwanda, 1930s to 1990s

In the 1930s the Belgian administration asked every Rwandan to declare whether he was Hutu, Tutsi or Twa. The answer was entered in local records and on identity cards that adults then had to carry. The registration counted about 15 per cent Tutsi, 84 per cent Hutu and 1 per cent Twa, and children, including those of mixed marriages, took their father's category[124]. The date is usually given as 1933–34, though historians have found sources for several years in that decade[128, 129].

The record

  1. 1962

    Rwanda becomes independent. The new republican government keeps labelling all Rwandans as Hutu, Tutsi or Twa[124].

  2. 1990–1993

    In massacres before the genocide, Tutsi trying to pass roadblocks are identified by their cards and killed. Donors discuss but do not insist on removing the label[124, 125].

  3. April–July 1994

    During the genocide, roadblock teams are told to check identity cards. A witness told Human Rights Watch: "At the barrier, you showed your identity card and they killed you if you were Tutsi"[125].

  4. 1994–1995

    In September 1994 Paul Kagame says identity documents will carry no ethnic reference[126]; a Rwandan minister later dated the change to 1995[127].

Today

Since 2013 a national programme, Ndi Umunyarwanda ("I am Rwandan"), has promoted a single Rwandan identity[130], and a 2018 law sets prison terms of five to seven years for genocide ideology, denial and justification[131]. Amnesty International has argued that vaguely worded laws of this kind have been used against critics[132].

What survived
For three decades after independence, the colonial category itself, printed on every adult's card.
What changed
The label was removed after 1994; public use of ethnic categories is now restricted by law.
Who gained
Under Belgian rule, Tutsi elites favoured by the administration[124].
Who lost
Under the republics, Tutsi, for whom the card became the means of identification in 1994[125].

Often misstated

It is often said that the Belgians classed anyone with ten or more cattle as Tutsi. Human Rights Watch says this "is not correct": a ten-cow threshold existed in tax rules, but "the procedure for population registration took no account of ownership of cattle"[124, 129].

Categories

Communities declared criminal by notification

India, 1871 to now

The Criminal Tribes Act of 1871 let a provincial government report any "tribe, gang or class of persons" as "addicted to the systematic commission of non-bailable offences". Once notified as a criminal tribe, its members could be registered, confined to set limits, required to carry passes and placed in settlements, and the notification was "conclusive proof" that no court could question[141]. Introducing the bill, T. V. Stephens argued that if crafts were hereditary under caste there must also be "hereditary criminals"[134].

The record

  1. 1911 and 1924

    New versions extend the system. At its height it covers well over a hundred communities and millions of people[133, 134].

  2. 31 August 1952

    The Act is repealed and the communities are "denotified"[135]. The anniversary is marked as Vimukt Jati Diwas, officially so in Uttar Pradesh since 2025[136].

  3. 1950s

    States pass Habitual Offenders Acts aimed at individuals, which critics say carried the same stigma forward[137, 138].

  4. 2007

    The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination says denotified and nomadic tribes "continue to be stigmatized" and urges repeal of the habitual offenders laws[139].

  5. 2017

    A national commission identifies 1,262 denotified, nomadic and semi-nomadic communities[135].

  6. 3 October 2024

    In Sukanya Shantha v Union of India, prompted by the journalist's own investigation, the Supreme Court strikes down caste-based work allocation in prisons and says "the tendency to treat members of denotified tribes as habitual to crime" reinforces a stereotype[137, 140].

Today

The Supreme Court ordered prison manuals rewritten and told states that references to "habitual offenders" must follow their own statutes, not caste[137, 140]. In a 2023 lecture Supreme Court judge K. V. Viswanathan said the Act's "evil shadow" still looms over these communities[138].

What survived
The suspicion, carried into police and prison practice through state habitual offender laws[139, 140].
What changed
The legal notion of a criminal community, abolished in 1952.
Who gained
Colonial policing, which got powers of registration and confinement without trial[141].
Who lost
Nomadic and denotified communities: surveillance, lost livelihoods and arbitrary arrest[138].

Often misstated

The Act is often said to have been replaced by a single Habitual Offenders Act of 1952; even the UN committee used that shorthand[139]. The replacements were state laws, such as Rajasthan's of 1953[137].

Money

A currency still pegged through Paris

West and Central Africa, 1945 to now

The CFA franc was created on 26 December 1945, the day France ratified the Bretton Woods agreements; the initials originally stood for "colonies françaises d'Afrique"[143]. France guaranteed its convertibility, and in return the issuing central banks kept a share of their foreign reserves in an operations account at the French Treasury and gave France seats on their boards[147]. Most member states became independent in 1960[149], and kept the currency.

The record

  1. 1973

    Under an operations-account convention of December 1973, the West African central bank must keep 65 per cent of its reserves with the French Treasury, a rate that holds until 2005[142].

  2. 12 January 1994

    The CFA franc is devalued by half against the French franc[143].

  3. 1 January 1999

    The peg moves to the euro at 655.957 CFA francs per euro[143].

  4. 2005

    The deposit requirement falls to 50 per cent[142].

  5. 21 December 2019

    In Abidjan, Alassane Ouattara and Emmanuel Macron announce that the eight West African members will rename the currency the eco, stop depositing reserves in Paris and remove French representatives from their central bank's bodies[144, 145].

  6. 2020–2021

    France's Senate gives final approval to the agreement in January 2021[146], and the operations account is closed: the French Treasury dates this to January 2020, the central bank's 2021 annual report to April 2021[147, 148].

Today

The name has not changed. The Banque de France still describes the franc CFA in the West African union, and the peg to the euro and the French guarantee remain; the six Central African members' arrangements, including the reserve deposit, are unchanged[150, 147]. In August 2025 the West African central bank's governor said no member had officially signalled it would leave, despite talk from leaders of the Sahel military governments[151].

What survived
The fixed peg, France's guarantee, the Central African arrangements and the name.
What changed
For West Africa: no reserves in Paris and no French seats on the central bank's bodies, since 2020–21.
Who gained
Member governments, whose peg is backed by an unlimited French guarantee; France now calls itself a "strict financial guarantor"[147].
Who lost
Anyone holding CFA francs in January 1994, when the devaluation halved their value against the French franc[143].

Often misstated

"The CFA franc was abolished in 2019" is wrong: the reform was announced, and the currency and its peg are still in place[150].
