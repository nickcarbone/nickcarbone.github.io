Register of culinary births, claimed and recorded

Twelve Dishes With a Paper Trail

Twelve famous dishes, the people and places that claim to have invented them, and what the earliest menus, newspapers and archives actually show.

In the autumn of 1963 the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New York, took out newspaper advertisements for fried chicken wings.1 The bar's own account of the dish that made it famous begins on a Friday night in 1964.2 A historic marker put up outside in 2026 repeats the later date.3

Restaurant origin stories are usually like this. They arrive decades after the event, from the people with most to gain, and they are told with a named customer, a missing ingredient and a kitchen about to close. Some of them are true. The way to find out is to set the story beside the oldest surviving piece of paper: a dated menu, a newspaper column, a cookbook, a trademark filing, a court settlement.

This register does that for twelve dishes. Each entry records the claimed date and place of birth, the people named as parents, the rivals, and the earliest document anyone has found. It then says what stands at the address now, as of November 2026, and whether you can still order the dish there. Two findings recur. The name of a dish tends to reach print about a decade after the story says it was invented. And the best-documented fact is almost always the place; the person at the stove is the part nobody wrote down.

The gap between the story and the paper

Each row runs from the year the origin story gives to the year the dish's name has first been found in print or on a menu. Carpaccio and the Margherita have no second mark, because no first printing of the name has been pinned down. The Buffalo wing's line runs backwards.

Entry No. 01 of 12

Caesar salad

Unproven

Born, as claimed
4 July 1924, Caesar's Place, Tijuana, Mexico
Named parent
Caesar Cardini, restaurateur, born 1896 in Baveno, Italy; died 1956 in Los Angeles4,5
Rival claims
His brother Alex Cardini; the family of Livio Santini, a cook in the restaurant
First on paper
31 July 1946, Dorothy Kilgallen's newspaper column, located by Barry Popik5

The Fourth of July story comes from Cardini's daughter Rosa: a holiday rush, a depleted kitchen, a salad improvised from what was left.6,7 Cardini made the claim himself in an interview with United Press in 1952.6

Others wanted it. Alex Cardini said the salad was his, first served as an “aviator's salad”, and was still saying so to the Chicago Tribune in 1967.6 Livio Santini's family holds that the recipe was his mother's, and the restaurant now credits him with making it at the table in 1924.7,8 A 1949 item in the New York Herald Tribune gave it to a head waiter at the Coronado Hotel.5

The documents are later than all of them. Los Angeles Times items of 1933 and 1936 mention Cardini's Tijuana restaurant and say nothing about a salad. The name turns up in July 1946, when Kilgallen reported it as a Hollywood craze about to reach New York.5 The earliest detailed recipe the desk found described is Cardini's own from 1952, and it has no anchovies.6

At the address today

Sources do not agree on where the 1924 restaurant stood. La Prensa San Diego has the business opening in an alley in 1923 and moving twice before settling on Avenida Revolución.9 The Caesar's that trades on Avenida Revolución today closed in 2009; La Prensa reported a reopening under the Plascencia family, set for 24 July 2010.9 The salad is made at the table; the Associated Press reported in 2024 that the dining room turned out as many as 300 a day, and diner reviews were still being posted in December 2025.7,10

Desk verdict: unproven. Tijuana and the Cardini restaurant are well attested by the 1930s. Which person in that kitchen made the first one cannot be recovered from any document yet found.

Entry No. 02 of 12

Buffalo wings

Contradicted

Born, as claimed
A Friday night in 1964, Anchor Bar, 1047 Main Street, Buffalo, New York
Named parent
Teressa Bellissimo, who ran the bar with her husband Frank
Rival claim
John Young, of Wings and Things on Buffalo's East Side
First on paper
September to November 1963: five Anchor Bar advertisements for fried chicken wings with barbecue sauce1

When Calvin Trillin went to Buffalo for The New Yorker in 1980, the Bellissimo family gave him two stories that cannot both be true. Frank said a delivery of wings had arrived by mistake and Teressa turned them into bar food. Their son Dominic said it was a Friday night, the regulars were Catholic, and the wings went out free at midnight when meat was allowed again.2

Trillin also met John Young, who disputed that the Anchor had been first. Young sold whole wings, breaded and dressed in what he called mambo sauce. The Buffalo History Museum dates his Wings and Things on Jefferson Avenue to 1966, and Young told a local paper in 1981 that he had been selling 5,000 pounds of wings in 1962.2,11,12

The advertisements complicate the Anchor's version from the other direction. A Buffalo News reporter, Francesca Bond, found them on microfilm of the Courier-Express in 2025: five notices from the autumn of 1963, three of them promising a Roquefort garnish.1 Fried wings were on sale in Buffalo long before either man, as it happens. The museum holds an 1857 hotel bill of fare listing “Chicken Wings, fried”.11

At the address today

The Anchor Bar still occupies 1047 Main Street. A William G. Pomeroy Foundation marker dated 2026 stands there, reading that Buffalo wings were served in 1964 by the Bellissimo family.3 The desk found no current outlet for John Young's recipe.

Desk verdict: contradicted. The address is right and the date is not. The Anchor was advertising wings a year before the night of its legend, and Young's competing claim has never been disproved.

Entry No. 03 of 12

Eggs Benedict

Unproven

Born, as claimed
1894, the Waldorf Hotel, Fifth Avenue and 33rd Street, New York
Named parent
Lemuel Benedict, a broker with a hangover, by his own account in 1942
Rival claims
Commodore E. C. Benedict; Mr and Mrs LeGrand Benedict at Delmonico's; Delmonico's chef Charles Ranhofer
First on paper
January 1894, “eggs a la Benedict” in a short story13,14

Lemuel Benedict told The New Yorker in December 1942 that 48 years earlier he had walked into the Waldorf and ordered buttered toast, crisp bacon, two poached eggs and hollandaise, and that the maître d'hôtel, Oscar Tschirky, had liked the idea enough to put a version on the menu.13,15

The rivals surfaced later still. In 1967 the New York Times food editor Craig Claiborne printed a recipe passed down, at two removes, from the banker Commodore E. C. Benedict. A reader wrote in reply that the dish had been created for her relative Mrs LeGrand Benedict at Delmonico's around the turn of the century.14 Delmonico's itself credits its nineteenth-century chef Charles Ranhofer.16 Ranhofer's cookbook The Epicurean is often cited for a recipe called “Eggs à la Benedick”, and Popik and others date that recipe to the book's first edition of 1894. The food writer Rachel Wharton suggests the 1894 edition lacked it and that it was added in a 1912 printing.13,15,14

The earliest use of the name is in fiction. A story published in January 1894 has its characters lunch on “eggs a la Benedict”; Popik found it in the New-York Daily Tribune of 7 January and Wharton cites the Overland Monthly of the same month.13,14 The Waldorf had opened only in 1893.14 Wharton gives the first printed recipe as February 1897, in the magazine Table Talk, and notes that Tschirky's own Waldorf cookbook of 1896 leaves the dish out.14

At the address today

The Waldorf was demolished from 1929 and the Empire State Building went up on the site.17 Delmonico's, at 56 Beaver Street, reopened in 2023 after three years shut and was trading in March 2026, when its operator announced a second restaurant in Midtown.18,19 The restaurant says eggs Benedict remains on its menu.16

Desk verdict: unproven. Every personal claim dates from 1942 or later. A dish already familiar enough to name in a magazine story in the first days of 1894 leaves Lemuel Benedict's breakfast almost no time to have come first.

Entry No. 04 of 12

Nachos

Unproven

Born, as claimed
1940 or 1943, the Victory Club or El Moderno, Piedras Negras, Coahuila, Mexico
Named parent
Ignacio “Nacho” Anaya, waiter, born 1895 in Chihuahua; died 1975 in Piedras Negras20,21
Rival claims
None found
First on paper
30 December 1947, San Antonio Light, at a San Antonio restaurant21

The story has a group of American military wives crossing from Eagle Pass, Texas, and arriving at a restaurant when the cook was out. Anaya, who was working the floor, put cheese and jalapeño on fried tortilla pieces and gave the result his own nickname.22,23

The details move around. A San Antonio Express and News feature of May 1954, the earliest piece found that names Anaya, puts the event about 14 years before, which gives 1940. The Oxford English Dictionary researcher Adriana Orr and Anaya's son settled on 1943.21,23 Most accounts set it at the Victory Club. An interview with Anaya quoted in Robb Walsh's Tex-Mex Cookbook puts it at the Moderno.24

In print the dish appears first on the Texas side. The San Antonio Light of 30 December 1947 described nachos as a Mexican appetiser on offer at a restaurant in that city, and the dictionary's earliest quotation is from a 1949 cookbook, A Taste of Texas.21 Orr's searches in 1988 turned up a 1954 church cookbook from Eagle Pass with a recipe for “Nachos Especiales”; before her work, the earliest example in the dictionary's files was from 1978.22,25

At the address today

The desk could not establish an address for either restaurant or what occupies the sites. Texas Highways reports that El Moderno and Nacho's, the restaurant that bore his name, have both closed.24 Piedras Negras has a bronze plaque to him and holds a nacho festival each October, which reached its thirtieth edition in 2025.22,26

Desk verdict: unproven. Nobody else has ever claimed the nacho, which counts for a good deal. The paper trail begins seven years late and on the far side of the Rio Grande.

Entry No. 05 of 12

Cobb salad

Unproven

Born, as claimed
A late night in 1937, the Brown Derby, Los Angeles
Named parent
Robert H. Cobb of the Brown Derby, born 1899 in Moberly, Missouri; died 197027
Rival claims
Versions crediting a Derby chef, and dates of 1926, 1929 and 1938
First on paper
3 January 1947, a Los Angeles Times column, located by Barry Popik28

The Brown Derby's account is that Cobb raided the kitchen late one night, chopped up what he found, and shared it with the cinema owner Sid Grauman, who came back the next day and asked for a Cobb salad.29 It is the restaurant's story and, as one food columnist put it, legend is most of what there is.30

Retellings disagree on nearly every point. Some place the night at the Derby on Vine Street in Hollywood, others at the original on Wilshire Boulevard.31,32,33 One variation has the Derby's chef inventing it in 1929 and naming it for his employer.31 A Montana newspaper in 1967 dated it to forty years earlier.28

Popik's trawl of the Los Angeles Times found a 1936 feature on the Derby's buffet that lists a “chicken salad Derby” and no Cobb, a 1939 recipe headed “Brown Derby Chef's Salad”, and then, in January 1947, a columnist mentioning two portions of Cobb salad. The first full description he found is in a 1949 restaurant guide. A 1982 application to register “Cobb Salad” as a trademark, claiming first use in 1937, was abandoned two years later.28

At the address today

Neither Derby survives. The Wilshire restaurant closed in 1980; its hat-shaped dome was kept and now sits on top of a shopping centre.34,33 The Vine Street building closed in 1985 and was demolished in 1994, and the Historical Marker Database records that nothing of it remains.33,35 A licensed reproduction, the Hollywood Brown Derby at Disney's Hollywood Studios in Florida, listed Cobb salad on its menu in November 2026.36

Desk verdict: unproven. The Brown Derby's ownership of the salad is not in doubt by 1947. The night in 1937, the companion and even the branch rest on the restaurant's word.

Entry No. 06 of 12

Reuben sandwich

Holds up

Born, as claimed
A poker night in the 1920s, Blackstone Hotel, 302 South 36th Street, Omaha, Nebraska
Named parent
Reuben Kulakofsky, wholesale grocer, with the hotel's Bernard Schimmel
Rival claim
Arnold Reuben, Reuben's Restaurant, New York, about 1914
First on paper
1934, a Blackstone Hotel menu: “Reuben Sandwich”, 40 cents37,38

Omaha's version is set at a card game at the Blackstone, which Charles Schimmel owned. Kulakofsky asked for corned beef and sauerkraut, and Schimmel's son Bernard, in the kitchen, built the sandwich. History Nebraska gives the year as 1925, the hotel offers a range from 1920 to 1935, and Kulakofsky's family says he assembled it himself from a platter sent up.39,40,38 One account from the Schimmel family has Bernard returning to the hotel only in 1928.41

New York's version belongs to Arnold Reuben, who said he made a “Reuben Special” for an actress in about 1914. His daughter described it in a letter to Craig Claiborne of the New York Times in 1976: ham, turkey, Swiss cheese and coleslaw. Jim Rader, an etymologist at Merriam-Webster who went through the evidence, pointed out that this is a different sandwich.42,37

The menus are Nebraskan. A Blackstone menu from 1934 lists a Reuben sandwich at 40 cents.37,38 A 1937 menu from the Cornhusker Hotel in Lincoln, another Schimmel property, has one too.39 On the menu evidence Rader tentatively favoured Nebraska, with misgivings, noting that no first-hand witness to the card game had been found. The food historian Andrew Smith, who had argued for New York, accepted the menus as the earliest references to the sandwich by name.42,38 In 1956 the Reuben won a national sandwich contest; a trade magazine of that year credits the entry to Fern Snider, chef at the Rose Bowl restaurant in Omaha.37

At the address today

The Blackstone building reopened in 2020 as the Kimpton Cottonwood Hotel after a restoration.43,40 Its Orléans Room promotes the Reuben on the hotel's website, which was current in November 2026.44

Desk verdict: holds up. The oldest menus are from Omaha and nothing comparable has surfaced in New York. Who put the sandwich together at the card table, and in which year, no document says.

Entry No. 07 of 12

Tiramisù

Unproven

Born, as claimed
Between 1970 and 1972, Le Beccherie, Piazza Ancilotto 9, Treviso, Italy
Named parents
Alba Campeol, of the family that owned the restaurant, and its pastry chef Roberto Linguanotto
Rival claims
Norma Pielli, Albergo Roma, Tolmezzo; Mario Cosolo, Al Vetturino, Pieris
First on paper
1981, Giuseppe Maffioli in the magazine Vin Veneto45,46

Le Beccherie says the idea came from the egg-and-sugar pick-me-up Alba Campeol was given when she was expecting her son in 1955, and that the finished dessert went on the menu in 1972.47,48 Linguanotto dated it to 1970.49 In 1981 the Treviso gastronome Giuseppe Maffioli wrote that the dessert had been born in the city a little over ten years earlier and credited the restaurant and its pastry chef.45 In 2010 the recipe was deposited with the Accademia Italiana della Cucina by notarial deed.48,45

Friuli, the region to the east, has two claims. At the Albergo Roma in Tolmezzo, Norma Pielli reworked an older layered dessert with mascarpone and coffee in the 1950s. A 2016 book by Clara and Gigi Padovani reproduces a hotel bill of 1959 that reads “tirami su per 2”.46,50 Pielli was still pressing her case in 2013, at 96.51 In Pieris, Mario Cosolo served a cup he called “Tirime su”, a semifreddo of sponge and zabaione that contained neither mascarpone nor coffee.50

In July 2017 Italy's agriculture ministry added tiramisù to the list of traditional food products of Friuli Venezia Giulia.52 The president of the Veneto, Luca Zaia, asked for the decree to be suspended. The list records regional traditions and does not rule on who invented anything.53 Luca Cesari, writing in Gambero Rosso in 2026, called the dessert a collective creation.49

At the address today

The Campeol family closed Le Beccherie on 30 March 2014.54 It later reopened under new owners and was open in November 2026, according to its website.47 Ado Campeol died in 2021 and Roberto Linguanotto in 2024.55,56 The Albergo Roma is still a hotel in Tolmezzo, and the regional tourist board's guide to the town calls it the birthplace of tiramisù.57

Desk verdict: unproven. Treviso has the earliest printed attribution. Tolmezzo has earlier private bills, reproduced in a 2016 book. Three kitchens each hold a piece of the dessert, and no document awards it to one of them.

Entry No. 08 of 12

Pizza Margherita

Contradicted

Born, as claimed
June 1889, Naples, for Queen Margherita of Savoy
Named parent
Raffaele Esposito, pizzaiolo, of the pizzeria now called Brandi
Evidence offered
A letter of thanks dated 11 June 1889, signed Camillo Galli of the royal household, displayed at the pizzeria
First on paper
The toppings: a mid-nineteenth-century description of Naples pizzas. The name: not established58,59

The story is that Esposito was summoned to make pizzas for the queen during a royal stay in Naples, that she preferred the one with tomato, mozzarella and basil, and that he named it for her. The letter on the wall at Pizzeria Brandi thanks him for three kinds of pizza and names none of them.58,60

Tomato, mozzarella and basil were on Neapolitan pizzas well before 1889. Emanuele Rocco's chapter on the pizza-maker in Francesco De Bourcard's Usi e costumi di Napoli, published in the middle of the century, lists all three among the toppings in use.58,61 The queen and pizza had met before, too: the Washington Post of 25 July 1880 reprinted a report of her eating pizza in Naples.59,60

In 2014 the historian Zachary Nowak published a study of the letter. As summarised by National Geographic and others, he found no record of it in the royal household's archives, noted that its seal is a rubber stamp unlike the one on a genuine letter of Galli's, and judged that the handwriting did not match. He suggested it was produced decades later, and that the story took hold in the 1930s. That is his argument from the evidence; who wrote the letter has not been established.62,58,60,61 No newspaper report of the 1889 event has been found.60 The parts that do check out are that Esposito owned the pizzeria, that the royal couple were in Naples that June, and that Galli held the post.58

At the address today

Pizzeria Brandi trades at Salita Sant'Anna di Palazzo 1/2, with the letter and a centenary plaque from 1989 on display, and was listed as open in a guide published in September 2026.63,64,58

Desk verdict: contradicted. The pizza predates the queen's visit and the one document offered as proof has been seriously questioned. Whether any pizza was made for her in June 1889 is unknown.

Entry No. 09 of 12

Sachertorte

Unproven

Born, as claimed
1832, the kitchen of Prince Metternich, Vienna
Named parent
Franz Sacher (1816–1907), then a 16-year-old apprentice65
Rival account
Sacher's own, late in life, reportedly placing it in the 1840s in Pressburg, now Bratislava
First on paper
A “Chocolade-Torte à la Sacher” in Katharina Prato's cookbook: 1858 according to the Austrian ministry; historians cited by Wikipedia reject that date and are sure of it only by 187065,66

Hotel Sacher's history has the young Franz standing in for a sick chef and producing the cake for Metternich's guests.67 According to the Austrian agriculture ministry's register of traditional foods, the claim was first made in print by his son Eduard, in a letter to the Wiener Zeitung in 1888, more than half a century after the event.65

Eduard trained at the court confectioner Demel, made the cake there, and in 1876 opened the hotel that carries the family name.65,68,67 The food historian Michael Krondl, as cited by Wikipedia, has written that no contemporary document confirms the 1832 account, and that Franz Sacher's own recollection in a 1906 newspaper profile pointed to the 1840s, when he was running a business in Pressburg.66

The paper that does exist is legal. After the hotel went bankrupt in 1934, Eduard's son reportedly took the recipe to Demel, and the hotel's new owners began selling an “Original Sacher-Torte”. The two houses fought over the name for years. Most accounts end the case with an out-of-court settlement in 1963; a trademark lawyer who examined it in 2024 gives 1965 and describes a court-imposed outcome.65,69 Either way the hotel kept “Original Sacher-Torte”, with its layer of apricot jam through the middle, and Demel kept the right to sell its own version without that layer.65,69

At the address today

Both are open. Café Sacher is in the hotel at Philharmonikerstraße 4, and Demel is at Kohlmarkt 14, where it sells “Demel's Sachertorte”.70,71,69 The hotel has put its output at about 360,000 cakes a year.67

Desk verdict: unproven. The lawsuit settled a trademark. The scene in Metternich's kitchen has no document behind it older than the son's letter of 1888.

Entry No. 10 of 12

Pavlova

Holds up

Born, as claimed
1926, a hotel in Wellington, New Zealand; or 1935, the Esplanade Hotel, Perth, Western Australia
Named parents
An unnamed Wellington hotel chef; Herbert “Bert” Sachse (1898–1974) in Perth72
Occasion
The ballerina Anna Pavlova's tours of 1926 and 1929
First on paper
1929, a “pavlova cake” recipe in a New Zealand farming annual73,74

New Zealand's claim, that a Wellington hotel chef made the dessert for the dancer in 1926, comes from her biographer Keith Money; the food anthropologist Helen Leach was never able to verify it, and no hotel or chef has been named.75,76 Australia's rests on Sachse, who said in 1973 that he had created the pavlova at the Esplanade in 1935. He also said he had worked from a magazine recipe, and the Museum of Perth notes that a prize-winning meringue cake sent in from New Zealand had been published that April.77,78

Leach, of the University of Otago, spent years on the cookbooks. The name was first attached to other things: a strawberry dish in 1911, a layered jelly in a gelatine company's recipe book in the 1920s, and small coffee-and-walnut meringues from Dunedin in 1928.79,80,81 A meringue cake of the right kind appears in a Wellington cookbook in 1927 under the plain name “meringue cake”.75 The first recipe that is both a large meringue cake and called pavlova dates from 1929, in New Zealand, six years before Perth.73,74 Leach counted at least 21 New Zealand pavlova recipes by 1940.74,79

When the Oxford English Dictionary revised its entry in 2010 it gave a New Zealand first citation, from 1927, which was reported as a win for New Zealand. The citation is for the jelly.80,74 Two researchers, Andrew Paul Wood and Annabelle Utrecht, have since argued that the cake itself is older than either country's claim, descending from the meringue, cream and fruit tortes of German-speaking Europe.76,77

At the address today

The Esplanade Hotel was demolished in November 1972 and an office tower now stands on the site.78 The Wellington hotel has never been identified, so there is no address to visit.

Desk verdict: holds up. For the question the two countries argue about, the cookbooks favour New Zealand: the named cake is in print there in 1929. Sachse's 1935 claim is contradicted by his own account of copying a recipe.

Entry No. 11 of 12

Carpaccio

Unproven

Born, as claimed
1950, Harry's Bar, Calle Vallaresso 1323, Venice
Named parent
Giuseppe Cipriani, founder of the bar, born 1900; died 198082
Occasion
A dish for Countess Amalia Nani Mocenigo, named after the painter Vittore Carpaccio
First on paper
No dated menu or first printing was found

The Cipriani company's account is that the countess had been told by her doctor to eat raw meat, that Giuseppe Cipriani served her paper-thin slices of beef, and that he named the dish for a Venetian painter known for his reds.83 The company's heritage page gives no year. The date of 1950 is attributed to the memoir of his son Arrigo, which ties the naming to a Carpaccio exhibition on in Venice at the time, and it is the date most later sources repeat.84,85,82

Venice's civic museums staged a Carpaccio exhibition at the Doge's Palace in 2023 and described it as the first in the city devoted to him since the landmark show of 1963, which has a published catalogue.86,87,88 The desk found no record of a Carpaccio exhibition in Venice in 1950.

Other dates are in circulation. Anne Willan's reference work La Varenne Pratique says the dish became fashionable at Harry's Bar in 1961. The Associated Press, covering the 2023 exhibition, wrote only that Cipriani invented it “in the 1950s”.89,90 None of the sources the desk read addresses the mismatch, so it is offered here as an observation and not as a finding of theirs.

At the address today

Harry's Bar is still at Calle Vallaresso 1323, a few steps from the San Marco waterfront. Italy's culture ministry declared it a national landmark in 2001, according to the company and the national tourist board.83,91

Desk verdict: unproven. The bar and the man are uncontested. No document from the time fixes the year, and the only Carpaccio exhibition in Venice the desk could document came thirteen years after the date usually given.

Entry No. 12 of 12

Poutine

Unproven

Born, as claimed
1957, Café Idéal, Warwick, Quebec; or 1964, Le Roy Jucep, Drummondville, Quebec
Named parents
Fernand Lachance in Warwick; Jean-Paul Roy in Drummondville
Evidence offered
A federal trademark, L'INVENTEUR DE LA POUTINE, registered to the Drummondville restaurant's then owner in 199892
First on paper
22 August 1977, La Tribune of Sherbrooke, found by Barry Popik and reported by David Wilton93

In Warwick the story is that a regular, Eddy Lainesse, asked Lachance to put cheese curds in his bag of chips, and Lachance replied that it would make a “maudite poutine”, a damned mess.94 Gravy came some years later: 1963 according to the Canadian Encyclopedia, 1964 according to Canada's History.94,95 In Drummondville, Roy was already selling chips in sauce, saw customers adding curds, and put the three together on his menu.94 Roy bought the drive-in that became Le Roy Jucep in 1964.96 A later owner told the Toronto Star in 2008 that what Lachance made in 1957, without sauce, was not poutine.97

Roy Jucep displays its claim as a certificate. The underlying record in the Canadian Trademarks Database is an ordinary registration of the phrase L'INVENTEUR DE LA POUTINE for restaurant services, T-shirts and caps, applied for in 1997 by Daniel Leblanc and registered on 2 November 1998. It carries a disclaimer of any exclusive right to the word “poutine”. A trademark protects a slogan in trade and is not a finding about who invented a dish.92

The word in its modern sense is hard to find in print before the late 1970s. The earliest example Wilton records is from La Tribune in August 1977, which had to explain to readers that poutine was a salty dish of cheese curds, chips and sauce. The first English example he gives is from the Montreal Gazette in April 1980.93 The Canadian Encyclopedia concludes that the exact provenance is contested and the province is not.94 A third town, Princeville, has a claim through a restaurant founded in 1966, after both of the others.98

At the address today

The Warwick restaurant no longer exists; in 2008 the site was a charity shop.97 Le Roy Jucep is open at 1050 boulevard Saint-Joseph in Drummondville, the address on the trademark record.99,92 The trade press reported in September 2025 that it had been bought by the Groupe Ashton chain, with a promise to leave it unchanged.100 Fernand Lachance died in 2004.97

Desk verdict: unproven. Warwick claims the curds and the word, Drummondville the sauce, and the author of a 2007 book on the subject narrowed it to those two towns.101 Nothing printed at the time has been published for either.

How the register was kept

The four fields at the head of each entry are filled in from the sources numbered beside them. “Born, as claimed” is the date and place given by the claimant or the establishment. “First on paper” is the earliest dated document that names the dish and that a researcher has published; older ones may yet turn up, and several of these dates have moved back in the last twenty years.

A great deal of that searching was done by one person. Barry Popik, a contributor to the Oxford English Dictionary, has spent decades running the names of American dishes through digitised newspapers, and his findings sit behind seven of these entries. Helen Leach did the equivalent for the pavlova by reading New Zealand cookbooks one at a time.

The desk worked from published sources and did not inspect the original menus, letters or archives. Where a fact reached us only through a secondary account, the entry says so. Opening status is as found in November 2026 and will date. The three verdicts are a judgement about documents and say nothing about how good the dish is at the address that claims it.
